January 23, 1942, Friday
Seeing our slow work, the camp commander ordered the chief to assemble the company; he himself alerted the Arabs who still guard us, and only then, revolver in hand, began speaking sharply to us, announcing that if the company did not work at the rate of 1 cubic meter per person every day, he would not allow wine and meat to be issued, and would close the canteen.
By noon the pace of work had not improved, so there was no wine at lunch, and the camp commander began provoking some kind of incident. After lunch the company again worked slowly. A few minutes before the end of work the camp commander came over and began accosting several men for working too slowly, but they gave way to him — until he ordered Tomaszewski to light a fire and get to the wheelbarrows; the latter did not carry out the order, for which the camp commander personally marched him off to the arrest area. In the company someone shouted, "Don't let this happen, let's drop our tools!" and the whole company left its tools and started walking toward the camp, but the Austrian blocked their path and asked them to gather up the tools for him. The company turned back and gathered the tools.
While this was happening, the camp commander summoned the Arabs and blocked the company's path, but the company paid no attention to this and walked past unhindered. Only once the company had dispersed to the tents did he post guards by the tents, one Arab per two tents, and forbade anyone to leave them under threat of being shot at. When supper was announced, no one came out for it, and so the hunger strike began. We feel this was not the right course to take, but it's already done.

January 24, 1941, Saturday
We go hungry, lying in the tents. The weather is foul, as the sirocco blows with great force, so we wouldn't have gone to work anyway. In the company, the cooks and those on duty, like the storekeeper and the orderly, do not go hungry, because the latter is very afraid. The cooks secretly brought us a small piece of bread with onion at noon, and a cup of soup for supper. They did this so openly with the soup that it seems the French must have noticed.

January 25, 1942, Sunday
During the night more Arabs arrived by truck, and around 10 in the morning the major who commands the travailleurs arrived from Colomb-Béchar. He summoned Chief Benal and spoke with him. After this conversation the chief assembled the company and announced that the major had said he could not improve the food, and rejected our other demands as well; he only promised that we would get the money we were asking for, for which he gave his word of honor, and that if he could find another water tank somewhere, we would have Saturday afternoons free for laundry. As for Tomaszewski, he said that the man had disobeyed an order and would be court-martialed for it. As for the company, if it wants to starve or work, that's up to it — he would not force us. Here he reminded us that one condition of the armistice was that Poles be handed over to the Germans, but that they [the French] are hiding us — yet if we cause trouble, the German Commission will find out about us and take us away. Hearing this, Sgt. Gbur immediately ran to the major asking to be sent to Germany, adding that there were plenty of volunteers here for that. The major reportedly promised to send forms to fill out for

the commission. Sgt. Gbur claims he's doing this in order to have a chance to escape along the way. The political situation is such that it's not clear whether it would be better to escape to the English from Africa or from Europe. As he left, the major took Tomaszewski with him under arrest, and also took Chief Benal to the colonel in Colomb-Béchar to discuss our case. After the major left, the company took food, and those who had most urged the hunger strike now sit like frightened rabbits.

January 26, 1942, Monday
In today's orders it was announced that as long as work output remains at 1 cubic meter per person, we will not get wine and the canteen will not be opened. The company commander punished Tomaszewski with 8 days' solitary confinement, separated from the group. But despite such a strict order, the output we achieve today is 1/3 of what's required, because the stone we haul is available only in very small quantities, at a distance of 300 m from the track. I have the impression that they won't be opening the canteen — where I can buy dates, which I'm mad about — anytime soon.

January 27, 1942, Tuesday
Output is the same as yesterday, but at the request of the Polish and French overseers, the commander allowed wine to be issued and the canteen to open. That very evening I spent over 50 francs on purchases. Tonight Chief Benal arrived back from Colomb-Béchar, where he had been summoned to the Second Bureau rather than to the colonel, as he had been told. There, apparently, he got a proper dressing-down for stirring up the company. On the same train, Sierota and Ołota departed for Colomb-Béchar.

January 28, 1942, Wednesday
Today a new commander arrived at the camp, in the rank of captain — an older man, very fierce-looking, with a bristling mustache. He limps slightly, and on his chest are many decorations. On the night train, 3 Poles arrived: Piekarski, who once escaped from Saïda and was subsequently imprisoned, and two others who claimed to be officers, but those who came to our company from Morocco know them and say they are only privates.

January 29, 1942, Thursday
The new company commander is partly taking over the company.

January 30, 1942, Friday
January 31, 1942, Saturday
Today's train brought us 30 sheep. All these sheep escaped during the night, and when the comrade who tends the flock tried to bring them back, the Arabs turned him back at gunpoint. In the morning the commander sent the Arabs out searching and threatened that if the sheep weren't found, they would pay for it. Around 10 o'clock an Arab brought back one, and by noon they had brought back 28 head, along with one torn hide. The Arabs claim jackals devoured the sheep. On examining the hide, however, it turned out the skin had been cut with a knife.

The situation with water is very bad, because what they bring us is too little for washing clothes and ourselves, and on top of that the lack of soap is badly felt, as is the lack of any container for washing. Many in the company already have lice. Today at noon I stole a little water and washed my laundry, not with soap but with a washing powder called here "Lessive" — it removes dirt very well, but it also loosens the threads, and a shirt won't survive more than 3 washes, and we only have one shirt each. So everyone limits himself to washing once a month. So how, under these conditions, could one avoid getting lice.

(End of the First Notebook of Stefan Pisulewski's Diary)
