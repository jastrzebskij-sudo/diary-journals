to come see him after the roll call, and ordered the company to go to supper. The men dispersed, but no one went to supper, as a sign of protest, because he did not want to speak with us, and because the lieutenant had said at roll call that we, the workers, have no rights here — so nothing to say — only obligations. By a lucky chance, an officer from French intelligence happened to be at the camp conducting interrogations, and Hankiewicz went to him. He assured us that we are still considered combatants and asked us not to cause trouble and to work, and that everything would change, and that the Austrian's harassment would not happen again. As for those who were arrested, Budzyński is to be released in the morning once he sobers up, but Durkowski is held for a different matter — intelligence ordered his arrest, so he won't be released. Among other things, this officer reportedly said that they had given us 2 days to leave, but we hadn't taken advantage of it. I don't know now what that's supposed to mean. Most of the men went to supper after Hankiewicz's conversation with the intelligence officer, but a few were found who still reproached Hankiewicz for having handled the matter badly.

December 1, 1941
It seems that with the last day of November the evil fate left us, because the Austrian apologized to us, and because he asked the camp commander to lift the ban on issuing wine, which he had caused.

December 2, Tuesday - December 5, 1941, Friday
The Austrian must have heard a proper lecture from the French intelligence lieutenant, because he changed completely and sits as if he weren't there, no longer driving anyone
to work. Time passes in daily monotonous labor, and in the evenings there's nothing to do, because the lighting in the barracks (tent) — carbide lamps — is very poor, and it's cold. The mornings, too, are very cold, and there's frost on the tents and the railway ties. On Friday evening Dzida and Czarnecki arrived at the camp. The former had been held under arrest in Tlemcen for trying to escape to England, and the latter had been a cook in Mascara for our officers. The officers are held under arrest and the cadets under guard in a hotel, so Czarnecki claims.

December 6, 1941, Saturday
Today is the opening of the first section of the Mediterranean-Niger railway line. As a mark of this, the "Lux Torpédo" express passed over the track we're working on. We're supposed to earn 4 francs 50 centimes a day, but as for wages — nothing has come, and nothing keeps coming. Through the efforts of our chief, Benal, the French lieutenant agreed to grant credit at the canteen up to 4 francs a day, for which one can get 2 sandwiches — which one absolutely has to buy, or else go hungry. We asked that they sell plain bread instead of sandwiches, since it would be cheaper, but the Russian keeps making sandwiches because he earns more on them, despite the camp commander's orders. We complained about this to the camp commander, and he gave the "Russian fellow" a proper scolding. At the daily orders, the camp commander informed us that the railway had sent 3 francs a day for those working on the track and 4.50 for those in the camp, so on

behalf of the company he did not accept this money, asking instead for a raise. As it happens, he and the Austrian are both very courteous to us these days.

December 7, 1941, Sunday
In the morning Baron Kosiński, who is currently in Colomb-Béchar, arrived at the camp and brought several letters and news that there is money waiting for us with Lt. Janicki, so in the afternoon the chief, accompanied by one of the overseers, went into town and brought back a little news and 10,000 francs.

(photograph)
Camp in the Sahara, December 1941

December 8, 1941, Monday
It is not Saturday but today that is the opening of the railway line — we only saw the first passenger train and a few airplanes that came for the ceremony. Chief Benal Rudolf informed the camp commander that we had received money from the Polish Red Cross, so that he wouldn't think we were plotting something behind his back, and distributed the money to us. We received 50 francs each, and it's badly needed, because we were hungry and had no money to buy bread.

December 9, 1941, Tuesday
As a second breakfast, called here "casse-croûte," we've been getting soup for several days, but today the soup was bad, so the Austrian went into the camp and got us a can of sardines for every 3 men. This Austrian, after the last incident, is unrecognizable, he's improved so much.

December 10, 1941, Wednesday
Today we were told that we're to get more than 7 francs a day for work on the track. Our output is more than ½ cubic meter per person. News reaches us of the outbreak of the American-Japanese war.

December 11, 1941, Thursday
Out here in this wilderness we no longer think about the needs of a civilized man or any entertainments like those in Saïda, or about women, whom I haven't seen in a month and a half; my mind is occupied with only two questions of the day: whether there will be bread in the canteen today, and whether the platoon will manage the required 50 m of stones. As for politics, we are passive, and the news that reaches us is so uncertain and contradictory that it's not worth bothering with it and wearing out one's nerves.

December 12, 1941, Friday
Today while working my hand swelled up from the strain of breaking stones.

December 13, 1941, Saturday
The doctor forbade me from coming to work, because my right hand is swollen and an abscess is forming between my thumb and index finger.

December 14, 1941, Sunday
Last night I didn't close my eyes because of the pain in my hand. In the afternoon the doctor lanced the abscess and now it doesn't hurt so much.

December 15, 1941, Monday
From morning a strong wind blows with sand, and it's cold. The company went to work but worked only half the day, because it was impossible to continue. I have leave until Thursday. News came from Colomb-Béchar that the Russians have apparently taken Kiev and Smolensk, and that Poland has declared war on Japan.

December 16, 1941, Tuesday
I lie in the tent doing nothing, and my hand no longer hurts, although it's still swollen. So as not to be bored, I bought myself a couple of kilos (a full bread bag) of dates and I chew on them — I want to sicken myself of them, since I love them so much. For some time now I've had a partner in food matters, one Dziadkowicz, a man very cunning at gathering provisions. The weather has improved somewhat, as it rained at night and the wind no longer carries clouds of sand, though it's very cold. The company took blankets to work to cover themselves while breaking stones. In today's orders we were reminded that those excused from work by the doctor receive no pay at all, while those excused from heavy labor will receive 2 francs a day. I'm writing nonsense and forgot the most important thing — namely, since Saturday, that is December 13, we have had a French flag and a Polish flag in the camp. They are arranged so that the much larger French flag is on top, and below it, half the size, the Polish one, with the inscription "Honor and Fatherland." At the foot of the flagpole, comrade Szymański arranged stones and painted them to form the Polish eagle and a double-edged axe with the letters E.F., which is the emblem of the present-day French State.

December 17, 1941, Wednesday
The weather is beautiful and warm — makes you want to live. Rumors are going around the camp that our officers have been taken from Mascara somewhere near Colomb-Béchar.

December 18, 1941, Thursday
Nothing new.

(photograph)
Work and camp at Colomb-Béchar

December 19, 1941, Friday
Even though my hand hurts and is still swollen, I went to work, because it's very boring in the camp.

December 20, 1941, Saturday
With the casse-croûte, the story from December 9, 1941 repeated itself, when the company refused to eat the soup and the Austrian brought sprats. Chief Benal read us a letter from Mr. Czapski, in which he informs us

that at Christmas we'll get 15 francs each, and promises to come to the camp in the coming days.

December 21, 1941, Sunday
In the morning we dig small trenches on the camp square, meant to serve as benches for Christmas Eve. After lunch, smallpox vaccination. In the evening the camp was visited by two Legion non-commissioned officers with one woman. One of these NCOs was a Pole who had served in the Foreign Legion for over 10 years. He asked us to sing him a Polish song. Of course we obliged his request. The guests listened, very moved, and when we finished he thanked us and raised the cry "Long live France, long live Poland!" after which the choir sang the Marseillaise and "Jeszcze Polska" [Poland Is Not Yet Lost]. After the Marseillaise, the Austrian was the first to start shouting "Marsz, marsz, Dąbrowski."

December 22, 1941, Monday
December 23, 1941, Tuesday
The camp commander informed us that the priest whose arrival we had expected on the second day of the holidays would not be coming. Great bitterness in the company that even this is denied us. The altar had even been prepared, and now nothing will come of it. The chief apparently sent a telegram to Algiers for a Polish priest, but we don't believe he could come, since it's too late. The weather is foul, with a strong wind and very cold.

December 24, 1941, Wednesday, Christmas Eve
From morning the weather is very overcast; around 11 it turns to rain and we stop work. At noon it clears up a little,

so the French sent us back to work, but just as we started working, the rain drove us back into the tents again. We thought nothing would come of our shared Christmas Eve, but God had not forgotten us, because around 4 o'clock the rain stopped, so we hastily began building tables. The Austrian treated the company to a pack of cigarettes and two oranges per person, issued to us by the canteen. We began Christmas Eve at 5 o'clock with a prayer, after which Chief Benal R. spoke first, offering good wishes to everyone.
The Austrian was the most senior man in camp, since the lieutenant had gone to Colomb-Béchar, so the chief offered him wishes first, then the sergeants, and then everyone began wishing each other well — the Austrian gave his wishes to each man individually. Then we ate a modest meal and the choir sang a few carols. While we were eating, a caravan of camels and a flock of sheep passed by the camp. The Austrian bought a fine ram from this caravan for 5.50 francs and gave it to the company as a gift. In gratitude the company tossed him up in the air, and then the sergeants too. The sergeants had promised us a cup of wine each for Christmas Eve. After everyone dispersed, the canteen sold wine for cash to those who had it, and on credit, since few had money, as we still hadn't received our wages or the 15 francs from the Red Cross. The brotherhood got a bit tipsy and sang carols, and somehow forgot about our situation and about our loved ones. But there turned out to be one swine who spoiled these moments, because Fiedosiejew, drunk, stabbed Fiszer's hand with a knife; Podniesiński stood up in his defense and smashed with his boot the head of

the attacker. So unfortunately that they could not stop the bleeding, and an ambulance called from Colomb-Béchar took him to the hospital.

December 25, 1941, Thursday, Christmas Day
In the morning I was called to the office, where I received 100 francs from Wicek Zarychta of Adrar. This Wicek is a very decent fellow and understands our situation well. I paid off a few debts and gave a little to friends, and I don't go hungry today, since the canteen isn't giving credit.

December 26, 1941, Friday, Second Day of the Holidays
They won't let us celebrate. We were ordered to dig a pit for a water tank. Supposedly we're to be paid for a full day's work even though we only worked 3 hours. At night the guard posts were doubled, and our overseers walked among the tents all night.

December 27, 1941, Saturday
News reached the camp from somewhere that the travailleurs in Kenadsa had mutinied, and that the Arabs refused to sell sheep, fleeing with them into the mountains, which is why we were guarded so closely at night. Political news keeps coming in various forms, but increasingly better — about the Germans retreating, about an uprising in Poland, and about the English advancing in Libya.

December 28, 1941, Sunday
Rumors are going around that Mr. Czapski is in Colomb-Béchar.

December 29, 1941, Monday
Mr. Czapski, long awaited, arrived at the camp. It happened during work, which we stopped for a few minutes to give our report. Mr. Czapski stayed at the camp until late evening, spoke with everyone who wanted something from him, and was present for the lowering of the flag, which the Austrian reported to him, standing remarkably at attention and addressing Mr. Czapski as "mon colonel." The camp commander, on the other hand, behaved very badly, since during the report and during the conversation with Mr. Czapski he kept his hands in his pockets and generally behaved dismissively, which the Austrian pointed out to him. Also arriving with Mr. Czapski were Mr. Przesmycki, his deputy, and Mr. Borowski, delegate of the Commission for Aid to Poles.

December 30, 1941, Tuesday
Somehow we're working badly; the camp commander is dissatisfied with this and even locked one man up for slow work, but released him after the intervention of Chief Benal. Mr. Czapski, with his companions, paid us 50 francs each.

December 31, 1941, Wednesday
We work much better than yesterday.

January 1, 1942, Thursday
The New Year was announced in the camp with twelve strikes of a pickaxe, after which good wishes were offered to the camp commander and the choir sang 3 songs (Polish Banners on the Kremlin, and two carols).

There was drinking afterward until morning, since the canteen had brought in wine with special permission. From morning a sirocco blows, so that one doesn't feel like getting up or washing. Before evening Mr. Czapski arrived at the camp. He settled a few more matters with several men, and on leaving offered us New Year's wishes. Among other things he said that the road ahead of us is long and hard, but the goal is great, and for its sake we must endure.

January 2, 1942, Friday
A sirocco with snow and rain doesn't let us work. We sleep, doing nothing, and don't even feel like going out for food. During the night the sirocco tore one tent, so we had to take in its occupant.

January 3, 1942, Saturday
The wind has died down a little, but rain falls in the morning and we don't go to work.
Those found unfit went to Colomb-Béchar for a medical board. In the afternoon the weather improved and we work near the camp.

January 4, 1942, Sunday, and
January 5, 1942, Monday — Nothing new
January 6, 1942, Tuesday, Epiphany
A new overseer arrives from Saïda and brings news of the death of Cpl. Henryk Olszewski in the hospital in Saïda. He was buried

in that same town. Present at the funeral was one of the Polish officers (probably Capt. Szewalski), delegations of Legion veterans, travailleurs, and local combatants. The travailleurs bought a wreath. According to the doctors' opinion, the late Henio Olszewski died of diabetes, but those who knew him from Warsaw, like Mietek Grzegorzewski, claim it was a family history of consumption [tuberculosis]. Poor Henio marked our road with his grave. As I later learned, the funeral took place on January 5, 1942, so he died on January 2 or 3, 1942.

January 7, 1942, Wednesday
During the night a sirocco blew with unusual force, and because of this we don't go to work until 9 o'clock. Just before lunch the sirocco picked up again, and during lunch it kept piling sand into our mess tins until it drove us back to camp.

January 7, 1942, Wednesday - January 8, 1942, Thursday
January 9, 1942, Friday
A certain group of Poles was supposed to leave for the new location of our camp somewhere near Bou Arfa, but for some unknown reason it did not come about. In the evening, by the light of carbide lamps, a ceremony was held to honor the memory of the late H. Olszewski. The chief said a few words and ordered a minute of silence, after which the choir sang a funeral march. To close, everyone sang the camp song.

January 11, 1942, Sunday
After lunch I went with Zawistowski to the castle that can be seen from the camp. But upon reaching it, the "castle" turned out to be a rock formation of stones resembling castle ruins.

January 12 and 13, 1942, Monday and Tuesday — Nothing.
January 14, 1942, Wednesday
We are preparing to change our camping location. The camp commander allowed one man from each tent to go to Colomb-Béchar for shopping, but in the afternoon many others went without permission too — myself and Zawistowski among them. Our goal was to help Kotala carry back the purchases. We met him in town, drank a little, took part of the purchases, and returned safely, but Kotala went missing somewhere and didn't get back to camp until 5 in the morning, without Wołk's briefcase and Karwaciński's boots, which he had borrowed from them. He went back into town once more for them, but without success.

January 15, 1942, Thursday
In the morning a train was brought for us and carried us 60 km north toward Bou Arfa. At the new site we have a few mud huts where the kitchen, the office, and lodgings for the overseers have been set up. We live in tents. On the 16th and 17th we set up the camp. Under the tents we dig a half-meter pit, which gives us more room in the tent and makes it much warmer. My tent was so unfortunate that half the pit had to be chiseled out of rock. I badly strained my hand doing this work.

January 18, 1942, Sunday
I lie in bed all day, since I've caught a chill and my hand hurts.

January 19, 1942, Monday, and January 20, 1942, Tuesday
After a lot of fuss, I was excused from work on the track. Instead I work peeling carrots and turnips for the lunches, since that's all they feed us here. As payment for a full day's work, they credit me with 2 hours.

February 21, 1942, Wednesday
I go back to work on the track. Chief Benal and Sgt. Porwal have some conflict with Baron Kosiński, Mieczysław, who is the provisions quartermaster, so they asked the camp commander to remove him from this post, but the commander refused. Wanting to protest this, they worked alongside us instead of supervising. The company, seeing that the chief was in conflict with the camp commander, slowed the work pace in order to press its own demands for better food and for the canteen to be allowed to make purchases in town more often. The Austrian, seeing the slowed pace of work, began to drive us on, but eventually gave up and went to the camp commander to complain. In the evening he took heart drops from the doctor and showed up completely drunk for the evening roll call, but it passed without incident.

January 22, 1942, Thursday
The company continues its slow work.
