At lunchtime I had a small quarrel with Corporal Jodłowski, and if I had been a bit more short-tempered, it would have come to blows.
In the afternoon, while out for a walk, I came across a cork tree. This is a tree whose bark grows back and is used to make corks.
Remnants of Polish units arrived at the beach camp, but all of them wanted to return to their homes. In general, almost all our countrymen from France have been raised in the French spirit, and think only of having (boku manże — a garbled phonetic rendering of French, roughly meaning "food and drink," i.e. a comfortable life), while the patriotic spirit in them has died out.

July 3, 1940, Wednesday
Word reaches us that the English sank several units of the French war fleet because they refused to fight on England's side and also did not want to be interned. Reportedly one aircraft carrier was sunk, the cruiser Strasburg was sunk, and the cruiser Dunkierka was said to be badly damaged.
Because of this there was great satisfaction among the Poles, while the French shook their heads.

July 4, Thursday
Nothing important, except that I went to steal apricots in the fields, because I didn't have a single centime and I really wanted some fruit. Apricots are very cheap here, 2 francs per kilogram.

July 5, Friday
After lunch the sergeant major gathered the company and shared the news that there is a

ship in the port of Vendres that could take us to Algiers, and that the air force major had gone to the port about this matter, and that Lieutenant Osiecki had gone to fetch civilian clothing. He ordered us to pack up and not go anywhere, because we could leave at any moment. Great joy broke out, but it came to nothing. Civilian clothes were brought, but they weren't handed out.
The gendarmerie began harassing us — one airman got slapped in the face by a gendarme, two were handcuffed for some trivial reasons, and one had his bicycle taken away. Our authorities intervened in this matter, with results. One of the gendarmes was arrested for insulting the whole unit.
In the evening some of the guys organized an expedition to the women's camp, and the next day there were plenty of amused comments about it.

July 6, 1940, Saturday
We received civilian clothing, and the lieutenant informed us that the ministry has taken an interest in us and will help us leave, but as civilians, and that we must change into civilian clothes because the French are expecting a German commission at the camp, and we cannot be here as the Polish army, which has already been dissolved. If the gendarmerie should bother us, we are to say (que suis Polonais) — "I am Polish." We are not very eager to take off our uniforms, because the civilian clothes are terrible cheap junk, but the boys manage as best they can to look decent somehow. The black soldiers, who as I mentioned above became great friends with us, are once again a bit too much for us.

26

After we had settled down to sleep, Senior Private Pała came to our barrack, and we had a great laugh at his expense. He is generally the camp's laughingstock.
July 7, Sunday
I got up from my bunk after lunch, having spent the whole morning lying in bed reading a book (The Unknown Island). After lunch I went with 3 friends to get fruit in the fields. About 3 km from the camp we entered what was practically a forest of apricot and peach trees. We stuffed ourselves until our teeth ached. During the whole afternoon in that orchard we met 2 men and 2 women on a cart full of fruit, and they gave us beretfuls of apricots.
Corporal Komar photographed our group together with the black soldiers. I am leaving a place for it in the diary.

July 8, 1940, Monday
At 10 o'clock a company assembly was ordered, at which an informal comrades' tribunal was held against two soldiers for theft. The first was Private Liberadzki, commonly called "the Gypsy." His offense was selling a government-issue leather jacket and two revolvers stolen from fellow soldiers. The sergeant major laid out the charges against him, having irrefutable proof of his guilt, which he denied, and for that he got a few punches in the face in front of the company. Liberadzki began to run away, and on the way got one more kick.

The second culprit was some former policeman, though not from our combat company but one of the stragglers. The sergeant major accused him of robbing an apartment near where our trucks were parked. The culprit was not present at the assembly; the sergeant major had to fetch him from the barracks, shoving him along, and as soon as he arrived several men jumped up and punched him in the face. Then the sergeant major struck him a few times across the back with a rubber hose, and later across the hands, amid the encouraging shouts of the whole company. Then the sergeant major called for a few men to lay him on a bench, because he wanted to give him a few strokes of the hose on his backside, but during the scuffle with him, Lieutenant Stanek, who was present the whole time, stood up in the culprit's defense and told him to run away, though on his way out the sergeant major did not fail to give him a few more strokes with the hose. The lieutenant ordered the company to disperse, except for those who had revolvers and binoculars, whom he made give their word that they would not sell their pistols, but on the contrary would keep them very carefully hidden.
In the afternoon the sergeant major exchanged my civilian suit for a better one.

July 9, Tuesday
Toward morning we were woken by a visit from a civilian, a gendarme, and the commander of the guard — they were searching for something in the barracks. Only after breakfast did I find out that it was the father of one of the Frenchmen who are in our company, and that he had come for his son. He took four Frenchmen away with him.
In the evening one of the guys played the violin. Even the French gathered and listened, and it was clear they enjoyed it.

27

July 10, Wednesday
We received our pay, a total of 829 fr. After lunch I go into town wanting to buy a shirt. But I couldn't get one; I only bought slippers for 12 francs.
Corporal Wojtkowiak, who had stayed behind at the positions with Platoon Sergeant Sum, joined the company and told us about the death of Platoon Sergeant Sum and his driver. According to his account, the tank was knocked out by the French with a 75mm gun, by mistake. The sergeant did not die inside the tank but had left it because it was on fire. The shell tore off his head and both arms, and his entrails spilled out.
Driver Senior Private Świecicki was severely wounded in the left leg near the hip and in the left arm in two places, and also had several shrapnel fragments in his stomach. Doctors from the French Red Cross took him to the hospital, but held out no hope of keeping him alive.

July 11, Thursday
Boredom, an almost hopeless wait — several ships have already sailed from the port, and no one wants to talk with us. Our passports have been made, but there are no visas. Corporal Jodłowski took his passport without a visa from the lieutenant and is trying to arrange things on his own.
It's raining, and sitting idly in the barrack like this, I ponder our situation, and despair overtakes us — we are almost without a way out.

July 12, Friday
I was in town wanting to buy bread, but it's hard to get. We mostly live on tomato salad because it's cheap, and the food from the kitchen is

very bad. They give us rice thick one day and rice watery the next, and for breakfast and dinner black, bitter coffee. In the evening, next to the barracks of the black soldiers' company, the black soldiers put on dances, beating on tin cans. It was exactly like what's shown in films, and even more so, because there were such moves as could not be shown on film without corrupting people. The guard that watched over the camp, which had been kept by French airmen, was abolished.

July 13, Saturday
Corporal Kanar took a few photographs of me. In the evening, while on a walk with Corporal Sadowski, Kania, and Walewicz, as well as a Frenchman known as Jurek, I saw fishermen pulling their nets from the sea. The catch was very poor. They caught a fish similar to an eel, except it had a beak-like snout, like a swordfish's [sujka in the original], a fifth of its total length.

July 14, 1940, Sunday
In the evening I was out having wine with a few friends, and we found three Spaniards there. As it later turned out, one of them was a captain in the aviation of the Republican army. They complained bitterly about General Franco, saying that he had brought foreigners into his country while driving out all the Spaniards. And that the Republican army had not been communist.

July 15, Monday
The sea is very rough.

July 16, Tuesday
Corporal Sadecki brings me a suitcase from Perpignan; the suitcase, shirt, and tie together cost 100 francs. In the evening, while bathing in the river, I lost my little gold cross, but after a long search I found it.

July 17, Wednesday
Black dances and a drunk French soldier liven up our evening. After the evening news, I was with Corporal Jan Sadecki at a café having beer, and there the plan arose to go to Perpignan

July 18, 1940, Thursday
I went with Corporal Sadecki to Perpignan to go shopping. I bought light-colored trousers and a shirt of a similar color, plus a tie, for 38 fr., a windbreaker for 69 fr., a small case for 5 fr., and I also visited the Hotel Ambasador — in total I spent 400 francs.
I spend the beautiful moonlit evening, as usual, on a walk.

July 19, 1940, Friday

Boredom. The President of the United States of America, Roosevelt, spoke on the radio. Among other things, he said that the Germans claim they want to bring something new into the world, whereas they are only recreating what existed 15 centuries ago in the time of the pharaohs — a tyranny worthy of contempt.

July 20, 1940, Saturday
In the evening I was at the cinema in Argeles with Corporal Sadecki. The program was good.

July 21, 1940, Sunday
At the service which took place in the little church on the beach, so many people went up to Holy Communion that the priest ran out of hosts. Four black soldiers also received Holy Communion. In the afternoon we found out that the commandant of the Polish camp, Air Force Major Moszkowski, had fled to England. All the Poles were outraged at him.

June 22, 1940, Monday
The company received its pay, including war supplements.

July 23, Tuesday
At 6:30 we set out on a hike into the mountains. Janek Sadecki, Stawiarski, Świątkowski, and I go. Along the way we wanted to buy bread in town, but it could not be bought anywhere. We went to a peak visible from afar, on which there is an observation tower. Along the way we found a mulberry tree with very large, sweet fruit.

29

I liked them very much, even though I don't usually care for mulberries. There is a spring right below the summit, from which the water flows down through pipes. I tried out my pistol with a few shots. I was also at the top of the tower, which has no stairs, so one had to climb the wall at great risk to life. In short, I spent a very pleasant time. We decide to go up to the summit once more, but in a larger group and with a camera.

The rough sea at the beach in Angeles.

July 24, Wednesday

Yesterday, while I was on the hike, the sergeant major found a new thief and gave him a beating, later handing him over to the French at the camp. Today there was trouble again, because apparently some officer cadet was stealing gasoline from civilian cars, and when he was caught, they beat up a policeman. Because of this they intend to curtail our freedom. The same sort of thing is happening with our officers, who occupied villas without permission and brought women there, which the French look upon with disapproval. In general, people who have pretensions to being cultured turn out to be the worst. In the evening Lieutenant Osiecki held a company assembly and announced that the French are demanding that we hold formations — in short, activities — because troops sitting idle become demoralized.

During the assembly, a gendarme brought in those who had been stealing gasoline in Argeles and handed them over to the lieutenant. But the lieutenant said nothing to them. After the assembly, though, the soldiers themselves gave them a beating in the barracks, the officer cadet not excluded. Janek Sadowski in particular took an especially active part in this action. Lieutenant Osiecki released 10 of us for a hike in the mountains the next day.

July 25, Thursday

At 6 o'clock the seven of us set out on a hike to the same peak as before. We had plenty of provisions and revolver ammunition. Platoon Sergeant Michał Wolski and Corporal Daniel Kanar had cameras and took several photographs. We had a lot of laughs with Platoon Sergeant Wolski, because he's very cheerful. On the way back we bathed in a pool located halfway up the mountain. After descending into the valley we rested in the park of a beautiful little manor house, which we also immortalized on film.

July 26, 1940, Friday

We rest after yesterday's hike. There are supposedly activities scheduled, but only half the company showed up for assembly. The day's activity was a swim in the sea. In the afternoon I repaired my boots, which had been torn yesterday.

In the evening I was with Janek Sadecki in Argeles (the town) at a film titled The Miserable Ones [Les Misérables].

July 27, 1940, Saturday

All day I watched and helped pull an aircraft engine out of the sea. But it was very heavy and the ropes kept breaking. I tried diving in a gas mask; for this purpose I twisted together 10 hoses from other masks into one, so that the end could be held at the surface of the sea by another man, and I submerged myself several times near the shore. But the attempts produced no results, because the water pressure was so great that it pressed the mask tightly against my face, making breathing so difficult that one could not last underwater longer than two minutes.

July 28, 1940, Sunday

The French gendarmes ordered us to move out of our barrack into the large one. In the new spot we settled in reasonably well, even though there's no floor there, but we made one for ourselves out of bunk boards, of course only next to our own bunks. In the evening I was with Janek Sadecki at a café having fish. For the past few days he's been treating me, because I have no money.

July 29, Monday

During the morning activities someone brought up the matter of Sergeant Gbur, who had spent several days in Toulouse. And since he had previously often said that he wanted to go back under German rule, he was suspected of having gone to the German consulate about it. I too had told Lieutenant Stanek that Sergeant Gbur had said he would go back under German rule, and that by doing so he was greatly demoralizing the company. Sergeant Gbur was absent from the morning activities. In the afternoon Lieutenant S. held an assembly of the professional NCOs and brought up this matter without saying that it was Sergeant G. who had done this.

Besides that, he announced that the professional soldiers were to pay 5 francs per day for food. Naturally, this pleased no one. After the assembly, Sergeant Gbur came to me with reproaches, saying that I had told

the lieutenant that he had gone to the German consulate. Of course I set the matter straight, though he did not believe it, and said that he would report this to the authorities at the appropriate moment, along with the fact that in Roan I had not carried out his order and had not allowed my men to bring coffee for his crew. My crew did not want coffee because there was no time to drink it.

July 30, 1940, Tuesday

First English lesson; I'm also learning French. Janek Sadecki went to the port of Vandres, to a villa near Kotani.

July 31, 1940, Wednesday

Studying English and French.

August 1, 1940, Thursday

Same as the previous day.

August 2, 1940, Friday

The company switched to independent self-catering, and the professional soldiers must pay for their food. They are to pay two weeks in advance and two weeks in arrears. The food improved a little in quality, but there isn't enough of it. Now provisions are drawn from the military quartermaster's office, rather than, as before, from the Spanish camp.

August 2, 1940, Saturday

Laundering underwear/linen and self-study of French and English.

August 4, 1940, Sunday

At High Mass I was in Argeles (the town) together with a few friends. At lunchtime three Englishmen who had escaped from German captivity arrived at the camp. They are from the Expeditionary Corps in France. We received them very warmly. They chose Polish surnames for themselves and are trying to get Polish papers. And as Poles they want to go to England. The Chief placed them in the care of Corporal Jodłowski and Rydlewski. In the evening I was at the cinema with Janek Sadowski. The program was very boring.

August 5, Monday

Nothing important.

August 6, Tuesday

Before noon, instead of English lessons, there was pay disbursement. During the payout a misunderstanding arose with Corporal Świątkowski over the war supplement. Świątkowski insulted Lieutenant Stanek, saying he had robbed the company. At the afternoon assembly the matter was clarified. They did not receive the war supplement — only 300 francs per head for travel, donated by the consulate. Lt. Stanek is very offended at the company and declared that he would try to have the camp disbanded.
