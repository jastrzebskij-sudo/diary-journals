did not find them there, because they had gone to Carpiagne. I waited there 5 hours.
After returning to the camp I learned that a large number of men had left just
a few minutes earlier. Among them Sadecki and Rej, and I was supposed to go too,
but I was late and someone else was taken in my place. Wolski showed me
which direction they had gone, so I ran after them. I caught up with them
waiting in a small wood by the sports field. At my request the lieutenant told me to
pack and come along, while Ślęzak, who had a watch at the watchmaker's, was to
stay behind. And so it was done.
We were led secretly out of the wood to a crossroads where a guide was supposed
to be waiting. The guide did not come, although we waited until
1 in the morning, from 9 in the evening. While we waited, the captain, commander of
the group, explained that ships were supposed to be waiting for us at Cassis and would take us
to Gibraltar. Passage for one person was supposed to cost 3000 francs, and there
were 120 of us. The money was to be collected by them only after we boarded, one
half then, and the other half a week later they could withdraw from the bank. Toward
two in the morning we returned to the barracks, still hoping that tomorrow might be better.

September 19, 1940, Thursday
The company draws its uniforms. The denims and underwear are new, the wool
uniforms and coats old. I take all of it anyway, since I don't know what
awaits me, or indeed all of us. Janek went to Marseille, so I took
uniforms for him too. He bought me a small suitcase in Marseille for my
personal things. The evening bulletin from London reports that the French
island of New Caledonia has come out in favor of General de Gaulle.

September 20, 1940, Friday
Nothing worth noting, except that dinner is being cooked for supper and
vice versa. Otherwise the food has improved slightly.

September 21, 1940, Saturday
I write out French words into the notebook that Janek S. bought me yesterday.
Almost the whole day there was fog; only in the evening did the sun appear.
One of the men returned from the territories occupied by Germany and tells
about conditions there. It's supposedly not as bad as it seems.

September 22, 1940, Sunday
Before evening I went with Janek S. and Michał W. to take photographs
in order to use up the roll of film on which our French friends appear, and thereby
speed up getting their pictures developed. With Janek I took the film to be developed
in Cassis; on that occasion I looked around the little town and bought a couple of
postcards as souvenirs. As for going to England, I have lost hope.

September 23, 1940, Monday
In the evening a camp bonfire, better organized than the previous one and in
a very good spot, at the narrowest point of the ravine; the audience
sat on the rocks and could see everything very well. At the bonfire a
French colonel was present, along with several French officers assigned to
us. The bonfire ended with songs sung all together:

"Warszawianka," "Pod Twą obronę," and a camp song that
arose in Hungary or Romania. The colonel was bid farewell in French,
everyone together saying "Bon soir mon colonel." Afterward the colonel
said that he firmly believed Poland and France would rise again.
The audience answered with a shout of "Vive la France," the colonel
added "Vive la Pologne," and to him, in Polish, three times "niech żyje" [long live].
After that bonfire I felt greatly uplifted in spirit.
Our French friends came to visit us, but I did not see them. Janek S. and Józek R.
saw them off. They are said to have been quite offended with me over that. The radio
reports that the Italian offensive on Egypt has been halted and that General de Gaulle
has landed with troops at Dakar.

September 24, 1940, Tuesday
I went with Misiek W. to the highest mountain peak in this area. From
there Marseille could be seen as if on the palm of one's hand. Misiek was going to take
photographs, but poor visibility got in our way. We promised each other that as
soon as the weather is suitable we will go there again to take pictures.
Misiek climbed the rocks in such a way that it even amazed me. Janek and Józek
brought back photographs from Cassis. They came out well. The situation at Dakar with General de
Gaulle is unclear.

September 25, 1940, Wednesday
I receive pay amounting to 7 fr. 50 c. and set it aside for the gravestone of
a Pole from America who died these past days in the camp. I also got

my demobilization form. In the evening we bring the photographs to our
French friends. They are pleased with them. But they are very outraged
at England for wanting to take away their colonies. Apparently the English at Dakar
sank one French submarine. The French, in turn, bombarded
Gibraltar.

September 26, 1940, Thursday
Misiek W., Janek S., Michał Bardzki and I went on an excursion to the
highest peak, taking a few photographs there. In a beautiful ravine
we found a scorpion, whose sting causes terrible suffering.
We burned it in a fire. A French captain has been assigned to the company and
today he held a briefing with our officers.

September 27, 1940, Friday
The four of us went to Marseille for vehicles. Misiek W. and Janek S.
were issued a truck and a field kitchen, while Józek R. and I got
an old Ford which we could not get started, so it went in for repair.
With us were French non-commissioned officers, very likeable and
keen supporters of General de Gaulle. We had a bit of a drink with them.
The only newspaper published in Lyon in the Polish language writes in a
pro-German spirit, but jokingly gives one to understand that one shouldn't
believe everything it writes. As an example I am pasting in a clipping from this
newspaper.
(clipping) "From ill tidings.

MY PROVERB
Not every tiding —
Holds the truth inside it. J.M.
More and more I am beginning to feel longing for my country and family, but
I felt it most painfully today, when Stach Sadowski received a letter from Poland from
his fiancée, with news in it about his mother, who lives in Ożarów.
(photograph)
Camp de Carpiagne, general view
And yet I have family in Opatów, which lies so close to
Ożarów. I wanted to write, but I am still holding back, because I don't want
the Germans to know that I am in France.

September 28, 1940, Saturday
Before noon Miszel W. wouldn't hear a word about going to England, but
in the afternoon he got so keen on it that he actually argued with us, saying that
we were egging him on (out of spite) to make him stay.

September 29, 1940, Sunday
The Mistral is blowing, so everyone is sitting in the barracks. We receive news
that tomorrow we are to go out to work. Captain Hussakowski went to the
site to check and get acquainted with the situation. In the evening at roll call the captain
announced that conditions there are poor and that the order has been withdrawn.

September 30, 1940, Monday
In the afternoon our French friends came to visit us. Józek was the first to

meet them and let us know. We stayed with them while he went with a
Pole to Marseille for photographs, because now we urgently have to give them
photos in a green shirt with an open collar.
I walked our friends halfway home along with Jaś, and then we came
back. The platoon that had gone out to work two weeks ago came back home.
The lads spoke well of the stay there, even though, apart from their keep, they were only
given 6 francs. The captain held roll call with us and reportedly said that
we do have to go out to work after all, but the company refused. I am curious what will
come of this. Personally I would go, but for the sake of the group I do what
everyone else does. Our company puts up opposition to the French at every turn.

October 1, 1940, Tuesday
Captain Hussakowski urges the company not to resist and to go out to
work. He also asked Major Laskowski, of the camp command, to help. Nothing,
however, helped.

October 2, 1940, Wednesday
Despite a speech which Major Laskowski gave once more, the company
refused to go. Only some other major persuaded us, saying that
we must bear this sacrifice for the good of the cause, so as not to make it harder for others
to leave.
The company went. All the way there it rained, so that even our leather jackets
got soaked through. We traveled by rail to Marseille from Aubagne. There we were
quartered in barracks. The huts were the same, except that in one

hut two platoons sleep. Captain Hussakowski resigned his post and
left without saying goodbye. The photographs that Corporal Szempruch took didn't turn
out well, because he cut everyone's heads off.

October 3, 1940, Thursday
We get our bearings. Some of the men make a volleyball court. 20 men, myself
among them, fitted handles onto shovels, pickaxes and hammers for tomorrow's
work. We worked too zealously, but Lieutenant Sztrauch told us not to overexert
ourselves at work, so that too much wouldn't be expected of us. The food is
tasty but too scanty, though the French promise that tomorrow there will be more.
Outside of duty hours we have free leave into town.
In front of the barracks a photo booth (instant photos) was set up; I
had my picture taken.

October 4, 1940, Friday
First day of work. Before starting work a prayer was sung. The food
has not improved at all. Rumors are going around that any day now
we will be able to leave France.

October 5, 1940, Saturday
We work until 11 o'clock, and in the afternoon do laundry. The locality
where we are staying is called Ste. Marthe.

October 6, 1940, Sunday
A battalion of French troops, surrounded on all sides by gendarmes, arrived
at the camp here. They are probably soldiers returning from Africa. They were
locked up in several barracks surrounded by barbed wire and are closely
guarded by double sentries of both soldiers and gendarmerie. They are
undergoing quarantine here.
The whole day I did not go anywhere.

October 7, 1940, Monday
We march to work singing, as indeed we did before too.
The French like it, because they come out of their barracks and listen with great
interest. I have not yet heard the French army sing.
And once more we hand over photographs, two apiece. I must have already
given out about fifteen photographs, with no result.
Today at noon I lost my watch in the washroom.

October 8, 1940, Tuesday
For two days now the Pole Edward (a cadet officer) has been promising us that
there will be a radio, and there isn't one. The company gives him no peace over it.

October 9, 1940, Wednesday
An exceptionally lucky day. The food improved in quantity; from the
barracks the unneeded bunks were cleared out, giving us more room,
so much so that between the beds a table and two benches can now be put. This

was not accomplished, however, without opposition from certain individuals. Finally
the Pole brought the radio. A very good set. Janek S. had a tooth pulled today and
walks around looking poisoned.
Michał Bardzki's neck hurts, and my hands are covered
in blisters, and so on — everyone has something ailing them. As for work, the French
don't push us hard, so that whoever doesn't want to work doesn't have to work much. I in
this work seek forgetfulness; I work so as not to think about what is happening.

October 10, 1940, Thursday
Shirking is beginning in the company; nearly half the men are missing from
work. 300 Germans arrived at the camp, returning from the Foreign
Legion. One of the French officers asked us not to talk with these
Germans. No doubt the French are afraid of a fight breaking out between
us and them. Some contagious illness has appeared in the company, and because of
that we are forbidden to go into town.

October 11, 1940, Friday
Among the Germans who arrived there is one Pole. Apparently a rate of
1 franc an hour has been set for us. The Poles are to vacate the Carpiagne camp
by Sunday.

October 12, 1940, Saturday

For the first time in France I bathe in a military bathhouse; it is very well equipped. To my platoon a schoolmate of mine from the 7th form of St. Florian's School in Kraków was assigned — Lipka Stanisław.

October 13 Sunday, 14 Monday, 15 Tuesday, and 16 Tuesday (should read Wednesday).
Work on the road.

October 17, 1940, Thursday

Second Lieutenant Chromiński discreetly slipped 100 fr. into my pocket. It fell to me as if from heaven, since for a good while now I've been living on Jaś S.'s charity, and he doesn't have anything either.
In the evening Janek and I were at the home of a certain French family, fixing their stove. Janek found this job. What matters to me above all is the chance to practice conversation in French.

October 18, 1940, Friday
Lieutenant Sztranek is coming down harder on the company, keeping order more and more strictly. Rumors are going around that several men from our company went to Nîmes to get demobilized a second time. There is suspicion regarding a few men in this matter, but I am not certain of it. Besides, in such cases it is better not to bandy names about.

October 19, 1940, Saturday

We receive 20 francs of relief money from the Red Cross each. In the evening I was again with Janek at the home of the French family living next to the camp, fixing the stove and having a talk. We also ate a very modest supper.
Among the civilian population one senses greater hunger than in our company.

October 20, 1940, Sunday

I spend the day reading a book titled White Fang.

October 21, 1940, Monday

In the evening, while fixing the stove at our acquaintances' home, we met two Greek girls. On the radio the Prime Minister of Great Britain, C[hurchill], addressed the French nation.
The people of France are becoming more and more disposed toward the Germans.

October 22, 1940, Tuesday

While repairing the stove a bolt broke on us and we had to finish earlier. One of the Greek girls we met yesterday called on those French people twice. It looked as if she were making these visits on purpose.

October 23, 1940, Wednesday

I quarreled over something foolish with Jaś, and so, to avoid further quarrels, I went off to work with a different group. In the evening we were at a

little chat at the French family's home. I'm beginning to understand a bit of French.

The radio reports on the meeting of Hitler and General Franco at the Spanish border, as well as of Prime Minister Laval with Hitler. Details are lacking.

October 24, 1940, Thursday

We stage a strike for the second time, because the French don't want to pay us for our work as they promised. They only give a wage of 50 cents a day. We are going through moral torment, because about 6000 soldiers arrived from Syria for demobilization, and most of them come from Alsace and Lorraine. And when they found out we are Poles, they look at us as the worst people in the world. Besides that, every twist of the political situation reflects on us, because here we are, so to speak, a link between France and England and between France and Germany. The French feed us with one hand and want to strangle us with the other. On top of that, the uncertainty of tomorrow weighs badly on our souls.
Truly, our lot is not to be envied.

October 25, Friday

We get word that our departure is well underway. Lieutenant Sztraub told me so, though when it is to be, they don't know. The flame of hope has been rekindled once more.

October 26, 1940, Saturday
In the evening I was at the cinema to see "Suez," a very good film. I saw it in Poland too.
The London bulletin reports that France has signed a peace under which it was to lose a certain part of its northern territories to Germany, Tunis and the region of Nice to Italy, Morocco to Spain, Indochina to Japan.
This turned out to be untrue.

October 27, 1940, Sunday
In the evening I was out walking with Jaś and on the way we stopped in at the cinema. On the way back, while waiting for the tram, we talked with a French policeman. From that conversation I sensed that France was only forced into signing that agreement. Because the policeman winked meaningfully and said that Pétain has a head on his shoulders.

October 28, 1940, Monday
The Italians attacked Greece. Our nerves are beginning to fail us. We already have an example of this in Fiedosiejew, who fell into some kind of frenzy, having also had a bit too much to drink.
He wanted to shoot Lieutenant Strauch, the sergeant-major, accusing them of doing nothing for the company and only playing cards. The reason for this was that he had been at the American Consulate and learned that the lieutenant was helping Poles to leave. He ran around the barracks with a revolver; fortunately the officers

cleared out of the barracks, and the sergeant-major hid under a blanket on another bed.
Fiedosiejew even fired the pistol once in the barracks, but into the air.
Slowly we managed to calm him down.
Yet there is nothing so bad that no good comes of it. Because our authorities no longer play cards for hundreds of francs. I am no communist, and I don't mean to say by this that they are wrong to have so much money, because I haven't looked into that, but it hurt me to see the gentlemen playing poker all night long for hundreds of francs while I don't have a single penny. It hurts because after all the same misfortune has struck us all, and we are fighting for one and the same goal, and yet there is no unity. It's as if a hungry man were brought into a dining hall where guests are eating tasty dishes and enjoying themselves, and he can only dream about it. Jasiu offered me 20 [francs], but I refused to accept it, because I hadn't gone to help him for the sake of earning money. He treats me to what he can as it is. He himself has just as little as I do.

October 29, 1940, Tuesday

The sergeant-major gave up his post after yesterday's row and in the afternoon worked with a shovel. Only about half the company is working, the rest are loafing.
Lieutenant Sztraube was writing down the names of those who have some arrears of unpaid wages or salary, and on Thursday this matter is to be settled. As for me, though, I couldn't recall anything owed to me. Except for the war supplement, which still hasn't been paid to anyone. And I remain without hope of getting money from anywhere.

October 30, 1940, Wednesday

The French captain does not agree to the dismissal of the chief, so he remains in his post.
Many comrades brought in forms to fill out concerning the trip to the United States of North America.

October 31, 1940, Thursday

I too filled out such a form, but I have no hope of being able to leave. Besides, we are receiving radio news that Greece is defending herself well, so perhaps some changes in the situation will occur.

November 1, 1940, Friday. All Saints' Day

The patriotic spirit still remains strong within us — proof of this is the singing of the hymn "Boże Coś Polskę" [God Save Poland] in the parish church by a number of Poles, even though not the whole company attends — only those who wish to. The French stand for the entire duration of the singing of this hymn, and no one leaves the church until we finish. Jasio treats me to a beer, even though we had quite a serious argument.
