am performing. In the evening we pool money for 3 liters of wine. We go to sleep at 9:30.

January 1, 1940 - NEW YEAR

After the service we exchange New Year's greetings with acquaintances. For dinner we draw a jug of wine as our ration. Walczak, whom we call "Daddy," brings pastries and tangerines. Mr. Baranowski visits us. I have only 3 centimes in my pocket.

January 2, 1940 - Corporal Zegarłowski went to Comblessac twice to see Lieutenant Gulin, once alone and once with me, regarding confirmation of his officer-candidate qualification (cenzus). The lieutenant confirmed it and gave Zegarłowski 100 francs. A transport arrived which included former Senior Rifleman Struzik, career Corporal Sieradzki of the castle gendarmerie platoon, Corporal Wojtalik, and Corporal Masłowski.

January 3, 1940 - I reported requesting a transfer to an infantry regiment. The colonel did not agree. Sagański was assigned to the Armored Battalion. Raczkowski to the Heavy Artillery.

January 4, 1940, Thursday

Zegarłowski is assigned to the 2nd Infantry Regiment. So are Struzik and Miklas. Rumors are going around that the Assembly Station is going to be disbanded. Sergeant Winogrodzki was transferred to the 2nd Infantry Regiment, and Staff Sergeant Walter to the 3rd. We get a new roommate in the person of Sergeant Stanisław Basta.

January 5, 1940, Friday

In the evening we throw ourselves a little drinking party. The guests are Corporal Kowalski and Mr. Iwański, commonly known as "Spirytusik" (Little Spirit). Vodka is not allowed to be sold, but nevertheless Spirytusik managed to get hold of 2 bottles of cognac at 10:30 p.m. Sagański spent the night at my place.

January 6, 1940, Saturday - Epiphany (Three Kings' Day)

I hand over barrack D 81, 79, 78 to the Command Company of the Training Regiment. Szczupakowski left for the Motorized Cavalry. I received a postcard from Mr. Stanisław Budynek with New Year's greetings and

an invitation for leave.

January 7, 1940, Sunday

We set off with the nativity play to the villages where units are billeted. In one locality, whose name I don't remember, we performed the nativity play against the backdrop of a small manor house. The manor, once beautiful, is now abandoned; two women live in it, one of whose husband is at the front holding the rank of sergeant. After the nativity play Mr. Bożenko performed a piece called "Strąś," and Spirytusik sang the "Koczkodański" couplets. Aleksander played the harmonica and the choir sang two songs, titled "When Autumn, Winter, Spring Pass" and "March to Poland" (music by Mr. Tychowski).

Then we went to Guer, but we performed nothing there because they had not been informed of our arrival. (In general, Senator Rembieliński is a disaster.)

From Guer we went to Comblessac and performed the same thing there at the school of the 11th Company. The unit billeted there received us very warmly. After the performance there was a reception at a café with wine and vodka, chocolate, cheese, bread, and pastries. Aleksander played the harmonica and a tenor from Polish radio sang beautifully. We returned singing the whole way; only in Coetquidan did the senator forbid singing, which drew the indignation of the whole unit upon him. We are hungry, and if not for Walczak, who always thinks of us, this time too we would have gone to bed hungry.

January 8, 1940, Monday

It was announced in the order that tomorrow's exercises, from 7 to 7:45 a.m., will be led by Corporal Maciołek.

January 9, 1940, Tuesday

The announced exercises went generally well. Rifleman Filipczak or Filipowicz of the Administrative Company returns from leave and said that he lives not far from Miss Irena Ciemniak, and that she sends me her regards. Two gendarmes arrive from the Castle - Senior Warrant Officer Kołodziejczyk. In the evening we have a few drinks; Corporal Orłowski treats us to cognac since he's back from leave.

January 10, 1940, Wednesday

I receive a letter from Senior Warrant Officer Zarychta along with 30 francs, 20 for me and 10 for Staff Sergeant Kurnik. In the evening I have a lot of work with the storeroom.

January 11, 1940, Thursday

Nothing important to note.

January 12, 1940, Friday

I receive a postcard from Senior Warrant Officer Zarychta and a rather warm letter from Miss Irena Ciemniak. Lieutenant Colonel Hoffbauer promised me he would send me back to a regiment, but I don't know which one.

From Mr. Bożenko I receive 3 photographs from the nativity play, and in the camp newsletter there is a description and photograph of the nativity play performed at the officers' casino before President Raczkiewicz.

January 13, 1940, Saturday

Corporal Kanigowski arrives by car from Paris; I ran into him by chance in front of camp headquarters. We went to see Warrant Officer Sepkowski and agreed that we would go to Campaniac. He spent the night at my place, on the table, on which we laid two straw mattresses. In the evening the "castle men" gathered.

January 14, 1940, Sunday

We drive to Campaniac with Kanigowski, Warrant Officer Sepkowski, one sergeant, and one civilian. We stopped at Staff Sergeant Kurnik's, who is the chief of the quartermaster company. Whole companies came for the service, so did everyone from the castle column. Kanigowski went to fetch Sagański. And just before departure, Świtajewski showed up.

We were at a café for cognac, then continued it at the billet. We return to Coetquidan by 12 o'clock. I am completely drunk.

The Coetquidan orchestra gave a concert in front of headquarters. Right after the concert I say goodbye to Kanigowski, who leaves for Paris, while I go off to perform the nativity play. We are missing Tychowski, Iwański, and Raczkowski.

We drive by truck to Augan, where the artillery is stationed, to pick up Raczkowski. There we perform one show without him. We perform on the school square. Then we drive to Campaniac. At the entrance to the town we come across a plane crash. The pilot

lies dead on the road, and the aircraft is leaning against and smashed on the stone parapet of a bridge. The plane's tail lies in a meadow 200 meters from the plane. It is a French training aircraft. Eyewitnesses say that another plane clipped its tail with its landing gear in the air and cut it off completely. The whole town's mood is spoiled by this accident. Even so, there were a lot of people. We performed the nativity play in a field and changed clothes in a roadside café (restaurant).

Afterward we were invited to the officers' casino, where we ate and drank well. Major Żyrkiewicz, whom I already know from near Łuck, a very nice man, said that if not for the plane accident, people had already been assigned with drums to go around calling everyone to the performance.

Then we went to a place whose name I don't remember, but which we nicknamed "Pink-Ponk." The motorized cavalry is billeted there, and Janta-Połczyński, the author of the nativity play, is an officer there. We performed the nativity play against the backdrop of the walls of an old church, by torchlight; it turned out very well. After the performance we were taken to the recreation room for a reception. We drank only tea. We return to Coetquidan at 8:30 p.m.

January 15, 1940 - I make efforts to be released from the Assembly Station, but without success. In the evening Corporal Miller spreads rumors that the Germans have attacked Holland. Later it turned out they had only concentrated troops there, some 15

divisions strong.

January 16, 1940, Tuesday

The first light snow fell during the night. A few degrees [of frost], and in the evening a proper blizzard. Kanigowski arrived, ate dinner at my place, and afterward we went over to the canteen where Kanigowski treated us to beer, then he left for Paris. In the morning I went to barrack H65, where the Armored Forces assembly station is, so that an Armored Forces officer would agree to accept me into the Armored Forces. Unfortunately Lieutenant Czerniewski had left for Rennes. Nevertheless Warrant Officer Czapiński, with Lieutenant Colonel Hoffbauer's consent, sent in a request for my transfer to be included in the order. In the afternoon French artillery was firing on the range. I went to see, but it was too late; instead I saw old-type French tanks.

January 17, 1940, Wednesday

Snow and frost down to 7 degrees, and on top of that a temporary lack of coal. I was transferred by order of Camp Command to the Armored Forces Battalion. Order No. 16 of January 17, 1940. I seem to have luck with this number, since my arrival at the camp and confirmation of rank were also in Order No. 16, of October 10, 1939. Tomorrow I am to hand over the storeroom to Corporal Walczak, with Warrant Officer Kapusta doing the accounting.

All day I am occupied with the stamping of blankets by the French. The padlocks that I had bought at one point, and for which Second Lieutenant Plewnia had reimbursed me, I handed over - 9 of them.

January 18, 1940, Thursday

In the morning I go to report to the Armored Forces Assembly Station in barrack H65, but after waiting two hours I did not find the officer in. I return to the Assembly Station and hand over the storeroom to Corporal Walczak, with Warrant Officer Kapusta doing the accounting. The warrant officer fussed over the details, on account of which I would have missed the truck I was supposed to leave on for Campaniac, so I asked Lieutenant Colonel Hoffbauer to speed up the matter, which helped.

In the afternoon I go looking for the truck, but without success. In the evening I hold a farewell party with money we collected together.

January 19, 1940, Friday

I go to barrack H65, where the drivers sleep, and go with them to get the truck. After loading the provisions we drive off to Campaniac via Guer. After reporting to headquarters I was assigned to the Quartermaster Company. I was given quarters where 12 comrades live. Conditions here are much worse than in Coetquidan, since we sleep on straw in an enormous hall with a medieval fireplace. Coffee is given out twice a day, and once a day, at dinner, they give meat, soup, and potatoes or pasta.

The company sergeant major is Staff Sergeant Kurnik.

January 20, 1940, Saturday

In the morning I was in the forest gathering firewood. In the afternoon, chopping wood.

January 21, 1940, Sunday

Mass at 9 o'clock, celebrated by a Polish priest; we spend the rest of the day playing cards.

January 22, 1940, Monday
January 23, 1940, Tuesday
Time passes playing cards and eating. We play cards in such a way that the winner pays nothing, and the losers each pay 25 centimes for light (candles).

January 24, 1940, Wednesday

We go to the training ground for wood, but because of firing we were not let in, so we gathered wood in the farmers' fields, where there was plenty. I write a letter to Irenka. My cut finger is bothering me.

January 25, 1940, Thursday
Same as the previous day - going for wood.

January 26, 1940, Friday
After the flags are raised, an hour of exercises.

January 27, 1940, Saturday. Nothing important.

January 28, 1940, Sunday
A trip to Coetquidan. On the way, at the training ground, I find beautiful

shells, which I take as a souvenir. Walczak had my hair cut at his own expense and gave me a straw mattress. Miller told me that Mr. Filipiak had been here and said he has a package and a letter from Irena C. for me. There are rumors that we're leaving on Thursday.

January 29, 1940, Monday
Nothing important, in good spirits.

January 30, 1940, Tuesday
In the evening Corporal Francuz takes me out for hot wine.

January 31, 1940, Wednesday
Preparations for departure.

February 1, 1940

At 5 o'clock Corporal Biliński woke me up, saying I was to go with him and Mr. Wolski to the station at Augan with the provisions. Before departure I receive 21 francs in pay. At 7:30 we are at the station in Augan. We find the station deserted, because the stationmaster - the one and only man who handles everything connected with running the station - had gone to a café. We finally find him. Units gradually stream in, and at 2:45 p.m. the transport departs via Guer and Rennes, Tours, Bourges, Lyon, to Orange station. The journey lasted 38 hours.

February 3, 1940, Saturday

We unload at the station - new assignments. I am assigned to the Armored Forces Training Center. The commander here is Lieutenant Józef Jaroński, the sergeant major is Staff Sergeant Aleksander Sagański. Corporal Albert Świtajewski is also here.

We are billeted in the locality of Serignan. The quarters themselves are just outside the town, in coach-houses and stables, but they look reasonably decent.

The area is very beautiful, surrounded on all sides by mountains, and one very high peak is even still covered in snow, although no snow is to be seen anywhere else in the area, and the midday temperature reaches 20 degrees. The fields here are like gravel pits, so many stones lie on them, and everywhere one sees nothing but vineyards. Right after arriving on the spot I am appointed uniform NCO (in charge of clothing supplies).

February 4, 1940, Saturday

I hand over the storeroom, which holds 250 blankets, to Sergeant Kasprowicz, and I become the office NCO. The sergeant major treats us to dinner at a restaurant, since the provisions were not delivered.

February 5, 1940, Sunday

The provisions were still not brought to us and we have nothing to eat. Świtajewski buys bacon, bread, sugar, and tea, for which my kettle bought in Coetquidan and a frying pan came in handy. In the afternoon I go with Corporal Francuz and Rifleman Witkowski, who holds the cenzus (officer-candidate qualification), on a trip into the mountains. The view was wonderful. That high peak in particular looked beautiful.

February 6, 1940, Tuesday

The time goes on making lists. In the evening I go out for a walk with Corporal Francuz; during the walk we meet Engineer Witkowski and Mr. Gilewski. In the course of the conversation Mr. Witkowski says that he had just gone to look for a little cellar which we had found on Sunday's walk in the mountains. I made a bet with him of 1 liter of mulled wine that I would find it despite the darkness. We set off to search for the cellar, located 2 km away in the mountains and woods. I found it, winning the bet. But there was no money for wine, since the engineer only had 1 franc 50 centimes. On the way back we sat down in front of a café where the sergeant major, Sagański, was sitting and drinking.

After a while he came out and asked what we were doing there. And when he learned about the bet, he borrowed 5 francs from a corporal and lent it to me. Five of us went off for wine, which was very good.

February 7, 1940, Wednesday - Ash Wednesday

In the morning the company went to church.

In the evening, by order from the battalion, I was transferred to the 2nd Battalion, 1st Company.

February 8, 1940, Thursday

The sergeant major spoke with the lieutenant about keeping me at the Training Center, but nothing could be done; after dinner we set off for Sainte-Cécile, to km 2131. I get a good billet; there are 8 of us NCOs sleeping there.

February 9, 1940, Friday

In the morning, a talk on terrain reconnaissance; in the afternoon, exercises; in the evening I go to collect back pay - I get 175 centimes for 1 day, but I had no change to give, so I received 2 francs. In the evening I go to bed early.

February 10, 1940, Saturday

Terrain reconnaissance, and in the afternoon free time for laundry and tidying up. For the first time in France I take up laundry, consisting of 2 towels, 1 handkerchief, 2 pairs of socks. Up until now it always somehow worked out that someone washed for me, since I had a few francs; now there's a crisis.

February 11, 1940, Sunday

A funeral mass on account of the death of one of the citizens of the town of Sainte-Cécile-les-Vignes, and for this reason we do not sing in church.

In the afternoon I meet Corporal Roman Francuz, who holds the cenzus, and his friend, and we go for wine which costs 2 fr. 50 c. a liter, but it is not allowed to drink it inside the establishment, so we borrow glasses and sit on stones by the shop entrance out on the street, drinking. At 3:30 we part ways for supper, arranging to meet again at 4:15. After meeting up we go for a walk to Rochegude, and on returning, to the café for hot wine with cinnamon and cloves. We recall Tuesday's wine with oranges in Serignan.

Monday, February 12, 1940

Despite the rainy weather, drills; in the afternoon during gymnastics there was a small clash between the 2nd Lt., deputy company commander, and one Cpl. The professional NCOs were paid their first salary. I was registered as a long-service (extended-term) soldier.

Tuesday, February 13, 1940

The weather changed overnight. Strong wind and frost down to 5 degrees. Despite this we set out for drills, but the battalion commander turned us back along the way. I received a letter from Irenka C. For the past few days letters have been checked by the company commanders. The company commanders are cautioning us about writing letters, and they are absolutely right to do so.

Wednesday, February 14, 1940

The weather hasn't changed at all. I'm on patrol duty together with Sgt. Gbur and one Sr. Pvt.

Thursday, February 15, 1940

The weather is unchanged; in the morning I rest after patrol duty and do some laundering of handkerchiefs. In the afternoon, singing lessons.

Friday, February 16, 1940

Laundry: 2 shirts and 2 pairs of long underwear.

Saturday, February 17, 1940

I get a letter from Grześkowiak Czesław; he informs me that he's been promoted to Cpl. I receive a money order for a parcel from Irenka C. I went to Serignan to collect it, but it turned out I had to go to Orange for it, so I turned back, since we're not allowed to go to Orange because of an epidemic of meningitis. I left the money order at the restaurant across from the bus stop so the driver could bring the parcel to me. It should arrive Monday afternoon.

Sunday, February 18, 1940

At Mass in church I met Sgt. Świtajewski, and he told me that Sr. S/Sgt. Sagański is now driving for Col. Dworak. Before noon I iron my laundry.

Monday, February 19, 1940

Nothing important to note.

Tuesday, February 20, 1940

The matter of my verification is finally cleared up, and I draw my pay, totaling 262 francs. While collecting my pay I run into Sr. S/Sgt. Sagański, who gave me back 15 francs for the kettle I had left with him in Serignan. In the evening we celebrate our pay with cognac.

Wednesday, February 21, 1940

I send Lt. Łowczowski back 50 francs, and in the evening I write a letter to Lt.

10

Łowczowski, and to Grześkowiak and Irenka C.

The weather, yesterday and today, is very nice and warm. I lent Cpl. Sułek 30 francs, since he's going on leave and has no money for anything.

Thursday, February 22, 1940

In the evening I went to see Sgt. Świtajewski but didn't find him in, as he had gone on leave. Then I went to the cinema, where they were showing a film with Marta Eggerth — in Poland, I believe, it was shown under the title "The Nightingale of Vienna" — along with a very silly French comedy and a P.A.T. newsreel.

Friday, February 23, 1940

I buy myself a pocket flashlight, paying 18 francs.

Saturday, February 24, 1940

At 1 p.m. we're supposed to leave for Serignan or Orange to bathe, but we're late, and the bus pulls away right from under our noses. We set off on foot, and along the way we try hitchhiking; one car picked up three of the men, who had our laundry with them, while Cpl. Dierzyk and I go on foot. For the first time this year I saw a tree in bloom.

In Serignan I bathed under the shower, while Cpl. Dierzyk went on to Orange, since he didn't want to bathe there; afterward I had to put the same clothes back on, since his and my laundry were with the ones who had gone to Orange.
