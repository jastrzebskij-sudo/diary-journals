Lieutenant Skowronski didn't want to grant leave in the first round, since I hadn't yet finished my tank-driving exams (driving over obstacles). But at my insistent requests, he agreed.

May second, nineteen forty Not everyone who was granted leave by the company commander gets to go, because the battalion staff ran short of blank leave cards. I, however, am the lucky one who got one. Besides me, Corporal Dzierzyk also received one, as did the company sergeant-major, Sr. S/Sergeant Kobylanski, and one Sr. Pvt. whose name I don't know. We take a taxi to Orange, because, owing to Dzierzyk's slow nature , popularly nicknamed "the grump" or "baldy" , we're running late for the bus. We leave Orange on the leave train at twenty-one:forty-five, traveling via Lyon.

May third, nineteen forty. Dzierzyk didn't want to change trains at Lyon, insisting he'd been told the change was only one hundred kilometers before Paris, so we rode on, sleeping, not knowing where we were going. Morning found us at Besançon, by the Swiss border. Using the map I had gotten hold of before setting out, we realized we were going the wrong way, and got off. We can't make ourselves understood, since neither of us knows French. By gestures we learned that the leave train wouldn't come for another nine hours, and we were told to wait, and were sent to the canteen.
