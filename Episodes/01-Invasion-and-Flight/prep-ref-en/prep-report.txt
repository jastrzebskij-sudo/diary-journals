1-2	I begin this diary with a summary of past events, starting from the moment war broke out, that is, from September first, nineteen thirty-nine.
1-2	On that historic day, being at Fort Vola for supplies, I was bombed; several bombs fell very close by.
1-2	After the air raid, which took place around three in the afternoon, many people still did not believe it was war.
1-2	The next few days passed under the threat of continual bombardment.
1-2	On September sixth I left Warsaw, evacuating the remaining equipment of the Supply Company of the third Armored Battalion.
1-2	The column, under the command of Lieutenant Yaronski, waited for the rest of the Battalion at Saska Kempa.
1-2	Around eleven o'clock the rest arrived and we set off for Lublin via Otvotsk.
1-2	We drove without lights; fortunately the night was moonlit.
1-2	The column drove in complete order along the Vistula embankment, but once it reached the Warsaw-Lublin highway, where there was enormous congestion, the column fell apart.
1-2	Near Garvolin I met several vehicles from our column, but none of our officers were among them.
1-2	We stood for an hour, then drove on; past Riki we were attacked by German aircraft and took cover in the forest.
1-3	After the planes flew off, a few more vehicles joined us.
1-3	Among those who arrived was Sergeant Vrona, head of the garage on Goorchevska Street.
1-3	Around one in the afternoon we set off again, one by one so as to be safer from air raids, with the assembly point designated at the barracks of the Armored Battalion in Lublin.
1-3	I arrived first; after some time a few more vehicles joined.
1-3	By eight in the evening none of the officers had arrived, and we had no fuel.
1-3	After a conference we drove onto the highway toward Hewm.
1-3	We spent the night on the highway.
1-3	Toward morning the Park Column of the second Battalion passed by, under the command of Captain Ciaglinski, and they gave us fuel; I joined this column because I saw order here.
1-3	Traffic on the highway was so heavy it was hard to drive in a column.
1-3	In Hewm we learned that a motor column was standing in the forest beyond the town, so we drove to join it.
1-3	Just as we reached it, German planes swept in and bombed the railway stations where our column had stood five minutes earlier.
1-3	We drove on toward Wootsk; nothing important happened along the way.
1-3	In Wootsk the column took different streets, and the rest of us, through one driver's carelessness, went in a different direction.
1-3	We lost a couple of hours finding the column.
1-4	We found it in the forest near Ulana.
1-4	Wootsk had already been bombed.
1-4	The infantry barracks were completely destroyed, and so were the Armored Corps barracks.
1-4	The entire Armored Battalion left Wootsk for the forest and joined our column, forming an Armored Corps center.
1-4	We sat in the forest for a few days; no one was allowed to drive vehicles or walk along the firebreaks.
1-4	Only walking through the forest itself was allowed.
1-4	No one was permitted to go far from the forest.
1-4	After great effort I nevertheless obtained permission to go into Wootsk for supplies.
1-4	In town you could not get bread.
1-4	You could get sausage, but you had to search for hours, because whatever the butcher prepared was snapped up at once, and then you had to wait for a new batch.
1-4	One evening we received orders to move out.
1-4	The night was dark , you couldn't see three steps ahead , and no lights were allowed.
1-4	As we pulled onto the highway, my jeep's engine stalled and the whole column drove off without me.
1-4	I struggled with the vehicle alone, since no one would help me; luckily I had bought a pocket flashlight in Wootsk the day before.
1-4	At that point several local residents came along and pushed me, and the engine started.
1-4	I caught up with the column and we drove along country roads, through ditches and sand, without lights.
1-5	That was one of the worst nights.
1-5	We drove through Brody to Podkamien.
1-5	At Podkamien we stood in the forest for one day, and with nightfall we drove on through Tarnopol, Trembowla, Kopyczynce.
1-5	We stopped in the forest near Czortkow.
1-5	Around eight o'clock planes flew over, but from the east, which greatly puzzled me, and they circled over the forest for a long time.
1-5	Two hours later one of the officers rushed in and ordered engines started; in great haste we abandoned the camp and drove by side roads to Czortkow.
1-5	It was Sunday and there was unusual activity in town.
1-5	A few kilometers past Czortkow we met a column of Soviet tanks.
1-5	We passed them.
1-5	They behaved very politely; we offered them cigarettes and they said they were coming to help us, that they were coming to beat the Germans.
1-5	We reached Horodenka, where we witnessed a bombing.
1-5	From there to quarters at Serafince.
1-5	We spent the night in Serafince and I repaired the clutch.
1-5	In the morning I went out to gather news, and it turned out the column had crossed the Romanian border during the night.
1-5	I met Major Segda, who had also stayed behind at the quarters, and the column had left without waking him.
1-6	On returning to the vehicles I found a second lieutenant of the Air Force waiting for me, as the senior man of this small group of vehicles, who proposed forming a sabotage unit.
1-6	Of course I agreed very willingly.
1-6	The second lieutenant told us to drive to the border crossing point to draw fuel, while he himself went to look for more.
1-6	At the border point there were no longer any vehicles with fuel, and the second lieutenant, after talking with Major Segda, gave up on the sabotage unit idea.
1-6	We stood at the border in a field the whole day, not knowing what to do with ourselves.
1-6	The odometer on my vehicle read thirteen,zero.
1-6	It is Irena's name day, the name day of my last sweetheart from Warsaw.
1-6	Before evening the decision was made to cross the border.
1-6	I removed the eagle insignia from my shoulder straps and cap, put on a beret instead, and off we went.
1-6	Crossing the border I felt so pained that I could not hold back tears; I glanced sideways at my traveling companion, whose surname I don't remember, and saw that he too was crying, so I could no longer restrain myself and wept aloud.
1-6	After several hours of driving we found ourselves in Czerniowce.
1-6	We arrived in the evening and spent the night on the main street.
1-7	In the morning the nearby residents were already giving us breakfast; my companion in misfortune received twenty-five kilograms of grapes from some gentleman.
1-7	We had food beyond counting, and everyone walked around pleased at how warmly we were received.
1-7	Mister Podsudek, a Pole who owned a bacon factory, made the greatest effort on our behalf.
1-7	Around noon the police drove us out beyond the city so as not to obstruct traffic, which we managed only with great difficulty.
1-7	We stood outside the city on the highway for two days and two nights; everyone sold whatever they could, and I did the same, earning two,zero lei that way.
1-7	We could not travel further because we lacked fuel.
1-7	The Romanians would not give us any and instead ordered us to hand over our vehicles at the Makabi sports field.
1-7	We did not want to do this, but I received an order from Major Segda and drove my van to the sports stadium.
1-7	The Romanians loaded all the drivers onto trucks and drove them off to a camp; I had already decided to go along and put my knapsack onto the truck.
1-7	The previous night someone had stolen my suitcase containing a large number of photographs.
1-7	Just before departure a young, elegant gentleman came up to me and proposed that I go with him to Bucharest as his driver, because he had a car which up to then had been driven by his friend, whom the Romanians had arrested.
1-8	I agreed very willingly, took my suitcase, and got into the cab that was already waiting for us.
1-8	On the way this gentleman introduced himself as Zygmunt Krosnowski.
1-8	I showed him my identification from the Castle Company and the driving license I had received in the third Armored Battalion.
1-8	The car was a CHEVROLET limousine.
1-8	I felt a bit awkward driving it at first, but after a while I got the hang of it.
1-8	Mister Krosnowski introduced me to his brother-in-law, Deputy Minister Tyszka, who within an hour arranged a photograph and passport for me.
1-8	People sometimes waited days for a passport.
1-8	From Czerniowce we set out with two cars; in the first rode Mister Tyszka and Mister Krosnowski, with an engineer driving his own car whose surname I don't remember, and in the second I drove Mrs.
1-8	Tyszkowa with her son.
1-8	We reached Bucharest safely, where we stopped at the Hotel Nissa on Boulevard Elissabeta.
1-8	The first night I slept in the hotel, and for the rest of my stay I slept in the car, which at night I would drive out and park by the Polish Consulate on Strada Polonia.
1-9	My time in Bucharest was spent constantly driving around the city on its narrow, one-way streets.
1-9	I ate supper and breakfast at the eatery by the consulate, since, as I mentioned above, that is where I spent my nights.
1-9	There too I had many acquaintances.
1-9	One evening we even celebrated a wedding.
1-9	The bride and groom had met on the road while fleeing Poland, and since the young lady had no one to look after her, they married.
1-9	I ate lunch at an eatery on the other side of the Hotel Nissa.
1-9	There I again had a friend, a fifteen-year-old Romanian, with whom I communicated by gestures and who would not let anyone cheat me.
1-9	In return he received a couple of silver Polish coins as a keepsake.
1-9	I also had another friend, a Warsaw taxi driver, with whom I nearly always ate lunch, and once I went with him to see some girls.
1-9	He took me to a brothel, very elegantly furnished.
1-9	The women, dressed in transparent garments through which the whole shape of their bodies could be seen, sat in a glassed-in room, and if a client picked one out he would call her over and go with her to a nicely
1-9	furnished little room.
1-9	My chosen one was Hungarian, and I communicated with her by gestures.
1-9	After two weeks Mister Tyszko sent his wife to Hungary and sold the car, giving me two,zero lei from the sale.
1-9	I went to the embassy and enrolled in the Polish Army in France, receiving five,zero lei for the journey and a French visa.
1-9	Mister Krosnowski arranged the Yugoslav visa for me, and an Italian one was not needed since it was a country of transit for tourists.
1-9	At the Consulate I met General Schally, Captain Krynski, Lieutenant Gulin, Mister Baranowski, and Sergeant Kurnik.
1-9	I drove Lieutenant Gulin and Mister Baranowski from the Embassy to the hotel where they were staying with General Schally.
1-9	Through Lieutenant Gulin's intervention I received two hundred zloty from the general, which I later exchanged in France for francs, receiving one,zero francs.
1-9	I left Bucharest in the evening hours, and by around eight o'clock in the morning I was at the Yugoslav border.
1-9	In Yugoslavia (in Subotica) ladies of the Red Cross gave us lunch and asked that anyone who wished write a letter to family in Poland.
1-9	I wrote that I was alive and nothing more.
1-9	In the evening we were in Zagreb, where we were welcomed by representatives of the Polish consulate and ladies of the Red Cross; from these ladies I received a sweater.
1-10	In the crush that filled the train car, my wallet was stolen, containing one hundred francs and one hundred zloty.
1-10	At dawn the next day I was in Trieste.
1-10	The rail line runs above the sea along a high cliff.
1-10	It was the first time I had seen the sea and I was enchanted by the sight of it.
1-10	By midday we were in Venice.
1-10	Then through Milan and Turin to Modane, the first railway station in France.
1-10	In Modane I met Mister Krosnowski, who had left Bucharest a day before me.
1-10	In the afternoon hours we left Modane for Paris.
1-10	We arrived in Paris in the morning hours.
1-10	We took the metro to the other station, where we left our suitcases, and set off to see Paris.
1-10	I visited the Church of the Invalides; Napoleon's tomb, like other things, was covered with sandbags for protection against bombs.
1-10	I stood beneath the Eiffel Tower , one was not allowed up the tower because of the war.
1-10	I saw Napoleon's Arc de Triomphe, beneath which lies the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier.
1-10	The Champs-Élysées are lovely.
1-11	I left Paris around two in the afternoon for Rennes and from there to Guer.
1-11	And there a sergeant of the Polish Army already drove us to Coëtquidan.
1-11	I was temporarily quartered in barrack B fifteen, from where I was assigned to the billeting platoon.
1-11	After a two-week stay I was appointed by Lieutenant Colonel Hofbauer as commandant of transit barrack B fifteen.
1-11	There I took Bombardier Jozef Walczak, a very honest man, as my assistant.
1-11	From the Castle unit I am the first, with number twelve sixty-two.
1-11	I borrowed a passport from a gentleman I did not know at all, named Mister Sumyk, and exchanged cash.
1-11	After some time the transit quarters were moved to building C one, where I am commandant of half the building.
1-11	The next to arrive from the Castle unit were Wladyslaw Golebiowski and Dominik Szpak.
1-11	After their discharge they had worked at the P.Z.L. plant in Mielec.
1-11	I took them on as assistants and they were with me for about three weeks, but they were not suited to such duties and I sent them off to the fourth Infantry Regiment.
1-11	Next to arrive were Senior Sergeant Leopold Kurnik from the Castle column, Senior Wachmistrz Jozef Zarychta with the gendarmerie, and Corporal Pawlowski and Corporal Matyjasik, both from the gendarmerie.
1-12	A few days later several riflemen arrived from the motor column, and Corporal of police, formerly a rifleman of the Castle Company, Dziadek.
1-12	One day Lieutenant Wlodzimierz Gulin arrived and stayed temporarily in my quarters.
1-12	About three weeks before Lieutenant Gulin, Mister Baranowski had arrived and is in the seventh Officer Cadet Company.
1-12	The officer cadet companies moved to quarters at Guer, and the eleventh Company to Comblessac.
1-12	I tried to get a transfer to the Armored Corps or to one of the infantry regiments, but Lieutenant Colonel Hofbauer would not allow it.
1-12	The Armored Battalion is commanded by Captain Ropelewski, former commander of the President's Motor Column.
1-12	Lieutenant Yaronski is also there.
1-12	Around December tenth, nineteen thirty-nine, a transport of five hundred men arrived from Romania and Hungary.
1-12	Among them are Warrant Officers Zygmunt Czajkowski and Sepkowski.
1-12	On the next transport Corporal Świtajewski arrived from the Castle Column.
1-12	From the last class year of the Castle Company only Senior Rifleman Czeslaw Grzeskowiak arrived, and he went off to the fourth Infantry Regiment.
1-12	The assembly station grew by a few more barracks and the staff also grew, to the point of excess, so that I now do virtually nothing.
1-12	For a time Lieutenant Colonel Hofbauer was replaced by Cadet Officer Strutynski, later by Cavalry Captain Rudnicki, who fell ill from the work, and in his place came Cavalry Captain Kalinowski.
1-12	Matters concerning non-commissioned officers are handled by Second Lieutenant Łowczowski and Warrant Officer Czapinski.
1-12	One of the assistants is Corporal Raczkowski.
1-12	The head of the Station's Supply Section is Sergeant Leon Winogrodzki.
1-12	With this I end the summary and begin writing daily entries.
1-12	/signed/ Stefan Pisulewski, Corporal Chronicle

2-3	December fifteenth, nineteen thirty-nine.
2-3	Warrant Officer Czapinski caught the first louse.
2-3	I enrolled in the drama circle and was given the role of Kacper, one of the Three Kings.
2-3	Walczak got hold of a bottle of delicious wine from somewhere.
2-3	December sixteenth.
2-3	A transport arrives from Belgium (about one hundred fifty men); we welcome them with a band.
2-3	I receive an invitation to spend the holidays in Lille from a certain Mister Stanislaw Budynek, whom I happened to meet in a café and, while drunk, gave him my address.
2-3	I don't know what to do.
2-3	At the drama circle meeting my role was changed to Balthazar.
2-3	I wrote back to Mister Budynek that I would not be coming for the holidays.
2-3	We arranged ourselves a magnificent bath in little tubs used for carrying coal.
2-3	Second Lieutenant Łowczowski lent me fifty francs.
2-3	December seventeenth, Sunday
2-3	I got up in good spirits, singing.
2-3	I was at High Mass in church.
2-3	The band played and the priest gave an uplifting sermon.
2-3	After the service the band gave a concert on the square.
2-3	In the afternoon Golebiowski and Szpak visited me, not counting the regular guests, Corporal Pawlowski and Corporal Matyjasik.
2-3	There was also another corporal whose surname I don't know, a sailor, a solid fellow.
2-3	We had food and drink beyond counting.
2-3	The frost was quite severe and our coal ration was greatly reduced.
2-3	December eighteenth, Monday
2-3	A transport of about ten hundred men arrived.
2-3	I took charge of three barracks in the H.eighty-one area, seventy-nine, seventy-seven.
2-4	Besides, my godmother sent me a package containing a sweater, a wool scarf, socks, gloves, chocolate, and cigarettes.
2-4	December nineteenth, nineteen thirty-nine, Tuesday
2-4	I received a package from one of the conscripts, from the Polish Women's Circle, from Mrs.
2-4	Marja Wojciechowska.
2-4	December twentieth, twenty-one, twenty-two, nineteen thirty-nine.
2-4	Nothing important to note.
2-4	We go to rehearsals at the cinema.
2-4	Pawlowski and Matyjasek left for Angers.
2-4	December twenty-third, nineteen thirty-nine - Christmas Eve
2-4	We perform the nativity play (yasevka) at the officers' casino in the presence of President Raczkiewicz, General Sikorski, and members of the government; we gave them a surprise.
2-4	Afterward we performed it again in the Administrative Company at the Assembly Station, and then had Christmas Eve supper standing in the recreation-room tearoom.
2-4	I have never had a Christmas Eve like this one - there were a few tins of sprats, potatoes, fish, and tea.
2-4	It was a fine sight to see a colleague, dressed as the King of England, eating fish while holding it in one hand and a piece of bread in the other.
2-4	After the meal - for it could hardly be called a proper Christmas Eve supper - we went to the officer cadets' casino and performed the nativity play there.
2-4	At nine o'clock I was asleep.
2-4	December twenty-fourth, nineteen thirty-nine, Sunday
2-4	We are putting on a performance at the cinema at three:thirty in the evening, and a second one at eight.
2-4	Taking part are also French soldiers and artists who came from Paris Radio.
2-5	December twenty-fifth - I am busy with performances all afternoon; at ten in the morning a transport arrived from Romania, among whom was a passing acquaintance from Bucharest, I believe a doctor from the State Forests [administration].
2-5	December twenty-sixth - Second day of the holiday, we are working.
2-5	December twenty-seventh - Nothing important.
2-5	December twenty-eighth - ditto.
2-5	December twenty-ninth - Corporal Walczak returns from leave, and on this occasion there is a feast well-lubricated with alcohol, attended by the sergeant major, Warrant Officer Czapinski, and Sergeant Pieszczuchowicz.
2-5	Sergeant Winogradzki was removed from his post over some unexplained matter.

2-5	Saganski and Zegarlowski went with me right away to Comblessac to see Lieutenant Gulin, whom we caught just as he was setting off for Guer; along the way we talked about all our acquaintances.
2-5	On the way we stocked up on wine, sprats, and sugar.
2-5	Zegarlowski and Saganski slept at my place.
2-5	Warrant Officer Grzyb stayed with a colleague, and Miklas in the general hall.
