My time in Bucharest was spent constantly driving around the city on its narrow, one-way streets. I ate supper and breakfast at the eatery by the consulate, since, as I mentioned above, that is where I spent my nights. There too I had many acquaintances. One evening we even celebrated a wedding. The bride and groom had met on the road while fleeing Poland, and since the young lady had no one to look after her, they married.

I ate lunch at an eatery on the other side of the Hotel Nissa. There I again had a friend, a fifteen-year-old Romanian, with whom I communicated by gestures and who would not let anyone cheat me. In return he received a couple of silver Polish coins as a keepsake.

I also had another friend, a Warsaw taxi driver, with whom I nearly always ate lunch, and once I went with him to see some girls. He took me to a brothel, very elegantly furnished. The women, dressed in transparent garments through which the whole shape of their bodies could be seen, sat in a glassed-in room, and if a client picked one out he would call her over and go with her to a nicely

furnished little room. My chosen one was Hungarian, and I communicated with her by gestures.

After two weeks Mister Tyszko sent his wife to Hungary and sold the car, giving me two,zero lei from the sale. I went to the embassy and enrolled in the Polish Army in France, receiving five,zero lei for the journey and a French visa. Mister Krosnowski arranged the Yugoslav visa for me, and an Italian one was not needed since it was a country of transit for tourists. At the Consulate I met General Schally, Captain Krynski, Lieutenant Gulin, Mister Baranowski, and Sergeant Kurnik. I drove Lieutenant Gulin and Mister Baranowski from the Embassy to the hotel where they were staying with General Schally. Through Lieutenant Gulin's intervention I received two hundred zloty from the general, which I later exchanged in France for francs, receiving one,zero francs.

I left Bucharest in the evening hours, and by around eight o'clock in the morning I was at the Yugoslav border. In Yugoslavia (in Subotica) ladies of the Red Cross gave us lunch and asked that anyone who wished write a letter to family in Poland. I wrote that I was alive and nothing more. In the evening we were in Zagreb, where we were welcomed by representatives of the Polish consulate and ladies of the Red Cross; from these ladies I received a sweater.
