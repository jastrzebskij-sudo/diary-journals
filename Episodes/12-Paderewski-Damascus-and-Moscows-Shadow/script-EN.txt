On the mast were placed the names of the Polish ships that have been sunk in this war. Everything was proceeding in the best order, but life always has its veto, and so it was here too, because the supplier who was to deliver 100 liters of wine — colloquially called "ink" — was stopped at the guardhouse and the wine was confiscated. A great loss, but we hope that Major Fuzeau will get it released for us.

June 29, 1941, Sunday

The wine was released from arrest and the barrel was set up under the mast with the flags, and the Feast of the Sea celebration began. Wine was drunk and eaten with bread and tomato salad. Several songs were sung, one of the participants did an impression of "Wiech" [a Polish humorist], and it ended with the singing of the national anthem. Thus ended the official part, at which the company commander, Lt. Piątkowski, was present. From afternoon until late evening unofficial drinking continued, and it reached such a point that one of the participants in this drinking bout used Tadek Mazur's guitar as a chamber pot.

June 30, 1941, Monday

The morning news reports the death of Ignacy Paderewski.

July 1, 1941, Tuesday

News from the Soviet front puts us in poor spirits. We fear that this war, so longed-for by us, will end too quickly and the Germans will not be sufficiently worn down. However, we have faith in the prophecies of Wernyhora, where in one place it says that the Black Eagle will turn to the east and return with a broken wing, and that the hammer and sickle will be defeated in the east (this refers to Japan).

July 2, 1941, Wednesday

Not 20 men but 40 now go to work on the sports field, and we hope that next week even more will go. Today Lt. Piątkowski locked up Oprychał and Kozakiewicz in the guardhouse for refusing to go to work.

I would like to write a letter to Poland, but I do not have even a centime for the letter and the postage, and besides, what would I write, since what I would want to say is not allowed.

July 3, 1941, Thursday

Our hall did not turn out for the morning roll call in protest over the locking up of Oprychał. Because he is a very quiet man, and since he refused to go to work he was in the right. After roll call the company commander summoned our whole hall to the office and looked into the matter; besides that, the hall asked for a change of hall commandant. Lt. Piątkowski gave no answer, but in the afternoon Oprychał was released from the guardhouse, while Kozakiewicz was transferred to prison for 15 days.

June [sic] 4, 1941, Friday
50 men are now working at the stadium today.

July 5, 1941, Saturday

On the day of Ignacy Paderewski's funeral (as the radio reports), a funeral service was held in the parish church, at which

Major Fuzeau, our friend, took part. I imagine what our life in Saïda would be like if someone else were the commandant here given today's political situation. We would have hell.

July 6, 1941, Sunday

As usual for every holiday. Holy Mass was celebrated by some visiting priest who, after finishing the Mass, during the singing of our "Boże coś Polskę," wanted to leave the altar, not knowing what this hymn was, but one of the altar boys (a French scout) held him back by the surplice and the priest remained until the end.

July 7, 1941, Monday
Normal, no changes.

July 8, 1941, Tuesday

In the afternoon the French drove us out again to the ravine because an Italian commission was supposed to arrive. The sun has already scorched all the grass; only the trees are green, but their leaves are already falling, as with us in autumn.

July 9, 1941, Wednesday

Rumors are going around that General Weygand is to come to Saïda. I am working on building a sports field. Radio from London reports that Syria is asking for a ceasefire.

July 10, 1941, Thursday

Last night Ślęzak was beaten for absolutely no reason. The perpetrators were Fiszer, Podniesiński, and one more whose surname I don't know, commonly called Bazyl. All of them were locked up under arrest. People just cannot sit still without causing trouble.

Apparently a letter came to Major Fuzeau saying we are to go to the Sahara.

July 11, 1941, Friday

We are working at the stadium, where we had no small amount of amusement because Mietek Grzegorzewski lost his license for all vehicles, since the mule he used to haul stones would not obey him at all, and the French lieutenant had to relieve him of that job. Radio reports that the Vichy government rejected the English ceasefire conditions.

July 12, 1941, Saturday

Briefing by the company commander, Lieutenant Piątkowski, at which he acquaints us with the situation. It is not as bad as it seemed to me.

(2 photographs)
Karwaciński Stanisław; Szymański Mieczysław.

June 13, 1941, Sunday

A truly unlucky 13th. I already had my fill of shame in church because the choir did not sing, since it had not been released from work duty for rehearsals during the time when

work was going on at the stadium, and in general nothing went right. General Deutz signs the ceasefire in Syria.

July 14, 1941, Monday

We honored the French national holiday with a service in church, free from work, and good food and very abundant wine — for example, we got 4 times more than usual.

July 15, 1941, Tuesday

I am putting in my turn of work at the stadium.

July 16, 1941, Wednesday

Today I found in a French newspaper the motto of the French combatants' legion, and it is "travail, famille, patrie," while ours is "God, Honor, and Fatherland." I wonder which of these is better. We lost our Fatherland but kept our faith and honor, while they lost their fatherland and their honor. Placing family before fatherland already proved ruinous for France, because the French soldier preferred to individually defend his family rather than fight at the front in the last war.

July 17, 1941, Thursday

Today a Polish priest arrived from Algiers and after supper gave the first talk in the dining hall. He spoke very well, so we all listened with great attention. It is impossible for me to give even a summary in the diary, but in general I will say it was a speech more patriotic than religious. At the end he asked that we sing together with him 3 verses of the "Rota." And while singing this song, hearts hardened by misfortune softened like wax under the influence of warmth, and I saw how a dozen or so men furtively wiped away a tear stubbornly forcing its way to their eyes. What power the words of the "Rota" hold within them will be felt only by one who has lived through what we are living through today.

July 18, 1941, Friday

Today those of the men who do not work at the stadium attended Mass and the talk in church, and in the afternoon the choir had confession.

July 19, 1941, Saturday

Confession for the rest, myself included. In general about ¾ of the company came to confession.

July 20, 1941, Sunday

At a special Polish Holy Mass we took Holy Communion, and the priest delivered a sermon, which surprised the French present at our Mass because he did not read from a book as French priests do. After the Mass the priest thanked the choir for the singing. In the afternoon a football match took place between our team and the legionnaires. We won 6–2. There are several good players among those who recently arrived to us from Tunis. Today 3 more Poles arrived who had spent a long time in prison for illegally crossing the border.

July 21, 1941, Monday

Today a Polish requiem Mass was held for the souls of soldiers of the 10th Armored Cavalry Brigade who fell. A French colonel unexpectedly came to Saïda to check whether the Poles are working, because Major Fuzeau had been writing reports saying the Poles were working, while Aspirant Grand-Jean wrote that they didn't want to do anything. Well, the colonel found the Poles working properly at the stadium of our enemy, blessed it thoroughly, and Major Fuzeau gave him the opinion that he is a ham and completely limited in mind, at the same time asking for him to be removed from his post. Lieutenant Raczyński told us about this.

July 22, 1941, Tuesday

Lieutenant Raczyński read us today an order that we are to draw uniforms for the Sahara and that we may leave any day now.

July 23, 1941, Wednesday

A radio bulletin from London reports that the Vichy government handed over the naval bases in Indochina to Japan and agreed to the occupation of that country by Japan.

July 24, 1941, Thursday

I am starting to attend choir rehearsals, to which Szpetnar invited me, though he himself is withdrawing from it because he has signed up for the legion's orchestra until he can legally leave Africa. Kula, Kaszubowski, and Czarnecki went along with him.

July 25, 1941, Friday

I am selling, through Cierkasz as intermediary, a leather jacket for 300 francs; from the Red Cross we get a relief payment of 40 fr.

July 26, 1941, Saturday
Nothing to note.

July 27, 1941, Sunday
Like every previous Sunday.

July 28, 1941, Monday

I am sending two letters, one to Poland and the other to Janek Sadowski in Marseille. The choir sang today during the requiem Mass for the soul of a legionnaire NCO who fell in the war. In the evening, with Dziadkowiec, Zarychta, and Zelek, we arranged a campfire for ourselves in the ravine with scrambled eggs and melon.

Today in the order there was a list of those who were designated for the Sahara. I am among them, as is nearly our whole room.

July 29, 1941, Tuesday

Today, vaccination against typhus, and right on the first day I got properly ill, so much that at midday I couldn't even go out for a natural need, but by evening I felt much better and slept well during the night.

July 30, 1941, Wednesday

We are resting after the vaccination and they are feeding us bouillon and coffee. The London radio news reports on the signing of an agreement between Poland and Russia, under which Russia recognizes Poland's eastern borders of 1939, that a Polish army will be formed in Russia, and that an exchange of ambassadors will follow immediately after the agreement is signed. This Sikorski really does have a head for politics.

June 31, 1941, Thursday

The third day after the vaccination I am completely well, and to the meatless dinner they added our normal portion of bread. Lieutenant Raczyński told us that a request had come in for craftsmen for the mine near Colomb-Béchar, where the pay is 50 to 70 francs a day. Apparently Major Fuzeau wrote to Oran that it would be better to send the Poles there.

August 1, 1941, Friday

The widow of a legionnaire who fell at the front, for whose soul the Mass was held last Monday, gave the choir her thanks and 200 francs.

August 2, 1941, Saturday

Today a French colonel who during the war had been attached to the staff of the 2nd Polish Division carried out an inspection of the garrison. He also went around our living quarters and asked the garrison commander what we do.

August 3, 1941, Sunday

Szpetnar continues to lead the choir, which as usual sang in church. After dinner I was at the cinema with Kosiński and Zarychta. The film was nice. In the evening our men played a football match against the Legionnaires and lost 4–1 because they underestimated them. They fielded a lineup of men who mostly barely play football at all.

August 4, 1941:

Today at noon several ringleaders got drunk and called a company assembly. Some men went of their own will, some out of fear, and still others didn't go at all — I didn't go either, nor did anyone from my room; Zelek and Kowacz didn't go either, and there were even cowards who were afraid to go one way or the other and hid in the latrine for the duration. The assembly was called in order to force Lieutenant Piątkowski to release from arrest Grajnert, who had been punished for trying to undermine the company commander's authority and for demanding that the lieutenant be replaced in command. The lieutenant did not come to the assembly but ordered an assembly at 3 o'clock instead. I was present at this official assembly too. At the assembly those ringleaders who had shouted the loudest hung back at the rear. Only after some thought did Kosarzewski speak up, demanding the above-mentioned matters, but the lieutenant categorically refused both points. After Kosarzewski a few more fools blurted something out, like Flond, Pieńkowski, and Kula, but to no effect. Kosarzewski bases his stand on the fact that the lieutenant gives overly harsh punishments, as a result of which Kozakiewicz, punished with 15 days of arrest for the same thing as Grajnert, fell ill and went to the hospital.

August 5, 1941, Tuesday

At the morning assembly Lieutenant Piątkowski announced that an order had come recalling the departure for the Sahara for now, adding that this was not the doing of the rabble, such as the Flonds, Kozakiewiczes, and others, but of someone else. A few days ago the ringleaders made a list of the company where it was written that those listed below did not agree to the trip to the Sahara, for which they were ready to bear all consequences. Many, however, did not sign this list — for instance, our entire room, with the apparent exception of Skrzypek. That is why the lieutenant added the remark about the rabble.

August 6, 1941, Wednesday

By today's order the company commander deducted pay for the underwear from those who had sold theirs — and there is a large percentage of them — and suspended the Red Cross relief payments for those he considered to be perpetrators of Monday's incident.

August 7, 1941, Thursday
No changes.

August 8, 1941, Friday

The garrison orchestra, half of whose musicians are Poles, gave its first concert today. The concert turned out well. Major Fuzeau was present at the concert along with several French officers with their wives, and Polish officers. It took place on the barracks square next to the Foyer.

August 9, 1941, Saturday

Today, vaccination, the second dose, but the choir has its postponed to Monday. In the afternoon an Italian-German commission unexpectedly showed up, so those who had not had their injections had to leave the barracks for a certain period of time, and by the back entrance at that, so the commission wouldn't see it, while those vaccinated had to lie in their bunks, and no one was allowed to be seen on the barracks square.

August 10, 1941, Sunday

Early in the morning, all the healthy ones were ordered to clear out of the barracks and the town. The choir did not sing either, because the Italian-German commission is visiting Saïda. In the afternoon the commission left and it was allowed to go into town. There an entertainment group is performing, with a carousel, swings, a shooting gallery, and lotteries. We spent the whole afternoon watching these amusements.

August 11, 1941, Monday

The choir was supposed to get its injections today but they were postponed until tomorrow.

August 12, 1941, Tuesday

Today I received the second injection. I felt fine all day, only in the evening and at night I had a slight fever. They are feeding us milk — for dinner and supper together they issued 2 liters of milk.

August 13, 1941, Wednesday

We still have leave from duty after the vaccination, but in the morning the choir has a rehearsal.

August 14, 1941, Thursday
Like yesterday.

August 15, 1941, Friday — Polish Soldier's Day
We begin with a service in the parish church and then a morning celebration in the dining hall decorated with Polish eagles and flags.
The morning celebration opened with an occasional address by Lieutenant Piątkowski, who ended it with the words of the "Rota," which the company picked up, and 3 verses were sung; then a short skit involving a card game, and the choir sang "Rozwiń swe skrzydła białe." Still before the end of the celebration the choir went for a second breakfast at the Jewish café, specially ordered with money received for singing at the requiem Mass for the NCO "legionnaire." Then the choir sang at the French High Mass. In the evening a tombola was held at the municipal theater, during which the garrison orchestra performed. I went to listen. A good deal of wine was added to dinner and supper, as a result of which several men got drunk and got into fights.

August 16, 1941, Saturday
One of our afflictions is a plague of bedbugs; plenty of these creatures are found in the walls, shelves, and beds. The nights spent in their company are the worst. Many emigrate at night to sleep on the barracks square or in the dining hall, where there are fewer of them. Some are so oversensitive on the subject of bedbugs that nothing else can be discussed with them, and they kill them all day long, while at night they turn on the light every 5 minutes and hunt bedbugs. I have found it is better to pay them as little attention as possible — at least then one's nerves stay calm.

August 17, 1941, Sunday
Last night a troop transport passed through Saïda, which was received by the local garrison with a supper and orchestra. Because of this, there was no bread for today's breakfast. A very sultry day, the early hour, and the lack of breakfast all contributed to the choir singing poorly at Mass. In the evening, at the municipal theater, the conclusion of Friday's tombola, and late in the evening again a troop transport passed through, received as yesterday. For several days now shirts have been disappearing in the company, and today one of our fellows recognized his shirt; pressed with questions, the thief gave up who he'd bought it from, and so one was uncovered, and we expect there will be more.

August 18, 1941, Monday
Today another shirt thief was tracked down and given a beating, and the company commander put him under arrest.

August 19, 1941, Tuesday
Rumors are circulating again that the departure for the Sahara is once more current, and that the workshop men are to leave by September 1.
