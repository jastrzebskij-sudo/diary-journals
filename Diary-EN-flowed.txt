Stefan Pisulewski A Record of My Fate from September 1939 to December 1942 Copied out from two notebooks of the diary kept by
Stefan Pisulewski Stefan's Diary I begin this diary with a summary of past events, starting from the moment war broke out, that is, from September 1, 1939. On that historic day, being at Fort Wola for supplies, I was bombed; several bombs fell very close by. After the air raid, which took place around 3 p.m., many people still did not believe it was war. The next few days passed under the threat of continual bombardment.

On September 6 I left Warsaw, evacuating the remaining equipment of the Supply Company of the 3rd Armored Battalion. The column, under the command of Lieutenant Jaroński, waited for the rest of the Battalion at Saska Kępa. Around 11 o'clock the rest arrived and we set off for Lublin via Otwock. We drove without lights; fortunately the night was moonlit. The column drove in complete order along the Vistula embankment, but once it reached the Warsaw–Lublin highway, where there was enormous congestion, the column fell apart. Near Garwolin I met several vehicles from our column, but none of our officers were among them. We stood for an hour, then drove on; past Ryki we were attacked by German aircraft and took cover in the forest. After the planes flew off, a few more vehicles joined us. Among those who arrived was Sergeant Wrona, head of the garage on Górczewska Street. Around 1 p.m. we set off again, one by one so as to be safer from air raids, with the assembly point designated at the barracks of the Armored Battalion in Lublin.

I arrived first; after some time a few more vehicles joined. By 8 p.m. none of the officers had arrived, and we had no fuel. After a conference we drove onto the highway toward Chełm. We spent the night on the highway. Toward morning the Park Column of the 2nd Battalion passed by, under the command of Captain Ciągliński, and they gave us fuel; I joined this column because I saw order here. Traffic on the highway was so heavy it was hard to drive in a column. In Chełm we learned that a motor column was standing in the forest beyond the town, so we drove to join it. Just as we reached it, German planes swept in and bombed the railway stations where our column had stood five minutes earlier. We drove on toward Łuck; nothing important happened along the way. In Łuck the column took different streets, and the rest of us, through one driver's carelessness, went in a different direction. We lost a couple of hours finding the column. We found it in the forest near Ulana. Łuck had already been bombed. The infantry barracks were completely destroyed, and so were the Armored Corps barracks. The entire Armored Battalion left Łuck for the forest and joined our column, forming an Armored Corps center. We sat in the forest for a few days; no one was allowed to drive vehicles or walk along the firebreaks. Only walking through the forest itself was allowed. No one was permitted to go far from the forest. After great effort I nevertheless obtained permission to go into Łuck for supplies. In town you could not get bread. You could get sausage, but you had to search for hours, because whatever the butcher prepared was snapped up at once, and then you had to wait for a new batch.

One evening we received orders to move out. The night was dark — you couldn't see three steps ahead — and no lights were allowed. As we pulled onto the highway, my jeep's engine stalled and the whole column drove off without me. I struggled with the vehicle alone, since no one would help me; luckily I had bought a pocket flashlight in Łuck the day before. At that point several local residents came along and pushed me, and the engine started. I caught up with the column and we drove along country roads, through ditches and sand, without lights. That was one of the worst nights. We drove through Brody to Podkamień.

At Podkamień we stood in the forest for one day, and with nightfall we drove on through Tarnopol, Trembowla, Kopyczyńce. We stopped in the forest near Czortków. Around eight o'clock planes flew over, but from the east, which greatly puzzled me, and they circled over the forest for a long time. Two hours later one of the officers rushed in and ordered engines started; in great haste we abandoned the camp and drove by side roads to Czortków. It was Sunday and there was unusual activity in town. A few kilometers past Czortków we met a column of Soviet tanks. We passed them. They behaved very politely; we offered them cigarettes and they said they were coming to help us, that they were coming to beat the Germans. We reached Horodenka, where we witnessed a bombing.

From there to quarters at Serafińce. We spent the night in Serafińce and I repaired the clutch. In the morning I went out to gather news, and it turned out the column had crossed the Romanian border during the night. I met Major Segda, who had also stayed behind at the quarters, and the column had left without waking him.

On returning to the vehicles I found a second lieutenant of the Air Force waiting for me, as the senior man of this small group of vehicles, who proposed forming a sabotage unit. Of course I agreed very willingly. The second lieutenant told us to drive to the border crossing point to draw fuel, while he himself went to look for more. At the border point there were no longer any vehicles with fuel, and the second lieutenant, after talking with Major Segda, gave up on the sabotage unit idea. We stood at the border in a field the whole day, not knowing what to do with ourselves. The odometer on my vehicle read 13,000.

It is Irena's name day, the name day of my last sweetheart from Warsaw. Before evening the decision was made to cross the border. I removed the eagle insignia from my shoulder straps and cap, put on a beret instead, and off we went. Crossing the border I felt so pained that I could not hold back tears; I glanced sideways at my traveling companion, whose surname I don't remember, and saw that he too was crying, so I could no longer restrain myself and wept aloud. After several hours of driving we found ourselves in Czerniowce. We arrived in the evening and spent the night on the main street. In the morning the nearby residents were already giving us breakfast; my companion in misfortune received 25 kilograms of grapes from some gentleman. We had food beyond counting, and everyone walked around pleased at how warmly we were received. Mr. Podsudek, a Pole who owned a bacon factory, made the greatest effort on our behalf. Around noon the police drove us out beyond the city so as not to obstruct traffic, which we managed only with great difficulty. We stood outside the city on the highway for 2 days and 2 nights; everyone sold whatever they could, and I did the same, earning 2,000 lei that way. We could not travel further because we lacked fuel. The Romanians would not give us any and instead ordered us to hand over our vehicles at the Makabi sports field. We did not want to do this, but I received an order from Major Segda and drove my van to the sports stadium. The Romanians loaded all the drivers onto trucks and drove them off to a camp; I had already decided to go along and put my knapsack onto the truck. The previous night someone had stolen my suitcase containing a large number of photographs. Just before departure a young, elegant gentleman came up to me and proposed that I go with him to Bucharest as his driver, because he had a car which up to then had been driven by his friend, whom the Romanians had arrested. I agreed very willingly, took my suitcase, and got into the cab that was already waiting for us. On the way this gentleman introduced himself as Zygmunt Krosnowski. I showed him my identification from the Castle Company and the driving license I had received in the 3rd Armored Battalion.

The car was a CHEVROLET limousine. I felt a bit awkward driving it at first, but after a while I got the hang of it. Mr. Krosnowski introduced me to his brother-in-law, Deputy Minister Tyszka, who within an hour arranged a photograph and passport for me. People sometimes waited days for a passport.

From Czerniowce we set out with two cars; in the first rode Mr. Tyszka and Mr. Krosnowski, with an engineer driving his own car whose surname I don't remember, and in the second I drove Mrs. Tyszkowa with her son. We reached Bucharest safely, where we stopped at the Hotel Nissa on Boulevard Elissabeta. The first night I slept in the hotel, and for the rest of my stay I slept in the car, which at night I would drive out and park by the Polish Consulate on Strada Polonia. My time in Bucharest was spent constantly driving around the city on its narrow, one-way streets. I ate supper and breakfast at the eatery by the consulate, since, as I mentioned above, that is where I spent my nights. There too I had many acquaintances. One evening we even celebrated a wedding. The bride and groom had met on the road while fleeing Poland, and since the young lady had no one to look after her, they married.

I ate lunch at an eatery on the other side of the Hotel Nissa. There I again had a friend, a 15-year-old Romanian, with whom I communicated by gestures and who would not let anyone cheat me. In return he received a couple of silver Polish coins as a keepsake.

I also had another friend, a Warsaw taxi driver, with whom I nearly always ate lunch, and once I went with him to see some girls. He took me to a brothel, very elegantly furnished. The women, dressed in transparent garments through which the whole shape of their bodies could be seen, sat in a glassed-in room, and if a client picked one out he would call her over and go with her to a nicely furnished little room. My chosen one was Hungarian, and I communicated with her by gestures.

After two weeks Mr. Tyszko sent his wife to Hungary and sold the car, giving me 2,000 lei from the sale. I went to the embassy and enrolled in the Polish Army in France, receiving 5,000 lei for the journey and a French visa. Mr. Krosnowski arranged the Yugoslav visa for me, and an Italian one was not needed since it was a country of transit for tourists. At the Consulate I met General Schally, Captain Kryński, Lieutenant Gulin, Mr. Baranowski, and Sergeant Kurnik. I drove Lieutenant Gulin and Mr. Baranowski from the Embassy to the hotel where they were staying with General Schally. Through Lieutenant Gulin's intervention I received 200 złoty from the general, which I later exchanged in France for francs, receiving 1,000 francs.

I left Bucharest in the evening hours, and by around 8 o'clock in the morning I was at the Yugoslav border. In Yugoslavia (in Subotica) ladies of the Red Cross gave us lunch and asked that anyone who wished write a letter to family in Poland. I wrote that I was alive and nothing more. In the evening we were in Zagreb, where we were welcomed by representatives of the Polish consulate and ladies of the Red Cross; from these ladies I received a sweater. In the crush that filled the train car, my wallet was stolen, containing 100 francs and 100 złoty.

At dawn the next day I was in Trieste. The rail line runs above the sea along a high cliff. It was the first time I had seen the sea and I was enchanted by the sight of it. By midday we were in Venice. Then through Milan and Turin to Modane, the first railway station in France. In Modane I met Mr. Krosnowski, who had left Bucharest a day before me.

In the afternoon hours we left Modane for Paris. We arrived in Paris in the morning hours. We took the metro to the other station, where we left our suitcases, and set off to see Paris. I visited the Church of the Invalides; Napoleon's tomb, like other things, was covered with sandbags for protection against bombs. I stood beneath the Eiffel Tower — one was not allowed up the tower because of the war. I saw Napoleon's Arc de Triomphe, beneath which lies the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier. The Champs-Élysées are lovely.
I left Paris around 2 in the afternoon for Rennes and from there to Guer. And there a sergeant of the Polish Army already drove us to Coëtquidan. I was temporarily quartered in barrack B 15, from where I was assigned to the billeting platoon. After a two-week stay I was appointed by Lieutenant Colonel Hofbauer as commandant of transit barrack B 15. There I took Bombardier Józef Walczak, a very honest man, as my assistant.

From the Castle unit I am the first, with number 1262. I borrowed a passport from a gentleman I did not know at all, named Mr. Sumyk, and exchanged cash. After some time the transit quarters were moved to building C 1, where I am commandant of half the building. The next to arrive from the Castle unit were Władysław Gołębiowski and Dominik Szpak. After their discharge they had worked at the P.Z.L. plant in Mielec. I took them on as assistants and they were with me for about 3 weeks, but they were not suited to such duties and I sent them off to the 4th Infantry Regiment. Next to arrive were Senior Sergeant Leopold Kurnik from the Castle column, Senior Wachmistrz Józef Zarychta with the gendarmerie, and Corporal Pawłowski and Corporal Matyjasik, both from the gendarmerie. A few days later several riflemen arrived from the motor column, and Corporal of police, formerly a rifleman of the Castle Company, Dziadek. One day Lieutenant Włodzimierz Gulin arrived and stayed temporarily in my quarters. About 3 weeks before Lieutenant Gulin, Mr. Baranowski had arrived and is in the 7th Officer Cadet Company.

The officer cadet companies moved to quarters at Guer, and the 11th Company to Comblessac. I tried to get a transfer to the Armored Corps or to one of the infantry regiments, but Lieutenant Colonel Hofbauer would not allow it. The Armored Battalion is commanded by Captain Ropelewski, former commander of the President's Motor Column. Lieutenant Jaroński is also there. Around December 10, 1939, a transport of 500 men arrived from Romania and Hungary. Among them are Warrant Officers Zygmunt Czajkowski and Sępkowski. On the next transport Corporal Świtajewski arrived from the Castle Column.

From the last class year of the Castle Company only Senior Rifleman Czesław Grześkowiak arrived, and he went off to the 4th Infantry Regiment.

The assembly station grew by a few more barracks and the staff also grew, to the point of excess, so that I now do virtually nothing.

For a time Lieutenant Colonel Hofbauer was replaced by Cadet Officer Strutyński, later by Cavalry Captain Rudnicki, who fell ill from the work, and in his place came Cavalry Captain Kalinowski. Matters concerning non-commissioned officers are handled by Second Lieutenant Łowczowski and Warrant Officer Czapiński. One of the assistants is Corporal Raczkowski. The head of the Station's Supply Section is Sergeant Leon Winogrodzki.

With this I end the summary and begin writing daily entries.

December 15, 1939. Warrant Officer Czapiński caught the first louse. I enrolled in the drama circle and was given the role of Kacper, one of the Three Kings. Walczak got hold of a bottle of delicious wine from somewhere.

December 16. A transport arrives from Belgium (about 150 men); we welcome them with a band. I receive an invitation to spend the holidays in Lille from a certain Mr. Stanisław Budynek, whom I happened to meet in a café and, while drunk, gave him my address. I don't know what to do.

At the drama circle meeting my role was changed to Balthazar.

I wrote back to Mr. Budynek that I would not be coming for the holidays. We arranged ourselves a magnificent bath in little tubs used for carrying coal. Second Lieutenant Łowczowski lent me 50 francs.

December 17, Sunday

I got up in good spirits, singing. I was at High Mass in church. The band played and the priest gave an uplifting sermon. After the service the band gave a concert on the square. In the afternoon Gołębiowski and Szpak visited me, not counting the regular guests, Corporal Pawłowski and Corporal Matyjasik. There was also another corporal whose surname I don't know, a sailor, a solid fellow. We had food and drink beyond counting. The frost was quite severe and our coal ration was greatly reduced.

December 18, Monday

A transport of about 1000 men arrived. I took charge of 3 barracks in the H.81 area,
79, 77. Besides, my godmother sent me a package containing a sweater, a wool scarf, socks, gloves, chocolate, and cigarettes.

December 19, 1939, Tuesday

I received a package from one of the conscripts, from the Polish Women's Circle, from Mrs. Marja Wojciechowska.

December 20, 21, 22, 1939. Nothing important to note. We go to rehearsals at the cinema. Pawłowski and Matyjasek left for Angers.

December 23, 1939 - Christmas Eve We perform the nativity play (jasełka) at the officers' casino in the presence of President Raczkiewicz, General Sikorski, and members of the government; we gave them a surprise.

Afterward we performed it again in the Administrative Company at the Assembly Station, and then had Christmas Eve supper standing in the recreation-room tearoom.

I have never had a Christmas Eve like this one - there were a few tins of sprats, potatoes, fish, and tea. It was a fine sight to see a colleague, dressed as the King of England, eating fish while holding it in one hand and a piece of bread in the other.

After the meal - for it could hardly be called a proper Christmas Eve supper - we went to the officer cadets' casino and performed the nativity play there. At 9 o'clock I was asleep.

December 24, 1939, Sunday

We are putting on a performance at the cinema at 3:30 p.m., and a second one at 8. Taking part are also French soldiers and artists who came from Paris Radio.

December 25 - I am busy with performances all afternoon; at 10 a.m. a transport arrived from Romania, among whom was a passing acquaintance from Bucharest, I believe a doctor from the State Forests [administration].

December 26 - Second day of the holiday, we are working.

December 27 - Nothing important.

December 28 - ditto.

December 29 - Corporal Walczak returns from leave, and on this occasion there is a feast well-lubricated with alcohol, attended by the sergeant major, Warrant Officer Czapiński, and Sergeant Pieszczuchowicz. Sergeant Winogradzki was removed from his post over some unexplained matter.

December 30 - Warrant Officer Grzyb, Staff Sergeant Aleksander Sagański, Corporal Tadeusz Zegarłowski, and Corporal Miklas arrived at the camp. Sagański and Zegarłowski went with me right away to Comblessac to see Lieutenant Gulin, whom we caught just as he was setting off for Guer; along the way we talked about all our acquaintances. On the way we stocked up on wine, sprats, and sugar. Zegarłowski and Sagański slept at my place. Warrant Officer Grzyb stayed with a colleague, and Miklas in the general hall.

December 31 - Sagański and Zegarłowski receive packages from the Christmas gift committee. In the afternoon we are at a performance in the cinema hall, I am performing. In the evening we pool money for 3 liters of wine. We go to sleep at 9:30.

January 1, 1940 - NEW YEAR After the service we exchange New Year's greetings with acquaintances. For dinner we draw a jug of wine as our ration. Walczak, whom we call "Daddy," brings pastries and tangerines. Mr. Baranowski visits us. I have only 3 centimes in my pocket.

January 2, 1940 - Corporal Zegarłowski went to Comblessac twice to see Lieutenant Gulin, once alone and once with me, regarding confirmation of his officer-candidate qualification (cenzus). The lieutenant confirmed it and gave Zegarłowski 100 francs. A transport arrived which included former Senior Rifleman Struzik, career Corporal Sieradzki of the castle gendarmerie platoon, Corporal Wojtalik, and Corporal Masłowski.

January 3, 1940 - I reported requesting a transfer to an infantry regiment. The colonel did not agree. Sagański was assigned to the Armored Battalion. Raczkowski to the Heavy Artillery.

January 4, 1940, Thursday

Zegarłowski is assigned to the 2nd Infantry Regiment. So are Struzik and Miklas. Rumors are going around that the Assembly Station is going to be disbanded. Sergeant Winogrodzki was transferred to the 2nd Infantry Regiment, and Staff Sergeant Walter to the 3rd. We get a new roommate in the person of Sergeant Stanisław Basta.

January 5, 1940, Friday

In the evening we throw ourselves a little drinking party. The guests are Corporal Kowalski and Mr. Iwański, commonly known as "Spirytusik" (Little Spirit). Vodka is not allowed to be sold, but nevertheless Spirytusik managed to get hold of 2 bottles of cognac at 10:30 p.m. Sagański spent the night at my place.

January 6, 1940, Saturday - Epiphany (Three Kings' Day)

I hand over barrack D 81, 79, 78 to the Command Company of the Training Regiment. Szczupakowski left for the Motorized Cavalry. I received a postcard from Mr. Stanisław Budynek with New Year's greetings and an invitation for leave.

January 7, 1940, Sunday

We set off with the nativity play to the villages where units are billeted. In one locality, whose name I don't remember, we performed the nativity play against the backdrop of a small manor house. The manor, once beautiful, is now abandoned; two women live in it, one of whose husband is at the front holding the rank of sergeant. After the nativity play Mr. Bożenko performed a piece called "Strąś," and Spirytusik sang the "Koczkodański" couplets. Aleksander played the harmonica and the choir sang two songs, titled "When Autumn, Winter, Spring Pass" and "March to Poland" (music by Mr. Tychowski).

Then we went to Guer, but we performed nothing there because they had not been informed of our arrival. (In general, Senator Rembieliński is a disaster.)

From Guer we went to Comblessac and performed the same thing there at the school of the 11th Company. The unit billeted there received us very warmly. After the performance there was a reception at a café with wine and vodka, chocolate, cheese, bread, and pastries. Aleksander played the harmonica and a tenor from Polish radio sang beautifully. We returned singing the whole way; only in Coetquidan did the senator forbid singing, which drew the indignation of the whole unit upon him. We are hungry, and if not for Walczak, who always thinks of us, this time too we would have gone to bed hungry.
January 8, 1940, Monday It was announced in the order that tomorrow's exercises, from 7 to 7:45 a.m., will be led by Corporal Maciołek.

January 9, 1940, Tuesday

The announced exercises went generally well. Rifleman Filipczak or Filipowicz of the Administrative Company returns from leave and said that he lives not far from Miss Irena Ciemniak, and that she sends me her regards. Two gendarmes arrive from the Castle - Senior Warrant Officer Kołodziejczyk. In the evening we have a few drinks; Corporal Orłowski treats us to cognac since he's back from leave.

January 10, 1940, Wednesday

I receive a letter from Senior Warrant Officer Zarychta along with 30 francs, 20 for me and 10 for Staff Sergeant Kurnik. In the evening I have a lot of work with the storeroom.

January 11, 1940, Thursday

Nothing important to note.

January 12, 1940, Friday

I receive a postcard from Senior Warrant Officer Zarychta and a rather warm letter from Miss Irena Ciemniak. Lieutenant Colonel Hoffbauer promised me he would send me back to a regiment, but I don't know which one.

From Mr. Bożenko I receive 3 photographs from the nativity play, and in the camp newsletter there is a description and photograph of the nativity play performed at the officers' casino before President Raczkiewicz.

January 13, 1940, Saturday

Corporal Kanigowski arrives by car from Paris; I ran into him by chance in front of camp headquarters. We went to see Warrant Officer Sepkowski and agreed that we would go to Campaniac. He spent the night at my place, on the table, on which we laid two straw mattresses. In the evening the "castle men" gathered.

January 14, 1940, Sunday

We drive to Campaniac with Kanigowski, Warrant Officer Sepkowski, one sergeant, and one civilian. We stopped at Staff Sergeant Kurnik's, who is the chief of the quartermaster company. Whole companies came for the service, so did everyone from the castle column. Kanigowski went to fetch Sagański. And just before departure, Świtajewski showed up.

We were at a café for cognac, then continued it at the billet. We return to Coetquidan by 12 o'clock. I am completely drunk.

The Coetquidan orchestra gave a concert in front of headquarters. Right after the concert I say goodbye to Kanigowski, who leaves for Paris, while I go off to perform the nativity play. We are missing Tychowski, Iwański, and Raczkowski.

We drive by truck to Augan, where the artillery is stationed, to pick up Raczkowski. There we perform one show without him. We perform on the school square. Then we drive to Campaniac. At the entrance to the town we come across a plane crash. The pilot lies dead on the road, and the aircraft is leaning against and smashed on the stone parapet of a bridge. The plane's tail lies in a meadow 200 meters from the plane. It is a French training aircraft. Eyewitnesses say that another plane clipped its tail with its landing gear in the air and cut it off completely. The whole town's mood is spoiled by this accident. Even so, there were a lot of people. We performed the nativity play in a field and changed clothes in a roadside café (restaurant).

Afterward we were invited to the officers' casino, where we ate and drank well. Major Żyrkiewicz, whom I already know from near Łuck, a very nice man, said that if not for the plane accident, people had already been assigned with drums to go around calling everyone to the performance.

Then we went to a place whose name I don't remember, but which we nicknamed "Pink-Ponk." The motorized cavalry is billeted there, and Janta-Połczyński, the author of the nativity play, is an officer there. We performed the nativity play against the backdrop of the walls of an old church, by torchlight; it turned out very well. After the performance we were taken to the recreation room for a reception. We drank only tea. We return to Coetquidan at 8:30 p.m.

Photographs from the nativity play in Coetquidan, photo by Bożenko January 15, 1940 - I make efforts to be released from the Assembly Station, but without success. In the evening Corporal Miller spreads rumors that the Germans have attacked Holland. Later it turned out they had only concentrated troops there, some 15 divisions strong.

January 16, 1940, Tuesday

The first light snow fell during the night. A few degrees [of frost], and in the evening a proper blizzard. Kanigowski arrived, ate dinner at my place, and afterward we went over to the canteen where Kanigowski treated us to beer, then he left for Paris. In the morning I went to barrack H65, where the Armored Forces assembly station is, so that an Armored Forces officer would agree to accept me into the Armored Forces. Unfortunately Lieutenant Czerniewski had left for Rennes. Nevertheless Warrant Officer Czapiński, with Lieutenant Colonel Hoffbauer's consent, sent in a request for my transfer to be included in the order. In the afternoon French artillery was firing on the range. I went to see, but it was too late; instead I saw old-type French tanks.

January 17, 1940, Wednesday

Snow and frost down to 7 degrees, and on top of that a temporary lack of coal. I was transferred by order of Camp Command to the Armored Forces Battalion. Order No. 16 of January 17, 1940. I seem to have luck with this number, since my arrival at the camp and confirmation of rank were also in Order No. 16, of October 10, 1939. Tomorrow I am to hand over the storeroom to Corporal Walczak, with Warrant Officer Kapusta doing the accounting.

All day I am occupied with the stamping of blankets by the French. The padlocks that I had bought at one point, and for which Second Lieutenant Plewnia had reimbursed me, I handed over - 9 of them.

January 18, 1940, Thursday

In the morning I go to report to the Armored Forces Assembly Station in barrack H65, but after waiting two hours I did not find the officer in. I return to the Assembly Station and hand over the storeroom to Corporal Walczak, with Warrant Officer Kapusta doing the accounting. The warrant officer fussed over the details, on account of which I would have missed the truck I was supposed to leave on for Campaniac, so I asked Lieutenant Colonel Hoffbauer to speed up the matter, which helped.

In the afternoon I go looking for the truck, but without success. In the evening I hold a farewell party with money we collected together.

January 19, 1940, Friday

I go to barrack H65, where the drivers sleep, and go with them to get the truck. After loading the provisions we drive off to Campaniac via Guer. After reporting to headquarters I was assigned to the Quartermaster Company. I was given quarters where 12 comrades live. Conditions here are much worse than in Coetquidan, since we sleep on straw in an enormous hall with a medieval fireplace. Coffee is given out twice a day, and once a day, at dinner, they give meat, soup, and potatoes or pasta.

The company sergeant major is Staff Sergeant Kurnik.

January 20, 1940, Saturday

In the morning I was in the forest gathering firewood. In the afternoon, chopping wood.

January 21, 1940, Sunday

Mass at 9 o'clock, celebrated by a Polish priest; we spend the rest of the day playing cards.

January 22, 1940, Monday
January 23, 1940, Tuesday
Time passes playing cards and eating. We play cards in such a way that the winner pays nothing, and the losers each pay 25 centimes for light (candles).

January 24, 1940, Wednesday

We go to the training ground for wood, but because of firing we were not let in, so we gathered wood in the farmers' fields, where there was plenty. I write a letter to Irenka. My cut finger is bothering me.

January 25, 1940, Thursday
Same as the previous day - going for wood.

January 26, 1940, Friday
After the flags are raised, an hour of exercises.

January 27, 1940, Saturday. Nothing important.

January 28, 1940, Sunday
A trip to Coetquidan. On the way, at the training ground, I find beautiful shells, which I take as a souvenir. Walczak had my hair cut at his own expense and gave me a straw mattress. Miller told me that Mr. Filipiak had been here and said he has a package and a letter from Irena C. for me. There are rumors that we're leaving on Thursday.

January 29, 1940, Monday
Nothing important, in good spirits.

January 30, 1940, Tuesday
In the evening Corporal Francuz takes me out for hot wine.

January 31, 1940, Wednesday
Preparations for departure.

February 1, 1940 At 5 o'clock Corporal Biliński woke me up, saying I was to go with him and Mr. Wolski to the station at Augan with the provisions. Before departure I receive 21 francs in pay. At 7:30 we are at the station in Augan. We find the station deserted, because the stationmaster - the one and only man who handles everything connected with running the station - had gone to a café. We finally find him. Units gradually stream in, and at 2:45 p.m. the transport departs via Guer and Rennes, Tours, Bourges, Lyon, to Orange station. The journey lasted 38 hours.

February 3, 1940, Saturday

We unload at the station - new assignments. I am assigned to the Armored Forces Training Center. The commander here is Lieutenant Józef Jaroński, the sergeant major is Staff Sergeant Aleksander Sagański. Corporal Albert Świtajewski is also here.

We are billeted in the locality of Serignan. The quarters themselves are just outside the town, in coach-houses and stables, but they look reasonably decent.

The area is very beautiful, surrounded on all sides by mountains, and one very high peak is even still covered in snow, although no snow is to be seen anywhere else in the area, and the midday temperature reaches 20 degrees. The fields here are like gravel pits, so many stones lie on them, and everywhere one sees nothing but vineyards. Right after arriving on the spot I am appointed uniform NCO (in charge of clothing supplies).

February 4, 1940, Saturday

I hand over the storeroom, which holds 250 blankets, to Sergeant Kasprowicz, and I become the office NCO. The sergeant major treats us to dinner at a restaurant, since the provisions were not delivered.

February 5, 1940, Sunday

The provisions were still not brought to us and we have nothing to eat. Świtajewski buys bacon, bread, sugar, and tea, for which my kettle bought in Coetquidan and a frying pan came in handy. In the afternoon I go with Corporal Francuz and Rifleman Witkowski, who holds the cenzus (officer-candidate qualification), on a trip into the mountains. The view was wonderful. That high peak in particular looked beautiful.

February 6, 1940, Tuesday

The time goes on making lists. In the evening I go out for a walk with Corporal Francuz; during the walk we meet Engineer Witkowski and Mr. Gilewski. In the course of the conversation Mr. Witkowski says that he had just gone to look for a little cellar which we had found on Sunday's walk in the mountains. I made a bet with him of 1 liter of mulled wine that I would find it despite the darkness. We set off to search for the cellar, located 2 km away in the mountains and woods. I found it, winning the bet. But there was no money for wine, since the engineer only had 1 franc 50 centimes. On the way back we sat down in front of a café where the sergeant major, Sagański, was sitting and drinking.

After a while he came out and asked what we were doing there. And when he learned about the bet, he borrowed 5 francs from a corporal and lent it to me. Five of us went off for wine, which was very good.

February 7, 1940, Wednesday - Ash Wednesday

In the morning the company went to church.

In the evening, by order from the battalion, I was transferred to the 2nd Battalion, 1st Company.

February 8, 1940, Thursday

The sergeant major spoke with the lieutenant about keeping me at the Training Center, but nothing could be done; after dinner we set off for Sainte-Cécile, to km 2131. I get a good billet; there are 8 of us NCOs sleeping there.

February 9, 1940, Friday

In the morning, a talk on terrain reconnaissance; in the afternoon, exercises; in the evening I go to collect back pay - I get 175 centimes for 1 day, but I had no change to give, so I received 2 francs. In the evening I go to bed early.

February 10, 1940, Saturday

Terrain reconnaissance, and in the afternoon free time for laundry and tidying up. For the first time in France I take up laundry, consisting of 2 towels, 1 handkerchief, 2 pairs of socks. Up until now it always somehow worked out that someone washed for me, since I had a few francs; now there's a crisis.

February 11, 1940, Sunday

A funeral mass on account of the death of one of the citizens of the town of Sainte-Cécile-les-Vignes, and for this reason we do not sing in church.

In the afternoon I meet Corporal Roman Francuz, who holds the cenzus, and his friend, and we go for wine which costs 2 fr. 50 c. a liter, but it is not allowed to drink it inside the establishment, so we borrow glasses and sit on stones by the shop entrance out on the street, drinking. At 3:30 we part ways for supper, arranging to meet again at 4:15. After meeting up we go for a walk to Rochegude, and on returning, to the café for hot wine with cinnamon and cloves. We recall Tuesday's wine with oranges in Serignan.
Monday, February 12, 1940 Despite the rainy weather, drills; in the afternoon during gymnastics there was a small clash between the 2nd Lt., deputy company commander, and one Cpl. The professional NCOs were paid their first salary. I was registered as a long-service (extended-term) soldier.

Tuesday, February 13, 1940

The weather changed overnight. Strong wind and frost down to 5 degrees. Despite this we set out for drills, but the battalion commander turned us back along the way. I received a letter from Irenka C. For the past few days letters have been checked by the company commanders. The company commanders are cautioning us about writing letters, and they are absolutely right to do so.

Wednesday, February 14, 1940

The weather hasn't changed at all. I'm on patrol duty together with Sgt. Gbur and one Sr. Pvt.

Thursday, February 15, 1940

The weather is unchanged; in the morning I rest after patrol duty and do some laundering of handkerchiefs. In the afternoon, singing lessons.

Friday, February 16, 1940

Laundry: 2 shirts and 2 pairs of long underwear.

Saturday, February 17, 1940

I get a letter from Grześkowiak Czesław; he informs me that he's been promoted to Cpl. I receive a money order for a parcel from Irenka C. I went to Serignan to collect it, but it turned out I had to go to Orange for it, so I turned back, since we're not allowed to go to Orange because of an epidemic of meningitis. I left the money order at the restaurant across from the bus stop so the driver could bring the parcel to me. It should arrive Monday afternoon.

Sunday, February 18, 1940

At Mass in church I met Sgt. Świtajewski, and he told me that Sr. S/Sgt. Sagański is now driving for Col. Dworak. Before noon I iron my laundry.

Monday, February 19, 1940

Nothing important to note.

Tuesday, February 20, 1940

The matter of my verification is finally cleared up, and I draw my pay, totaling 262 francs. While collecting my pay I run into Sr. S/Sgt. Sagański, who gave me back 15 francs for the kettle I had left with him in Serignan. In the evening we celebrate our pay with cognac.

Wednesday, February 21, 1940

I send Lt. Łowczowski back 50 francs, and in the evening I write a letter to Lt. Łowczowski, and to Grześkowiak and Irenka C.

The weather, yesterday and today, is very nice and warm. I lent Cpl. Sułek 30 francs, since he's going on leave and has no money for anything.

Thursday, February 22, 1940

In the evening I went to see Sgt. Świtajewski but didn't find him in, as he had gone on leave. Then I went to the cinema, where they were showing a film with Marta Eggerth — in Poland, I believe, it was shown under the title "The Nightingale of Vienna" — along with a very silly French comedy and a P.A.T. newsreel.

Friday, February 23, 1940

I buy myself a pocket flashlight, paying 18 francs.

Saturday, February 24, 1940

At 1 p.m. we're supposed to leave for Serignan or Orange to bathe, but we're late, and the bus pulls away right from under our noses. We set off on foot, and along the way we try hitchhiking; one car picked up three of the men, who had our laundry with them, while Cpl. Dierzyk and I go on foot. For the first time this year I saw a tree in bloom.

In Serignan I bathed under the shower, while Cpl. Dierzyk went on to Orange, since he didn't want to bathe there; afterward I had to put the same clothes back on, since his and my laundry were with the ones who had gone to Orange.

On the way back, with one of the corporals from my company as my companion, we hitchhike. In the car that stopped for us rode our 2nd Lt., who gave us a lift to Ste Cecile.

I received a letter from Walczak and Miler.

Sunday, February 25, 1940

After the service, at which the parish priest (a Frenchman who doesn't know Polish) intoned "Boże coś Polskę," I lost 6 francs playing cards with Sgt. Gbur. At 2 p.m. we go on a trip into the mountains, with a French corporal named Roman and Cpl. Świątkowski Tadeusz. We walked over three hours one way. In the town of Gigondes we stopped to buy wine. When the restaurant owner learned we were Poles, he wouldn't take payment from us for the 2 liters of wine. On top of the rocks we built a cairn of stones with a note bearing a suitable inscription, tucked inside my old worn coin purse. Then we sang the national anthem and headed back down. Along the way we stopped once more at the little café mentioned above, and ate a supper consisting of an omelet, 2 liters of wine, roasted potatoes, cheese, and stewed pears. In the place there were also 3 officers of the French air force and 6 privates of the same unit. The restaurant owner's daughters, very pretty and pleasant girls, took such good care of us that I count that evening among the nicest of my life. I returned to Ste Cecile at 24:30 (half past midnight).

Monday, February 26, 1940

I am very tired from yesterday's outing, and the toe which I bruised on that outing hurts a great deal and has swollen up. In the evening, night exercises. The men carry them out quite willingly, regarding them more as entertainment.

Tuesday, February 27, 1940

I take up my first duty as company duty NCO in France, after a 5-month break.

Wednesday, February 28, 1940

The duty private overslept and woke me half an hour late, that is, at 7. Even so, the company made it to assembly on time.

Thursday, February 29, 1940

Nothing to note.

Friday, March 1, 1940

During drills I lost my watch, but it was found by one of the privates. We draw our pay, totaling 452 francs; I set aside 20 of it for a shared supper. Everyone billeted together plays a card game called "a thousand."

Saturday, March 2, 1940

I bought myself a pocketknife, paying 10 francs.

Sunday, March 3, 1940

I write back to Grześkowiak and take a little walk toward Tulette.

Monday, March 4, 1940

I received a letter from Lt. Gulin. Irenka hasn't written back for quite a while now, so I intend to write to her.

Tuesday, March 5, 1940 — We get new officers

Wednesday, March 6, 1940 - Nothing important

Thursday, March 7, 1940 — Capt. Kruczyński conducts a records inspection.

Friday, March 8, 1940 - Nothing important.

Saturday, March 9, 1940 - The 1st Battalion went to fetch tanks.

Sunday, March 10, 1940 — I'm on duty.

Monday, March 11, 1940

Tuesday, March 12, 1940 — Nothing important, the equipment hasn't arrived.

Wednesday, March 13, 1940

Thursday, March 14, 1940 — Gen. Maczek arrived at the camp together with some French general. Today the tanks were brought to Cenle.

Friday, March 15, 1940 — Nothing to note

Saturday, March 16, 1940 — We're issued armored equipment and I become a driver. After lunch I go to bathe in Orange.

Sunday, March 17, 1940 — Palm Sunday

A very beautiful service, during which one of the officers played the violin beautifully. After the service, the first briefing with the new company commander. After lunch I'm on duty as commander of the garrison guard. This is the first guard duty on French soil. It was made eventful by the arrest of Cpl. Maczewski Edward for breaking a comrade's leg while drunk. The arrest protected him from being beaten by the comrades who stood up in defense of the injured man. The second man arrested, for drunkenness, was a Pole, a corporal who had come from Romania, but the duty officer had him released.

Monday, March 18, 1940

I rest after finishing my duty. The company returns from tank exercises full of enthusiasm. I learned that my tank has been given to another company.

Tuesday, March 19, 1940

A memorial service for the soul of the late Marshal Piłsudski, and normal duties.

Wednesday, March 20, 1940 — The British bomb Germany.

Thursday, March 21, 1940 — Nothing important.

Friday, March 22, 1940 — Nothing important.

Saturday, March 23, 1940 — Preparations for the święcone [the Easter blessing of food], and before evening the Polish święcone on the assembly square of the 2nd Battalion. In the middle of the square, 5 tables were set up, decorated with the flags of the allied nations and covered with sheets in place of tablecloths. Each company brought its own eggs, cold cuts, and wine — a certain proportion of it, of course. Everything reminded us of the święcone back home.

On one side of the tables the 1st Battalion lined up, and on the other, the 2nd Battalion.

All around us plenty of the local people watched this traditional observance with great curiosity, since they have nothing like it themselves.

The battalion commanders arrive, Maj. Zyrkiewicz and Maj. Harbowski; one of the captains gives the report, there is a greeting, and each major passes along the front of his Battalion.

Meanwhile the chaplain arrived, visiting us for the first time, and gave a moving address. He then blessed the tables and shared an egg with the battalion commanders and the delegates of each company.

After that the companies dispersed, taking their święcone food with them.

Sunday, March 24, 1940 — Easter.

Early in the morning we go to church, passing Frenchmen sweeping the streets along the way, and even one man out on the street weaving chair seats.

The service was conducted by a Polish priest, who gave such a moving sermon that many secretly wiped tears from their eyes. In this sermon the chaplain drew a connection between the Resurrection of Christ and the Resurrection of Poland.

At 10 o'clock, the święcone at the great gateway of billet 23. The company commander, Capt. Kruczyński, was not present, being away on a course. Lt. Skowroński was on leave, and Lt. Osiecki stood in for them.

Breakfast was good and plentiful, beautifully laid out and decorated with flowers, which our French neighbors gladly supplied us.

Everything waits for the Major. At last he arrives, accompanied by his chief of staff, receives the report, and offers us his wishes in these words:

"I will not wish you a Happy Easter, because it cannot be happy so far from our families, who are suffering back there in our distant Homeland — but I do wish you the swift receipt of modern armored equipment, and a return home with it as victors."

Then Maj. Habowski shared an egg with Lt. Osiecki and the delegates of the NCOs and privates, sat at the table for a few minutes, and went on to the other companies.

The various companies sent us their own delegations with good wishes, and we sent them the same. The święcone ended in good spirits, with singing. Everyone had a good drink, while our dear sergeant-major just went around encouraging everyone to sing the song "Czas do domu, Czas" ("Time to Go Home, Time").

After we broke up, the four of us — myself, Janowski, Chaniewski, and Stękowski — set off on a trip into the mountains, to the place I had already been to once, namely Gigadas.

I went up to the summit where I had left the coin purse with the note bearing our names the last time.

After coming down from the mountains we stopped at the little café we knew, and there I met Cpl. the Frenchman, with several of his friends. There were also French airmen there, six of them I believe.

We had a wonderful time, singing the Frenchmen Polish songs and they singing us French ones; we raised toasts to France, and they did the same for us in return.

We parted as warm friends. During the farewell, Sr. Pvt. Strękowski vanished from our sight. We searched for him for about half an hour without success.

So we gave up the further search and set off on the way back. Along the way I began to feel unwell and had to ride to ...... and afterward by bicycle, so as to get home sooner, because I was very weak. The bicycle belonged to the French corporal, but he hadn't wanted to ride it while walking with us, and he handed it to me when I grew weak.

I got back to my billet at 3 o'clock in the morning.

Monday, March 25, 1940

On the second day of the holiday we don't go to church, and I spend the whole day lying in bed — pardon, on the straw — resting. Janowski and I tell each other about how our trip went, and we poke fun at Sgt. Dziadkowiec and Cpl. Dzierzyk, and they at us. Cpl. Dzierzyk and Sgt. Dziadkowiec spent the holiday in Avignon.

Tuesday, March 26, 1940. Exercises with the tank.

Wednesday, March 27, 1940. It's been raining since morning, and lectures all day at billet 46.

Saturday, March 30, 1940. A new transport of troops arrives at Ste Cecile. The 1st and 2nd Battalions are now at full strength. Sgt. Wolski arrived with this transport, 14
Michał, and mjst Haze from the 3rd Battalion.

Sunday, March 31, 1940
Sr. S/Sgt. Kurnik hosts me and Wolski, along with a sergeant, a friend of his. I got thoroughly drunk.

Monday, April 1, 1940
Sgt. Wolski was assigned to the 1st Company of the 2nd Battalion.

Tuesday, April 2, 1940
I receive back pay and current pay, totaling 1337 francs.

Wednesday, April 6, 1940
The Germans have occupied Denmark and attacked Norway.

Thursday, April 11, 1940
First live-fire range in the mountains near Lagarde Paol.

Sunday, April 14, 1940
Yesterday, while bathing in Orange, I bought myself a gold chain and cross, and Cpl. Nocoń a silver one. On Sunday we throw ourselves a little party with wine and cakes, and in the afternoon the opening of the community room — in an old wine press. A very dull program, songs and recitations. In the evening a radio bulletin from Toulouse reports the sinking of 7 German destroyers in the Narvik fjord.

Monday, April 15, 1940

Inspection of the Battalion by the French.

Saturday, April 20, 1940
I'm transferred to billets outside town, popularly called the "folwark" [manor farm]. We live in one small room — a kitchen, really — the four of us, that is, me, Dierzyk, Tracz, and Helik.

Sunday, April 21, 1940
Beautiful weather; in the afternoon we ride bicycles to Gigadas for lunch. There were four of us — me, Świątkowski, Janowski, and his friend Zbyszek. Lunch cost me 56 francs for the four of us. Janowski also talked me into some Kirsch, which came to another 20 francs. On the way back Janowski took a fancy to go to Orange for some k-... and off he went. Świątkowski and I returned to Ste Cecile.

Monday, April 22, 1940
Some major arrived, accompanied by Capt. Berg (who recognized me at once) and another captain, and they were selecting drivers for the motorized cavalry.

Tuesday, April 23, 1940
I'm given tank no. 68086. Driving exams are held, on flat ground and over obstacles.

Wednesday, April 24, 1940
More exams, but this time in the presence of French officers.

May 1, 1940
I try to arrange for leave. Lieutenant Skowroński didn't want to grant leave in the first round, since I hadn't yet finished my tank-driving exams (driving over obstacles). But at my insistent requests, he agreed.

May 2, 1940
Not everyone who was granted leave by the company commander gets to go, because the battalion staff ran short of blank leave cards. I, however, am the lucky one who got one. Besides me, Cpl. Dzierzyk also received one, as did the company sergeant-major, Sr. S/Sgt. Kobylański, and one Sr. Pvt. whose name I don't know.
We take a taxi to Orange, because, owing to Dzierzyk's slow nature — popularly nicknamed "the grump" or "baldy" — we're running late for the bus. We leave Orange on the leave train at 21:45, traveling via Lyon.

May 3, 1940. Dzierzyk didn't want to change trains at Lyon, insisting he'd been told the change was only 100 km before Paris, so we rode on, sleeping, not knowing where we were going. Morning found us at Besançon, by the Swiss border. Using the map I had gotten hold of before setting out, we realized we were going the wrong way, and got off.
We can't make ourselves understood, since neither of us knows French. By gestures we learned that the leave train wouldn't come for another 9 hours, and we were told to wait, and were sent to the canteen.
In the canteen I had the impression I was in Africa, there were so many Senegalese and men of various nationalities and races. We ate breakfast and went out to the station, where we managed to make ourselves understood to a railwayman who knew a bit of Russian — that we wanted to go to Paris — and he pointed us to a train bound for Dijon and told us to change there. That's what we did. But that train wasn't actually permitted for us, since it was an express.
Just before Paris a gendarme came through checking tickets. He took mine and Dzierzyk's away, since we weren't traveling legally, and told us to report to the military office at the station in Paris to reclaim them. There, with the help of our own gendarmes, we explained how it had happened, and they let us go. Then the Polish gendarmes sent us from station to station until we found the right train. Dzierzyk left right away, but I had to wait until the next day, about 12 hours. I ate supper in the canteen and got a good night's sleep in a barrack built specially for that purpose. When the time came for my train to leave, I went to look for a seat in a carriage, when suddenly I heard a voice speaking Polish. It was some friends calling out to me, who had left Orange a couple of hours after me. To mark the occasion we drank 2 liters of wine, and off we went. Further along, 2 more Polish soldiers joined us; one of them knew Cpl. Grześkowiak. At Amiens both of them got off, and the three of us went on to Arras, where they got off too; I rode on alone for a few more stations and got off at Douai.
Here I somehow found my own way to the square that Słomnicki had told me about. Here I barely managed to ask my way to the bus for Pecquencourt. Along the way there were a few funny scenes, brought on by my not knowing French.
I got off the bus and tried to communicate with the French, but without success, so I went into a shop over which hung a sign bearing a Polish surname. And I chose well, for I met 2 Poles there, and they took me to Mr. and Mrs. Ciemniak's. I recognized Irenka at once, since I had seen her in a photograph. They received us very warmly, and we went to sleep, as it was late.

Sunday, May 5, 1940 — first day of leave
At 8 in the morning I go with Irenka to Polish Holy Mass at the parish church. I hadn't eaten breakfast before leaving, since I'd gotten up too late. After Mass, Mr. Pawłowski, whom I didn't yet know at the time, took me along with him to a café, and since I was too embarrassed to admit I still hadn't had breakfast, I had to drink on an empty stomach, since it isn't the custom there to drink with a snack. He then took me to the meeting of the scout troop, of which he was the head. There Mr. Ciemniak came looking for me, indignant that I still hadn't eaten breakfast while they were waiting for me. After the meeting, Mr. Pawłowski took us to his place for breakfast, and there, for the first time in France, I ate Polish sausage.
Finally Ira and Mrs. Ciemniak, unable to wait for me any longer, took the trouble to come and fetch me for breakfast themselves — but since it was already past 1 p.m., it turned into lunch instead. After lunch, back to the tavern again, where I met some Englishmen and struck up an acquaintance with them. I beat them at billiards
about 40 francs and I drank beer in very great quantities, because they [the English] only drink beer and are not allowed to be sold spirits — this is strictly forbidden by the English authorities. While drinking they raise a toast with the Polish words "Na zdrowie" (To your health). We got acquainted very warmly, and they took my Polish formation commemorative badges and gave me an English rifleman's lanyard cord and a cap badge. The English army impressed me greatly with its discipline. Before evening Ira came and wanted to take me to the cinema, but because of the Englishmen I didn't go.

May 6, 1940, Monday
I spend the whole day in Irenka's company, and only went with Mr. Ciemniak to report to the gendarmerie. I am starting to like Ira more and more. The food is purely Polish and plentiful, and on top of that all the household members keep urging me to eat.
In general, from the very first moment I feel as if I were with a very close family.

May 7, 1940, Tuesday
I feel a growing fondness for Irenka, and when Mama moved away, our first kiss took place. In the evening, while at Mrs. Lutomska's, we meet 2 English sergeants and they invite us to a café the following evening.

May 8, 1940, Wednesday
While out for a walk with Irena I met an Englishman driving a car, one with whom I had drunk on Sunday at Mrs. Lutomska's. He invited me to the café for Sunday. It was a very pleasant walk.

May 9, 1940, Thursday
I spend the whole day in Ira's company and it seems to me that I am in love. In the evening I go for another walk.

May 10, 1940, Friday
The Germans attacked Holland, Belgium and Luxembourg.
Before dawn an air-raid alarm woke us. After some time German airplanes flew over, which were greeted by English anti-aircraft artillery and fighter planes. Over the Lemay colony one English fighter fought a battle against 3 German bombers. One German bomber was shot down. Air-raid alarms continued all day, but I am more occupied with love.

May 11, 1940, Saturday
Alarms all day, as on the previous day, and the whole day similar to yesterday's.

May 12, 1940, Sunday
After the service, which I attended with Ira, I went with Mr. Ciemniak around the cafés, and so we visited about 5 Polish cafés. The Englishmen left for Belgium yesterday. In the evening we go for a walk with Ira and her friend. We spent this evening very pleasantly and properly.

May 13, 1940, Monday. End of leave.
From morning I get ready to leave. Ira packed so much food into my suitcase that I was almost ashamed to accept it. Mr. and Mrs. Ciemniak said goodbye to me very warmly, and Ira walked me to the station, about 4 km away. I am very grateful to her for that. In farewell she kissed me very tenderly and I felt that she was very sorry that I was leaving. At the station in Danai, where I changed trains, I met several Polish soldiers and traveled with them past Reims to the central station for men on leave.

May 14, 1940, Tuesday
I stay at this station for 36 hours because of continuous alarms and the track being occupied by troop transports. Finally, before evening, I depart, and by morning I am at the second central leave-station in Bicon.

May 15, 1940, Wednesday
I am now traveling illegally, by regular passenger train, in the direction of Marseille. My travel companion is a corporal of the Czech army. A very warm relationship developed between us.

May 16, 1940, Thursday
The train I was on didn't stop at Orange, and only stopped at Avignon. It was only half an hour later that I had a train back to Orange. I arrived at Ste Cecile before dinner, and here no changes had yet occurred. Cpl. Dzierzyk has not yet returned.

May 17, 1940, Friday
I was at the doctor's after my leave; he didn't examine me, just took my word for it. In the evening we arrange a feast at our quarters consisting of scrambled eggs made from 20 eggs and a tomato salad.

May 18, 1940, Saturday
Cpl. Dzierzyk still hasn't returned and the command has sent out a wanted notice. I am on duty as the company's duty NCO.

May 19, 1940, Sunday
The company received light machine guns and is to maintain anti-landing readiness for the whole week.

May 20, 1940, Monday
Ever worse news from the front. The Germans took the cities of Amiens and Arras. Świątkowski and I cook scrambled eggs from 40 eggs. I sent Świątkowski to the field for chives and he brought me onions, claiming that this was chives, only old. I fried the eggs and they were very tasty. In the evening, after the news bulletin in which there was news of the taking of Arras, I am very despondent and keep thinking about Irenka Ciemniakówna, because the poor girl is surely in the area now occupied by the Germans.

May 21, 1940, Tuesday
Outpost exercises. By the captain's order I am the outpost commander, and the captain observed me because he wanted to get to know me. The exercise turned out well overall.

May 22, 1940, Wednesday
The company digs anti-aircraft trenches.

May 23, 1940, Thursday
The part of the company that had already been vaccinated against typhus during my leave received the second dose. In the evening, news about the halting of the German offensive and the recapture of the city of Arras. Dzierzyk still hasn't returned.

May 24, 1940, Friday
We're loafing about alongside those who were vaccinated.

May 25, 1940, Saturday
I am transferred to the 2nd platoon and in connection with this I change quarters. As a farewell to the old quarters we arrange a little scrambled-egg feast. I make the new quarters nice for myself.

May 26, 1940, Sunday
In the afternoon I learn that 2nd Lt. Micherdziński (Pigułka) is leaving us and that command of the company is being taken over by Capt. Ziemski. I was very pleased about this, because I had heard much about his combat merits, but I feel sorry for the former commander because I had already grown close to him.
As a farewell, our comrade Kanar takes our photograph. Świątkowski made a fool of himself and went to ask if the girls who work at the silk factory right next door would be photographed with us. They did not agree and were even offended, and Dulemba (the cook from France) immediately felt the need to insult them. However, two girls unknown to us passed by and they did get photographed with us.

May 27, 1940, Monday
We prepare to depart. In the afternoon, departure by bus to Orange. On saying goodbye the French were very sad and some Frenchwomen cried. At the train station in Orange the professional soldiers receive an advance of 100 francs. Departure from Orange at about 8 p.m.

May 28, 1940, Tuesday
We travel through Lyon, Bourges, Orléans. At Vierzon station two trains from opposite directions pull up at one platform. One, full of singing, is carrying Polish troops heading to the front, and the other, sad, is carrying evacuees from Belgium. After a moment the singing quiets down, and the Poles, having learned who is on the train standing opposite, carry bread, tripe stew, and condensed milk to the carriages and share what they can with the refugees. The Belgians gladly accept. After some time the troops depart, warmly bid farewell by the evacuees. In Orléans we meet a transport of Polish troops (artillery).

May 29, 1940, Wednesday
We arrive in Versailles at 3 a.m. I stay behind to unload the battalion's food supply depot. Then I set off for the new quarters by military vehicle, but the driver is Polish. On the way I passed by the famous Palace of Versailles. At our quarters we learn sad news from the front about the French retreating and the capitulation of Belgium.

May 30, 1940, Thursday
We settle in at the quarters and draw equipment and uniforms. I am assigned to the platoon of Lt. Osiecki. With me in the second tank is Lt. Helik, and the deputy platoon commander is Officer Cadet Sokół Szachil.

May 31, 1940, Friday
We turn in the equipment issued yesterday. Engineer Kaczkowski and I go for a walk in the evening hours, and along the way we stop for tomato salad. 1 kg of tomatoes costs 10 francs. At another café in lower Chateaufort we meet Sgt. Suma with young Ziemski, my captain's brother, both of them completely drunk. The little walk to the forest outside town was very pleasant.

June 1, 1940, Saturday
We receive our pay. In the evening hours I go with two senior riflemen to bathe in a stream that we had heard flows nearby. After a long search we find it and choose ourselves a suitable spot in some park right in front of a very beautiful palace. In general the area here is very beautiful. At night I go on patrol.

June 2, 1940, Sunday
For the first time in my life I fire a cannon. They are very accurate.
In the afternoon I went out into the town and met Cpl. Helik, and went with him to a café. In the café were Kurcz, Sgt. Suma, Cpl. Leńczyk, Jodłowski, Komar and a few others sitting in the company of French airmen. Drinking began. I lost about 200 francs then. Kurcz started a brawl with some civilian. And Helik apparently shouted "Heil Hitler." I was no longer present for the brawl because I was very drunk and had left earlier.

June 3, 1940, Monday
At the morning report the corporal gave a sermon to the drunkards, because yesterday's brawl had spread widely; apparently there is to be an investigation. In the afternoon the corporal ordered all the professional soldiers to pay for their board two weeks in advance, and I was left penniless. The corporal laughed that now there would be nothing left to drink with.
At noon, a German air raid on Versailles and the airfield next to us.

June 4 to June 8
While waiting for equipment we go to exercises. In the evenings, with the engineer Kaczkowski, we go every day for tomato salad.

June 9, Sunday
The chaplain was late for Mass because he was hearing general confession for the 10th Armored Cavalry Brigade. He was very worked up and delivered a patriotic sermon. In the afternoon I receive my equipment. My driver is Cpl. Widło Roman.

June 10
We familiarize ourselves with the equipment. In the evening I take a bath in the stream. Paris is being evacuated. And the sight of fleeing cars has a negative effect on the army's morale.

June 11. We move with our equipment into the area of our post. The political situation is very serious. Italy has declared war on the Allies.

June 23, 1940, Sunday (date uncertain)
(Location: near Avignon)
I stopped writing because of the fast-moving events, but I will try to summarize them so I can keep writing. I don't remember any dates, so I won't give them. One evening we were alerted and went to sleep by our equipment near Versailles, and the next day we move the equipment to the area of (Chateaufort, phonetically). Here, one day's stop, and we go to load onto a rail transport. By rail we go around Paris, and all the little suburban stations are completely deserted. From Paris we travel the line toward Dijon. A few stations before Dijon we turn east and travel all day. There, the whole transport nearly fell into German hands. Thanks to Capt. Ziemski's cunning, we escape back.
At a certain station not far from Dijon our company (i.e., 1st Company, 2nd Battalion) plunders a freight car of chocolate and pickles. We unload at Dijon. A French general arrived and gave us orders for the defense of that city.
They supplied us with fuel, but the captain got out of it and we drive at night toward Roanne. There we were again put to work to defend the city along a 30-km stretch, 1st Company. We took up positions defending the city. We stood there the whole night and day. During the day Italian planes bombed us.
In the evening the captain gave the order to withdraw, and as it later turned out, without the French's orders. We were to go by platoons to the town of Le Puy. In the town of Toy the French authorities arrested us, saying that Capt. Ziemski was under arrest; Lt. Osiecki was also arrested. Finally an understanding was reached and Lt. Osiecki was released, but we know nothing about the captain. The French ordered us to defend the small town of Ste Just, near Ste Etienne.
Here news reached us that we are handing over the equipment to the French and the Polish army is going to England. Part of the tanks we hand over at that location, and 6 go to Monistrol, and there is a misunderstanding there, because the French captain thought that we didn't want to give up the tanks but were fleeing with them to England.
Finally an understanding was reached and they gave us food. We drive to Le Puy by trucks, the whole unit led by Lt. Stanek. There the French gave us orders to go to Marseille.

Near Avignon our officers went to get their bearings on the situation, and apparently we are to head toward Bordeaux.
That is for now as regards events from the theater of war, but I had one personal incident with Sgt. Gbur which I nevertheless want to remember. While stationed at Roanne we received an order to prepare and service the tanks, because in an hour we were going into action. And the sergeant ordered my crew to bring coffee for himself and for his own crew, of course, and I refused. A row started; the sergeant wanted to put me on report for failure to carry out an order, but seeing that everyone was laughing at him, he gave up.
It is in difficult moments that one truly gets to know people, and so it was again with Sgt. Gbur. Now that we are leaving France today and losing our pay, he says that we should go back to Poland, under Russian partition, because he must think of his wife and old age, and in the army he'll never live to see a pension.
In Avignon we were supplied with fuel and provisions, and from Ste Cecile 3 Polish soldiers joined us, and we travel through Alès, Florac. In the area of Florac we pass through beautiful, high mountains. The journey is like a beautiful sightseeing trip. Then through Millau, Montauban to Toulouse. There, at the Polish consulate, they supply us with fuel and provisions and order us to head toward Foix, Perpignan, to the port of Vendres, right by the Spanish border on the Mediterranean Sea. They are directing us to such a small port because all the ports on the Atlantic are occupied by the Germans. The transport is led by Lt. Stanek; in the company there is great discontent over the long stops and the cooking of meals. Right before Perpignan Cpl. Wołk crashes the tractor into a lorry.

June 25, 1940, Tuesday
We arrive at Port Vendres. We find three Polish soldiers whose ship has already left. They tell us that 5 hours before our arrival an English ship departed which took 1,900 Poles from the 1st Division.

June 25, 1940, Wednesday
While waiting for a ship I tour the nearby surroundings of the port. The port is situated in the mountains, almost natural.
Guarded by coastal artillery. In the port stand 3 French destroyers and 3 French merchant ships.
While touring the beautiful rocky coast I bathe in the sea for the first time. The water is salty and soap won't lather at all, so you can't do laundry.
In the evening a Yugoslav freighter, the Petar, puts into port. The company waits for a ship on the harbor square, joined by stragglers from other Polish units and Polish civilians. 2 of them are even married with their wives. Our officers are paying a lot of attention to one of them, a young and pretty one. About 10 French tank crewmen join our company, wanting to keep fighting, but the French government won't allow them to leave. They are now learning Polish. The thought inevitably comes to mind: "what goes around comes around" (lit. "the scythe's turn has come for Maciek too"). We used to learn French, and now they [learn] Polish.
From the newspapers we learn of the conclusion of peace (a ceasefire) with the Germans, under which France loses ¾ of its territory, including Paris and the entire Atlantic coast.

June 27, Thursday
Rummaging through the cars standing on the harbor square, left behind by French airmen, I found a small bottle of ink, with which I am now beginning to write.
The whole day I lie about and ponder over this war. I have already been through 2 wars, or rather two acts of the European drama, and I haven't fired a single shot, because I haven't even seen a single "boche," as the French call the Germans. But I think that in this third, decisive act, I really must do something. Toward evening we learn that we will still have to wait a few more days for a ship, so I decided to build myself a tent. I found myself a buddy and out of a tent-cloth we made ourselves a tent and enlarged it with tarpaulins found in the aircrews' cars. I fenced the tent with posts and wrapped it with electric cable on the posts, and I fastened flowers to it (cacti, which grow here as commonly as thistles do in Poland).
In the evening the lieutenant gathered the company and put the whole company on anti-aircraft defense duty, because a few fools like Jodłowski, Fatyga, Rydlewski, and Dzięcioł were riding around town on motorcycles like madmen. The French gendarmerie even brought Rydlewski back to the camp. Sergeant Major Kobyliński demanded the confiscation of revolvers, because they had been threatened with being shot at. In general the army is very demoralized.

June 28, 1940, Friday
I got up at 10:30 in the morning; my buddy brought me breakfast in bed. A very strong wind from the sea kept me from shaving.
Right before dinner the officers ordered us to move the tents to another spot and to completely clear that plot. And so again I had work to do for some 3 hours. And afterward, again nothing to do! I lie in the tent and think, and thoughts of Irenka Ciemniakówna come to my mind most of all. I found so much heartfelt warmth in her that I cannot forget.
In the evening I go for a stroll into town, but I got very bored in town, so I went with two Poles into the mountains, because I feel better in the bosom of nature than among people. Returning from the walk I found at our post 2 Polish majors who were urging us to go to a camp where we would be treated as soldiers and not as internees; however, we have no trust in that kind of camp.
But whatever the authorities decide is what we have to do. Some slipped away, but those were Poles from France, whereas I don't know French and have no papers at all, so I'm afraid that it could end even worse for me.

June 29, Saturday
Reveille, by order of Lt. Stanek, is sounded for us at 7 o'clock by the cook, which drew down the wrath of the whole company upon him. At 9 o'clock we loaded onto trucks and drive to a camp 10 km from the port. Before the camp the gendarmerie greeted us with shoving and jostling. The company was outraged; Lt. Stanek also protested and was shoved about too. The lieutenant himself formed us up and led us to the barracks, because the company didn't want to go, and it nearly came to shooting.
General outrage at the lieutenant for having led us here. We look around the barracks — filth, squalor, and fleas. Our area borders the area where Poles are confined, and, as the gendarmes say, communists, and further on are Spaniards and various other nationalities. There are also women, fenced off by a triple row of wire, much denser, so that the men cannot get to them. There are women there, even pretty young girls, and children. After getting my bearings in the terrain I go back to our area and found a more decent barrack — it at least has a floor, because in the others it's sand, and at the slightest movement it kicks up dust so that the barrack goes dark. The whole camp is situated on the beach, and at midday it's hard to bear here — it's like the Sahara.
Before evening I go with Rydlewski to bathe in the sea. We were called up from the seashore to a formation at which the lieutenant announced to us that the colonel of the gendarmerie apologizes to us for the morning's incident, because he thought we were headed to a concentration camp, and that our pay would be issued, only we should conduct ourselves in soldierly fashion. In the evening we sing, which the French liked very much. The prayer was also sung together.

June 30, Sunday
The company goes to the little church on the beach. A poor but very pleasant little church — it could barely hold 1 company. On top of that a few airmen arrived who are also awaiting departure, along with a few civilians. During the service we sang church hymns, and at the end, "Boże coś Polskę" [a Polish patriotic hymn]. Then the priest said a prayer in French for Poland and France. After the service the company marched back to quarters in formation of fours, singing. The French came out onto the road and watched us, because they generally don't sing. We showed them that we are soldiers, and they immediately began treating us differently — they even salute our officers now.
After lunch I enjoy a swim in the sea.

July 1, 1940.
Corporal Sadecki and I do our laundry in a nearby stream. The very clean, soft water, together with a very sunny day, helped me wash 3 shirts, 2 pairs of underpants, 2 towels, and 1 pair of socks — within 3 hours they were completely dry and neatly folded into squares. Corporal Sodecki was quite amazed at how cleanly they were washed. In the afternoon I take over duty as NCO of the day for the company. The company is only supposed to form up for roll call three times a day, and even that they don't want to do. I had to put in a lot of effort to get even half the company to assemble. The heat is unbearable — so much so that even sitting in the barrack without moving, everyone sweats until the sweat runs down them in streams.

July 2, 1940, Tuesday
A company of black soldiers from Sudan arrives at the camp. Black as tar, with hair on their heads like young lambs. One of them started combing his hair and couldn't pull the comb back out, even though the hair was no longer than half a centimeter — it was that thick and curly. They are very disciplined, only uneducated. In the evening we invited a few of them into our barrack. At first they were very shy, but once we treated them to wine, and made a little chain bracelet for one of them, they loosened up and talked freely, and we had no small amount of laughter at their expense. They come from Sudan, and have two or three wives each, or even more, depending on how rich a man is. They are of various faiths; several are Catholic, and these wear medallions and little crosses.
We laughed quite a lot when one of our friends showed them binoculars and they looked through them. When asked how old he was, one of them answered with fits of laughter, and finally said that maybe his father knew, but he didn't, because he was small then and didn't remember.
While out for a walk on the beach before evening, I saw a group of black soldiers sitting on the sand, when suddenly a French NCO came up with a camera and, without saying anything to them, wanted to photograph them, but he didn't succeed, because as soon as they saw him they scattered as if a bomb had fallen among them. After long negotiations he managed to persuade one of them to be photographed.
Rumors are going around the camp that the English bombed Berlin with 500 aircraft. That they bombed military targets in Poland and in France, and even bombed Paris. That the English, with the help of the Abyssinians, have nearly driven the Italians out of Abyssinia. And that Russia has seized Bessarabia and Bukovina from Romania, and Subcarpathian Rus' from Hungary.

At lunchtime I had a small quarrel with Corporal Jodłowski, and if I had been a bit more short-tempered, it would have come to blows.
In the afternoon, while out for a walk, I came across a cork tree. This is a tree whose bark grows back and is used to make corks.
Remnants of Polish units arrived at the beach camp, but all of them wanted to return to their homes. In general, almost all our countrymen from France have been raised in the French spirit, and think only of having (boku manże — a garbled phonetic rendering of French, roughly meaning "food and drink," i.e. a comfortable life), while the patriotic spirit in them has died out.

July 3, 1940, Wednesday
Word reaches us that the English sank several units of the French war fleet because they refused to fight on England's side and also did not want to be interned. Reportedly one aircraft carrier was sunk, the cruiser Strasburg was sunk, and the cruiser Dunkierka was said to be badly damaged.
Because of this there was great satisfaction among the Poles, while the French shook their heads.

July 4, Thursday
Nothing important, except that I went to steal apricots in the fields, because I didn't have a single centime and I really wanted some fruit. Apricots are very cheap here, 2 francs per kilogram.

July 5, Friday
After lunch the sergeant major gathered the company and shared the news that there is a ship in the port of Vendres that could take us to Algiers, and that the air force major had gone to the port about this matter, and that Lieutenant Osiecki had gone to fetch civilian clothing. He ordered us to pack up and not go anywhere, because we could leave at any moment. Great joy broke out, but it came to nothing. Civilian clothes were brought, but they weren't handed out.
The gendarmerie began harassing us — one airman got slapped in the face by a gendarme, two were handcuffed for some trivial reasons, and one had his bicycle taken away. Our authorities intervened in this matter, with results. One of the gendarmes was arrested for insulting the whole unit.
In the evening some of the guys organized an expedition to the women's camp, and the next day there were plenty of amused comments about it.

July 6, 1940, Saturday
We received civilian clothing, and the lieutenant informed us that the ministry has taken an interest in us and will help us leave, but as civilians, and that we must change into civilian clothes because the French are expecting a German commission at the camp, and we cannot be here as the Polish army, which has already been dissolved. If the gendarmerie should bother us, we are to say (que suis Polonais) — "I am Polish." We are not very eager to take off our uniforms, because the civilian clothes are terrible cheap junk, but the boys manage as best they can to look decent somehow. The black soldiers, who as I mentioned above became great friends with us, are once again a bit too much for us.

After we had settled down to sleep, Senior Private Pała came to our barrack, and we had a great laugh at his expense. He is generally the camp's laughingstock.
July 7, Sunday
I got up from my bunk after lunch, having spent the whole morning lying in bed reading a book (The Unknown Island). After lunch I went with 3 friends to get fruit in the fields. About 3 km from the camp we entered what was practically a forest of apricot and peach trees. We stuffed ourselves until our teeth ached. During the whole afternoon in that orchard we met 2 men and 2 women on a cart full of fruit, and they gave us beretfuls of apricots.
Corporal Komar photographed our group together with the black soldiers. I am leaving a place for it in the diary.

Photograph with black soldiers at the camp at Plage Angeles. Kneeling: Widło, Sadecki, Walewicz. Standing: Wołk, Sadowski, a black soldier, Kania, a black soldier, Pisulewski; in the middle, in a beret, Mazur's head.

July 8, 1940, Monday
At 10 o'clock a company assembly was ordered, at which an informal comrades' tribunal was held against two soldiers for theft. The first was Private Liberadzki, commonly called "the Gypsy." His offense was selling a government-issue leather jacket and two revolvers stolen from fellow soldiers. The sergeant major laid out the charges against him, having irrefutable proof of his guilt, which he denied, and for that he got a few punches in the face in front of the company. Liberadzki began to run away, and on the way got one more kick.

The second culprit was some former policeman, though not from our combat company but one of the stragglers. The sergeant major accused him of robbing an apartment near where our trucks were parked. The culprit was not present at the assembly; the sergeant major had to fetch him from the barracks, shoving him along, and as soon as he arrived several men jumped up and punched him in the face. Then the sergeant major struck him a few times across the back with a rubber hose, and later across the hands, amid the encouraging shouts of the whole company. Then the sergeant major called for a few men to lay him on a bench, because he wanted to give him a few strokes of the hose on his backside, but during the scuffle with him, Lieutenant Stanek, who was present the whole time, stood up in the culprit's defense and told him to run away, though on his way out the sergeant major did not fail to give him a few more strokes with the hose. The lieutenant ordered the company to disperse, except for those who had revolvers and binoculars, whom he made give their word that they would not sell their pistols, but on the contrary would keep them very carefully hidden.
In the afternoon the sergeant major exchanged my civilian suit for a better one.

July 9, Tuesday
Toward morning we were woken by a visit from a civilian, a gendarme, and the commander of the guard — they were searching for something in the barracks. Only after breakfast did I find out that it was the father of one of the Frenchmen who are in our company, and that he had come for his son. He took four Frenchmen away with him.
In the evening one of the guys played the violin. Even the French gathered and listened, and it was clear they enjoyed it.

July 10, Wednesday
We received our pay, a total of 829 fr. After lunch I go into town wanting to buy a shirt. But I couldn't get one; I only bought slippers for 12 francs.
Corporal Wojtkowiak, who had stayed behind at the positions with Platoon Sergeant Sum, joined the company and told us about the death of Platoon Sergeant Sum and his driver. According to his account, the tank was knocked out by the French with a 75mm gun, by mistake. The sergeant did not die inside the tank but had left it because it was on fire. The shell tore off his head and both arms, and his entrails spilled out.
Driver Senior Private Świecicki was severely wounded in the left leg near the hip and in the left arm in two places, and also had several shrapnel fragments in his stomach. Doctors from the French Red Cross took him to the hospital, but held out no hope of keeping him alive.

July 11, Thursday
Boredom, an almost hopeless wait — several ships have already sailed from the port, and no one wants to talk with us. Our passports have been made, but there are no visas. Corporal Jodłowski took his passport without a visa from the lieutenant and is trying to arrange things on his own.
It's raining, and sitting idly in the barrack like this, I ponder our situation, and despair overtakes us — we are almost without a way out.

July 12, Friday
I was in town wanting to buy bread, but it's hard to get. We mostly live on tomato salad because it's cheap, and the food from the kitchen is very bad. They give us rice thick one day and rice watery the next, and for breakfast and dinner black, bitter coffee. In the evening, next to the barracks of the black soldiers' company, the black soldiers put on dances, beating on tin cans. It was exactly like what's shown in films, and even more so, because there were such moves as could not be shown on film without corrupting people. The guard that watched over the camp, which had been kept by French airmen, was abolished.

July 13, Saturday
Corporal Kanar took a few photographs of me. In the evening, while on a walk with Corporal Sadowski, Kania, and Walewicz, as well as a Frenchman known as Jurek, I saw fishermen pulling their nets from the sea. The catch was very poor. They caught a fish similar to an eel, except it had a beak-like snout, like a swordfish's [sujka in the original], a fifth of its total length.

In the evening I was out having wine with a few friends, and we found three Spaniards there. As it later turned out, one of them was a captain in the aviation of the Republican army. They complained bitterly about General Franco, saying that he had brought foreigners into his country while driving out all the Spaniards. And that the Republican army had not been communist.

July 15, Monday
The sea is very rough.

July 16, Tuesday
Corporal Sadecki brings me a suitcase from Perpignan; the suitcase, shirt, and tie together cost 100 francs. In the evening, while bathing in the river, I lost my little gold cross, but after a long search I found it.

July 17, Wednesday
Black dances and a drunk French soldier liven up our evening. After the evening news, I was with Corporal Jan Sadecki at a café having beer, and there the plan arose to go to Perpignan

July 18, 1940, Thursday
I went with Corporal Sadecki to Perpignan to go shopping. I bought light-colored trousers and a shirt of a similar color, plus a tie, for 38 fr., a windbreaker for 69 fr., a small case for 5 fr., and I also visited the Hotel Ambasador — in total I spent 400 francs.
I spend the beautiful moonlit evening, as usual, on a walk.

July 19, 1940, Friday

Boredom. The President of the United States of America, Roosevelt, spoke on the radio. Among other things, he said that the Germans claim they want to bring something new into the world, whereas they are only recreating what existed 15 centuries ago in the time of the pharaohs — a tyranny worthy of contempt.

July 20, 1940, Saturday
In the evening I was at the cinema in Argeles with Corporal Sadecki. The program was good.

July 21, 1940, Sunday
At the service which took place in the little church on the beach, so many people went up to Holy Communion that the priest ran out of hosts. Four black soldiers also received Holy Communion. In the afternoon we found out that the commandant of the Polish camp, Air Force Major Moszkowski, had fled to England. All the Poles were outraged at him.

June 22, 1940, Monday
The company received its pay, including war supplements.

July 23, Tuesday
At 6:30 we set out on a hike into the mountains. Janek Sadecki, Stawiarski, Świątkowski, and I go. Along the way we wanted to buy bread in town, but it could not be bought anywhere. We went to a peak visible from afar, on which there is an observation tower. Along the way we found a mulberry tree with very large, sweet fruit.

29
I liked them very much, even though I don't usually care for mulberries. There is a spring right below the summit, from which the water flows down through pipes. I tried out my pistol with a few shots. I was also at the top of the tower, which has no stairs, so one had to climb the wall at great risk to life. In short, I spent a very pleasant time. We decide to go up to the summit once more, but in a larger group and with a camera.

July 24, Wednesday

Yesterday, while I was on the hike, the sergeant major found a new thief and gave him a beating, later handing him over to the French at the camp. Today there was trouble again, because apparently some officer cadet was stealing gasoline from civilian cars, and when he was caught, they beat up a policeman. Because of this they intend to curtail our freedom. The same sort of thing is happening with our officers, who occupied villas without permission and brought women there, which the French look upon with disapproval. In general, people who have pretensions to being cultured turn out to be the worst. In the evening Lieutenant Osiecki held a company assembly and announced that the French are demanding that we hold formations — in short, activities — because troops sitting idle become demoralized.

During the assembly, a gendarme brought in those who had been stealing gasoline in Argeles and handed them over to the lieutenant. But the lieutenant said nothing to them. After the assembly, though, the soldiers themselves gave them a beating in the barracks, the officer cadet not excluded. Janek Sadowski in particular took an especially active part in this action. Lieutenant Osiecki released 10 of us for a hike in the mountains the next day.

July 25, Thursday

At 6 o'clock the seven of us set out on a hike to the same peak as before. We had plenty of provisions and revolver ammunition. Platoon Sergeant Michał Wolski and Corporal Daniel Kanar had cameras and took several photographs. We had a lot of laughs with Platoon Sergeant Wolski, because he's very cheerful. On the way back we bathed in a pool located halfway up the mountain. After descending into the valley we rested in the park of a beautiful little manor house, which we also immortalized on film.

July 26, 1940, Friday

We rest after yesterday's hike. There are supposedly activities scheduled, but only half the company showed up for assembly. The day's activity was a swim in the sea. In the afternoon I repaired my boots, which had been torn yesterday.

In the evening I was with Janek Sadecki in Argeles (the town) at a film titled The Miserable Ones [Les Misérables].

July 27, 1940, Saturday

All day I watched and helped pull an aircraft engine out of the sea. But it was very heavy and the ropes kept breaking. I tried diving in a gas mask; for this purpose I twisted together 10 hoses from other masks into one, so that the end could be held at the surface of the sea by another man, and I submerged myself several times near the shore. But the attempts produced no results, because the water pressure was so great that it pressed the mask tightly against my face, making breathing so difficult that one could not last underwater longer than two minutes.

July 28, 1940, Sunday

The French gendarmes ordered us to move out of our barrack into the large one. In the new spot we settled in reasonably well, even though there's no floor there, but we made one for ourselves out of bunk boards, of course only next to our own bunks. In the evening I was with Janek Sadecki at a café having fish. For the past few days he's been treating me, because I have no money.

July 29, Monday

During the morning activities someone brought up the matter of Sergeant Gbur, who had spent several days in Toulouse. And since he had previously often said that he wanted to go back under German rule, he was suspected of having gone to the German consulate about it. I too had told Lieutenant Stanek that Sergeant Gbur had said he would go back under German rule, and that by doing so he was greatly demoralizing the company. Sergeant Gbur was absent from the morning activities. In the afternoon Lieutenant S. held an assembly of the professional NCOs and brought up this matter without saying that it was Sergeant G. who had done this.

Besides that, he announced that the professional soldiers were to pay 5 francs per day for food. Naturally, this pleased no one. After the assembly, Sergeant Gbur came to me with reproaches, saying that I had told the lieutenant that he had gone to the German consulate. Of course I set the matter straight, though he did not believe it, and said that he would report this to the authorities at the appropriate moment, along with the fact that in Roan I had not carried out his order and had not allowed my men to bring coffee for his crew. My crew did not want coffee because there was no time to drink it.

July 30, 1940, Tuesday

First English lesson; I'm also learning French. Janek Sadecki went to the port of Vandres, to a villa near Kotani.

July 31, 1940, Wednesday

Studying English and French.

August 1, 1940, Thursday

Same as the previous day.

August 2, 1940, Friday

The company switched to independent self-catering, and the professional soldiers must pay for their food. They are to pay two weeks in advance and two weeks in arrears. The food improved a little in quality, but there isn't enough of it. Now provisions are drawn from the military quartermaster's office, rather than, as before, from the Spanish camp.

August 2, 1940, Saturday

Laundering underwear/linen and self-study of French and English.

August 4, 1940, Sunday

At High Mass I was in Argeles (the town) together with a few friends. At lunchtime three Englishmen who had escaped from German captivity arrived at the camp. They are from the Expeditionary Corps in France. We received them very warmly. They chose Polish surnames for themselves and are trying to get Polish papers. And as Poles they want to go to England. The Chief placed them in the care of Corporal Jodłowski and Rydlewski. In the evening I was at the cinema with Janek Sadowski. The program was very boring.

August 5, Monday

Nothing important.

August 6, Tuesday

Before noon, instead of English lessons, there was pay disbursement. During the payout a misunderstanding arose with Corporal Świątkowski over the war supplement. Świątkowski insulted Lieutenant Stanek, saying he had robbed the company. At the afternoon assembly the matter was clarified. They did not receive the war supplement — only 300 francs per head for travel, donated by the consulate. Lt. Stanek is very offended at the company and declared that he would try to have the camp disbanded.

August 7, Wednesday

I was woken by an aircraft flying overhead which circled several times above the camp and then flew off to the north, descending very low, as if to land.

We receive pay totaling 722 francs, and after deducting 140 francs for food for a period of two weeks, 582 francs remained.

After lunch I went into town with Janek Sadecki, but nothing could be bought because the meager supplies that were there had been bought up by Spaniards who are returning to Spain from the internment camps. In the evening at a café I met Fredek Rydlewski with two Englishmen. One of them had run off after quarreling drunkenly with his companions, having taken Fredek's revolver. I treated them to an omelet and beer so as to repay the Englishmen for what they had given me when I was on leave. They last received their pay in May, so they are hard up.

August 8, Thursday

During the morning session Lt. Osiecki made us younger or older, for the purpose of changing our passports so we could leave. Three new photographs are needed for a new passport.

After lunch I played volleyball with the Englishmen, but they don't know how to play. In the evening I was at the café with Janek. We only got potatoes in sauce, and wine, of which there is plenty. For a few days now they've been selling grapes, costing 3.50 cnt.

August 9, Friday

Our platoon is on kitchen duty, and during this work Kanar took a photo of us. I wrote a letter to Irenka.

In the evening I was at the radio at Mr. Cymerman's.

August 10, 1940, Saturday

The French are checking the company's roster. At lunchtime Ślęzak and Stawiarski painted up one of the Black soldiers and we took a photo with him. There was a lot of laughter over it. In the evening I was with Janek and Wolski Mich. in Argeles (the town). We wanted to eat dinner there, but nothing could be had. We only bought bread and ate half of it on the way.

(photo)

With the painted-up Black soldier. Photo: Komar

August 11, 1940, Sunday

We were at Mr. Cymerman's for the radio, after the news broadcast, which could not be understood at all; Mr. Cym. gave us some information and then began asking about military matters, for instance the organization of the army. We brushed him off as best we could, but I'm curious what he needs it for.

August 12, Monday

Since morning I've noticed some secret conversations, some whispering. 2nd Lt. Chromiński gave me to understand that we're leaving tomorrow. So I'm getting ready for the road. I'm washing my linen. And before evening the first ones, i.e. the platoon of stragglers, are already leaving one by one. My watch is still at the watchmaker's. In the evening 2nd Lt. Chromiński announced at the briefing that we are allowed to take our military belongings with us and that we leave tomorrow.

August 13, Tuesday

We depart via Narbonne, where during the transfer I buy a shirt, paying 70 fr., and a cap for 12 fr. Then via Montpellier, Nîmes, Tarascon, Avignon, to Marseille. Just past Narbonne, the door caught Lt. Chromiński's fingers, and on account of the heat he fainted. In Marseille we wait several hours in the night. While waiting we sing Polish songs to guitar accompaniment. The French listened in very willingly.

August 14, Wednesday

We leave Marseille for Cassis station and from there for the Campagne camp. During the journey I felt very well. There are already many Poles in the camp, gathered from various units. The barracks here are much better than in Coetquidan and Argeles. We even got sheets. In the afternoon I was on top of the mountain overlooking the camp.

August 15, 1940, Thursday

A religious service was held, because it is Polish Army Day. I wander about the surrounding mountains. In the evening I met former riflemen of the Castle Company, namely Laszkiewicz, Szulżycki, Jezierski, Kotyła, Dziudzik.
A few minutes later I met one more, of the class of 1915, namely Długosz.

I had a drink with him. He told me he had been in Norway and had taken part in the attack on Narvik.

August 16, Friday

I was with Wolski and Rej in the small town, shopping. You can get everything here and the people are very well disposed toward Poles. One has the impression of having come to a different country. In the evening, in a nearby little wood, we lit a campfire and fried up scrambled eggs from 20 eggs, and rendered 1 kg of fatback into lard.

August 17, 1940, Saturday

Word is going around that the French have held Lt. Słomka along with the rest of the company as a labor company. In the afternoon, sunbathing in the mountains. I found a lot of people there sunbathing in Adam's costume. In the evening I visited the former Castle men.

August 18, 1940, Sunday

In the chapel located in one of the barracks a service was held. It was celebrated by a French priest, who delivered the sermon in French, while we sang Polish hymns. After the service Wolski and Długosz took photos of us (the Castle men). Word is going around that the Germans have launched an attack on England. In the afternoon I was in the small town of Aubagne with Janek Solec. and Józek Rej, at the photographer's and to listen to the radio. We couldn't listen to the radio because listening to the radio in public places is forbidden. Janek lent me 50 fr. because I badly needed it ....
We return by a different, longer road, but a very beautiful one, and the evening was a lovely
moonlit one.

August 19, 1940, Monday
Nothing worth noting.

August 20, 1940, Tuesday; August 21, 1940, Wednesday; August 22, 1940, Thursday
— With Sadecki and Rej we go to Aubagne and to a French family, very well
disposed toward Poles, for the radio.

August 23, 1940, Friday
The Englishmen who had been with us were sent by the camp commandant to Marseille, because
there is an English hospital there and they got care there!

August 24, 1940, Saturday
Wolski's photos didn't turn out well and I teased him about it, so he
tore up the photograph I was in, and I'm keeping my piece of it as a keepsake. (fragment of a photo) We toast my birthday with just a bottle of
beer, because there's no cash.

August 25, 1940, Sunday
After Holy Mass, celebrated by a Polish priest, I went with Skorupski to pick
blackberries, and I brought back a small suitcase full of them, and he a mess tin.

August 26, 1940, Monday
Nothing to note.

August 27, 1940, Tuesday
All sorts of rumors are circulating among the men, which are further
exaggerated on account of the nervous tension. Of those I
come into contact with, I like best Bernatowicz, Master of Laws, who thinks very
soberly and calmly, and has an excellent grasp of
European politics. He is here as a plain rifleman, and despite his
education does not put himself forward as an officer cadet, as others do
who don't even have a secondary education. Rumors are circulating that a labor camp is to be set up here.
(photo)
Returning from a walk: Rej Józef, Sadecki Jan, Pisulewski Stefan.

August 28, 1940, Wednesday
We each receive 320 francs sent from Argeles, which Capt. Pietruszka
collected from the post office and wanted to deduct from us for food. The Chief, Kobyliński, and Lt. Szajer intervened in the matter and it was paid out
to us. In the afternoon some captain took command of the Company. At the
French family's, where we go for the radio, I peeled potatoes for my own supper.

August 29, 1940, Thursday
2nd Lt. Chromiński designated me and Tadeusz Mazur for departure from
France. For this purpose we go to Marseille, to the Consulate. There we receive
instructions. Among those designated for departure was Edward Pała, but Mr.
Górski told the cavalry captain what sort of fellow he was, so the captain
had him replaced. We spend the night in Aubagne — I at Figiel's, and Mazur at another hotel.

August 30, 1940, Friday
We wait for departure all day at the American hospital, where the
shelter of the Polish Red Cross is now housed. In the evening
we learn from Sergeant Edward Kiercul that we leave tomorrow morning.
So we buy any old trifles just to get rid of our francs. I sleep in
a car near the hospital, because the hospital is so overcrowded that people even sleep in the
corridors. I got very cold there and bruised my sides.

August 31, Saturday
At 7:30 in the morning Lt. Pasionek comes for us — there are five of us — and takes us
into town, to a café, where we are to wait for the organizer of this
expedition. We waited like that until 2 p.m. with no result.
We returned to the hospital, and by the next tram Lt. Pasionek came chasing after us
and told us to come back in an hour to the same place; there we waited until 10 in the evening, with no result. We return to the hospital by the last tram;
I'm out of cash, and only Tadek Mazur is driving me around at his own expense.

September 1, Sunday
Anniversary of the outbreak of the war. On this occasion there was a service in the
Dominican church, at which, however, I was not present, because I had gone with Tadek
Mazur to Carpiagne. When I walked into the barrack I felt just
as if I'd come home on leave. All our own fellows there. There
we fill out demobilization forms and return to Marseille. At the hospital
we meet Sergeant Komisarz. He treats us to strong beer and we spend
a pleasant evening to the sound of Mazur's guitar and the guitar of one
of the hospital's residents.
(photo)
Tadeusz Mazur, September 3, 1940 September 2, Monday
We return to Carpiagne because the departure fell through. Lt. Pasionek also goes
to the camp. At the Company the captain makes difficulties about
taking us back into the company, since we had been absent for several days. Finally he
softened and said that maybe something could be arranged.
It was only in the evening that Opałko reminded me that it's my name day.

September 3, Tuesday
I do my laundry, and in the afternoon peel potatoes, because our barrack is on duty.

September 4, 1940, Wednesday
Before evening I went with Józek Rej and Janek Sadecki to
the small town of Penne, to that French family already mentioned above,
where we used to go for the radio. This couple invited us for figs, which grow here
half-wild. For the first time in my life I ate figs not dried but
straight from the tree. They resemble small pears and taste
very sweet and are very sticky. We also ate a few sweet
almonds, picking them straight off the tree. They are similar to apricots, and one eats
the pit. The shell splits like on a chestnut, and that pit
remains on the tree. Next we had them make us a salad of 1 kg of tomatoes
with the addition of cucumbers, onion, and vinegar.
We ate our fill properly, paying only 3 fr. 50 cm. The line that supplies
the electricity for lighting this house had been damaged and we couldn't
listen to the radio, but in this situation the couple's brother-in-law came to our rescue and
invited us to his place, as he lives nearby.
The news broadcast from London at 9:15 p.m. began with a speech by
the President of the Republic of Poland, Raczkiewicz. Then came bombardments
on both sides, and the handing over by the United States of 50
destroyers. Roman Długosz was also with us at the broadcast, along with a friend.
(6 photos)
Sadecki Jan; Rej Józef; Wolski Michał; Maj Edward; Kanar Daniel; Bardzki Michał September 5, 1940, Thursday
In the afternoon the captain held a company assembly at which he shared with us
the following news: that French officers have been assigned to the
company, and he asked that we treat them politely, because
they too are in the same position as us, since they come from northern
France and cannot return home.
That language lessons will be resumed in the company and a choir will be founded.
Finally he announced that our company will probably be demobilized tomorrow.
Here a dispute arose between the company and the captain, concerning the
demobilization bonus, totaling 1000 francs.
How this matter ended I don't know, because I slipped away, not wanting
to listen to these arguments.

September 6, 1940, Friday
In the evening, on the sports field, a bonfire was arranged through the efforts of the camp command,
at which the French commandant of the Carpiagne camp was present, together with
the officers assigned to the labor companies. The choir, not yet very well rehearsed,
sang a few beautiful songs. There were also a few recitations and
skits. All of this was translated for the French by one of the Poles. When
the couplets sang that we'd slip away from the French to the English anyway, one
of the French officers said, "and I'll go with you."
The French were very pleased, and at the close the French captain thanked us and concluded with the words that he would be very happy if
there were thousands of camps like this one throughout France.
From these words we understood that if there were many such camps
with such a spirit, France would rise up to fight the invader.

September 7, 1940, Saturday
The company receives a radio, through the efforts of the company commander, Capt.
Hucakowski, given as a gift by a friend of his, some Major (I don't know which one). Apparently
General Duch was in the camp yesterday and reportedly declared that we would
receive the demobilization bonus before long. In the afternoon the company was called
to demobilize, but the riflemen refused to sign the
demobilization card without the bonus being paid out. So the demobilization
was postponed.
(6 photos)
Three comrades notable for their good fellowship and calm temperament:
Walewicz Tadeusz; Sadowski Stanisław (he retouched this one himself); Kania Jan.
Seidel Bronisław; Company Chief Kobyliński Konstanty; Rydlewski
Fryderyk.

September 8, 1940, Sunday
In the evening we go to our French family for the radio. Besides
our foursome, Chief Kobyliński was there too. We ate supper there. The
radio news was hard to hear because a storm was raging and all the
thunder interfered. After the news we wanted to leave, but since it was raining, these good people equipped us with raincoats and umbrellas. At the
end they bid us a warm farewell, seeing us out to the door, and truly
we had the impression of leaving the home of the very best friends. On the
way back the downpour gave us a hard time, despite our having the
aforementioned articles of clothing. The Chief was very pleased and gave those children
lots of kisses.

September 9, 1940, Monday
We return the borrowed articles of clothing late in the evening.
The career non-commissioned officers refused to sign for demobilization without
receiving the bonus. 2nd Lt. Sztajer and Chromiński set the example.
Capt. Hussakowski urges everyone to sign the demobilization card without
the 1000 fr., and only collect it later, once it is granted.

September 10, 1940, Tuesday
Everyone sits in the barracks because it's cold, since the mistral is blowing
its customary three-day spell.

September 11, 1940, Wednesday
Capt. Hussakowski left without saying goodbye to the company.

September 12, 1940, Thursday
Nothing important (the mistral has died down).

September 13, 1940, Friday

In the morning, an assembly of all companies and an inspection by a French
divisional general. After the review on the parade ground, an inspection of some of the
barracks. One of the platoons of the 2nd company is sent off to work. General
Duch urges this, saying that conditions there will be materially better, and
that if Poles were to leave for England he would remember them. In that
platoon are former riflemen of the Castle Company. All day people talk about
a German offensive against England. I am, like all the
Poles for that matter, very excited about this. The evening news broadcast from London corrects this
report. It turned out to be untrue. The English expect the offensive
in two or three days.

September 14, 1940, Saturday
Cold and bad weather, so until noon I lie in bed, and in the afternoon I wander
about the barrack like a lost soul; such idleness is terrible, especially when,
on top of it, people talk of nothing else but politics and all sorts of
filth — such as how so-and-so promoted someone else, or put himself up for demobilization
as an officer or an officer cadet.

September 15, 1940, Sunday
In the camp, laziness is spreading, so that there's no one to work in the kitchen. By
order of the Camp Command, company 3 (the Podhale men) was designated as the duty company,
but it refused to go to work. So the kitchen commander, Master Sergeant
Szumilas, turned to our Chief, and our barrack went and took care of it.
In the afternoon it rains, and Józek Rej is in Marseille, because he went there to Lt. Osiecki, with Fatyga, about the matter of promotion to sergeant.
On the way back he stopped by our French friends and there
was treated to supper, and they asked him to stay the night, but he
did not agree to this.

September 16, 1940, Monday
At lunchtime we receive notice that our company is to be
demobilized in the afternoon. For this purpose an assembly was held to
discuss what to do. Some wanted to sign, others did not; after long arguments
it was settled that everyone should do as he liked. And so the company went to be demobilized,
because apparently some major (which one I don't know) said that we would
receive the 1000 fr. demobilization bonus before long. As we were going to the
demobilization, I ran into Janek Sadecki near our French
friends, who had come by on a stroll to visit us. I brought Magi and
Monika into the barrack so they could call for Rej and
Wolski. This caused such merriment that the whole barrack came running to see them.
They took my little tank from me, and only at their mother's intervention did they give it back to me. 2nd Lt.
Chromiński lent me 50 fr. as soon as I gave him to understand what I needed, and
we went to the café in the valley, where we had some beer, ate some fruit,
and chatted a little — partly in words and partly by hand. Afterward we
walked the ladies and children home, while the husband of the plumper one rode
ahead on a bicycle. Along the way Wolski took a few photos of us, and the time
passed very merrily. Once there, we ate supper for that 50 fr., consisting
of a ham omelet and 1 liter of wine.
We couldn't listen to the radio news bulletin because one of the Poles
(an officer) fiddled with the set for so long that he didn't manage in time. Our way
back was beautiful, since it's a full moon and the sky is cloudless.

September 17, 1940, Tuesday
We are waiting for demobilization, without result.

September 18, 1940, Wednesday
In the morning Fatyga hands out the ID papers of those newly promoted. They were
promoted to the rank of corporal-platoon [plut.]: Fatyga Jan, Rej Józef and Sadowski Stanisław,
and one more veteran corporal from Żurawica. To the rank of corporal a great many
senior privates were appointed, some who had been through the NCO school and some without it, depending on
what each man reported, because everything here is done on trust. Among them was
Kacperek Szczepan, Kowalski, Liberadzki and many others. With Seidel
some mistake occurred, because he was issued a new ID but with the same
rank. The appointment order is dated June 15, 1940.
I sign for demobilization, receiving pay for 1 month. After deductions
for food and the money that came from Argeles, I was left with 220
francs.
In the camp is rittmeister [cavalry captain] Kiersz. Lieutenant Chrominiec sends me to
Marseille to Lieutenant Osiecki, or failing him Górski, for 20 passports. But I did not find them there, because they had gone to Carpiagne. I waited there 5 hours.
After returning to the camp I learned that a large number of men had left just
a few minutes earlier. Among them Sadecki and Rej, and I was supposed to go too,
but I was late and someone else was taken in my place. Wolski showed me
which direction they had gone, so I ran after them. I caught up with them
waiting in a small wood by the sports field. At my request the lieutenant told me to
pack and come along, while Ślęzak, who had a watch at the watchmaker's, was to
stay behind. And so it was done.
We were led secretly out of the wood to a crossroads where a guide was supposed
to be waiting. The guide did not come, although we waited until
1 in the morning, from 9 in the evening. While we waited, the captain, commander of
the group, explained that ships were supposed to be waiting for us at Cassis and would take us
to Gibraltar. Passage for one person was supposed to cost 3000 francs, and there
were 120 of us. The money was to be collected by them only after we boarded, one
half then, and the other half a week later they could withdraw from the bank. Toward
two in the morning we returned to the barracks, still hoping that tomorrow might be better.

September 19, 1940, Thursday
The company draws its uniforms. The denims and underwear are new, the wool
uniforms and coats old. I take all of it anyway, since I don't know what
awaits me, or indeed all of us. Janek went to Marseille, so I took
uniforms for him too. He bought me a small suitcase in Marseille for my
personal things. The evening bulletin from London reports that the French
island of New Caledonia has come out in favor of General de Gaulle.

September 20, 1940, Friday
Nothing worth noting, except that dinner is being cooked for supper and
vice versa. Otherwise the food has improved slightly.

September 21, 1940, Saturday
I write out French words into the notebook that Janek S. bought me yesterday.
Almost the whole day there was fog; only in the evening did the sun appear.
One of the men returned from the territories occupied by Germany and tells
about conditions there. It's supposedly not as bad as it seems.

September 22, 1940, Sunday
Before evening I went with Janek S. and Michał W. to take photographs
in order to use up the roll of film on which our French friends appear, and thereby
speed up getting their pictures developed. With Janek I took the film to be developed
in Cassis; on that occasion I looked around the little town and bought a couple of
postcards as souvenirs. As for going to England, I have lost hope.

September 23, 1940, Monday
In the evening a camp bonfire, better organized than the previous one and in
a very good spot, at the narrowest point of the ravine; the audience
sat on the rocks and could see everything very well. At the bonfire a
French colonel was present, along with several French officers assigned to
us. The bonfire ended with songs sung all together:

"Warszawianka," "Pod Twą obronę," and a camp song that
arose in Hungary or Romania. The colonel was bid farewell in French,
everyone together saying "Bon soir mon colonel." Afterward the colonel
said that he firmly believed Poland and France would rise again.
The audience answered with a shout of "Vive la France," the colonel
added "Vive la Pologne," and to him, in Polish, three times "niech żyje" [long live].
After that bonfire I felt greatly uplifted in spirit.
Our French friends came to visit us, but I did not see them. Janek S. and Józek R.
saw them off. They are said to have been quite offended with me over that. The radio
reports that the Italian offensive on Egypt has been halted and that General de Gaulle
has landed with troops at Dakar.

September 24, 1940, Tuesday
I went with Misiek W. to the highest mountain peak in this area. From
there Marseille could be seen as if on the palm of one's hand. Misiek was going to take
photographs, but poor visibility got in our way. We promised each other that as
soon as the weather is suitable we will go there again to take pictures.
Misiek climbed the rocks in such a way that it even amazed me. Janek and Józek
brought back photographs from Cassis. They came out well. The situation at Dakar with General de
Gaulle is unclear.

September 25, 1940, Wednesday
I receive pay amounting to 7 fr. 50 c. and set it aside for the gravestone of
a Pole from America who died these past days in the camp. I also got my demobilization form. In the evening we bring the photographs to our
French friends. They are pleased with them. But they are very outraged
at England for wanting to take away their colonies. Apparently the English at Dakar
sank one French submarine. The French, in turn, bombarded
Gibraltar.

September 26, 1940, Thursday
Misiek W., Janek S., Michał Bardzki and I went on an excursion to the
highest peak, taking a few photographs there. In a beautiful ravine
we found a scorpion, whose sting causes terrible suffering.
We burned it in a fire. A French captain has been assigned to the company and
today he held a briefing with our officers.

September 27, 1940, Friday
The four of us went to Marseille for vehicles. Misiek W. and Janek S.
were issued a truck and a field kitchen, while Józek R. and I got
an old Ford which we could not get started, so it went in for repair.
With us were French non-commissioned officers, very likeable and
keen supporters of General de Gaulle. We had a bit of a drink with them.
The only newspaper published in Lyon in the Polish language writes in a
pro-German spirit, but jokingly gives one to understand that one shouldn't
believe everything it writes. As an example I am pasting in a clipping from this
newspaper.
(clipping) "From ill tidings.

MY PROVERB
Not every tiding —
Holds the truth inside it. J.M.
More and more I am beginning to feel longing for my country and family, but
I felt it most painfully today, when Stach Sadowski received a letter from Poland from
his fiancée, with news in it about his mother, who lives in Ożarów.
(photograph)
Camp de Carpiagne, general view
And yet I have family in Opatów, which lies so close to
Ożarów. I wanted to write, but I am still holding back, because I don't want
the Germans to know that I am in France.

September 28, 1940, Saturday
Before noon Miszel W. wouldn't hear a word about going to England, but
in the afternoon he got so keen on it that he actually argued with us, saying that
we were egging him on (out of spite) to make him stay.

September 29, 1940, Sunday
The Mistral is blowing, so everyone is sitting in the barracks. We receive news
that tomorrow we are to go out to work. Captain Hussakowski went to the
site to check and get acquainted with the situation. In the evening at roll call the captain
announced that conditions there are poor and that the order has been withdrawn.

September 30, 1940, Monday
In the afternoon our French friends came to visit us. Józek was the first to meet them and let us know. We stayed with them while he went with a
Pole to Marseille for photographs, because now we urgently have to give them
photos in a green shirt with an open collar.
I walked our friends halfway home along with Jaś, and then we came
back. The platoon that had gone out to work two weeks ago came back home.
The lads spoke well of the stay there, even though, apart from their keep, they were only
given 6 francs. The captain held roll call with us and reportedly said that
we do have to go out to work after all, but the company refused. I am curious what will
come of this. Personally I would go, but for the sake of the group I do what
everyone else does. Our company puts up opposition to the French at every turn.

October 1, 1940, Tuesday
Captain Hussakowski urges the company not to resist and to go out to
work. He also asked Major Laskowski, of the camp command, to help. Nothing,
however, helped.

October 2, 1940, Wednesday
Despite a speech which Major Laskowski gave once more, the company
refused to go. Only some other major persuaded us, saying that
we must bear this sacrifice for the good of the cause, so as not to make it harder for others
to leave.
The company went. All the way there it rained, so that even our leather jackets
got soaked through. We traveled by rail to Marseille from Aubagne. There we were
quartered in barracks. The huts were the same, except that in one hut two platoons sleep. Captain Hussakowski resigned his post and
left without saying goodbye. The photographs that Corporal Szempruch took didn't turn
out well, because he cut everyone's heads off.

October 3, 1940, Thursday
We get our bearings. Some of the men make a volleyball court. 20 men, myself
among them, fitted handles onto shovels, pickaxes and hammers for tomorrow's
work. We worked too zealously, but Lieutenant Sztrauch told us not to overexert
ourselves at work, so that too much wouldn't be expected of us. The food is
tasty but too scanty, though the French promise that tomorrow there will be more.
Outside of duty hours we have free leave into town.
In front of the barracks a photo booth (instant photos) was set up; I
had my picture taken.

October 4, 1940, Friday
First day of work. Before starting work a prayer was sung. The food
has not improved at all. Rumors are going around that any day now
we will be able to leave France.

October 5, 1940, Saturday
We work until 11 o'clock, and in the afternoon do laundry. The locality
where we are staying is called Ste. Marthe.

October 6, 1940, Sunday
A battalion of French troops, surrounded on all sides by gendarmes, arrived
at the camp here. They are probably soldiers returning from Africa. They were
locked up in several barracks surrounded by barbed wire and are closely
guarded by double sentries of both soldiers and gendarmerie. They are
undergoing quarantine here.
The whole day I did not go anywhere.

October 7, 1940, Monday
We march to work singing, as indeed we did before too.
The French like it, because they come out of their barracks and listen with great
interest. I have not yet heard the French army sing.
And once more we hand over photographs, two apiece. I must have already
given out about fifteen photographs, with no result.
Today at noon I lost my watch in the washroom.

October 8, 1940, Tuesday
For two days now the Pole Edward (a cadet officer) has been promising us that
there will be a radio, and there isn't one. The company gives him no peace over it.

October 9, 1940, Wednesday
An exceptionally lucky day. The food improved in quantity; from the
barracks the unneeded bunks were cleared out, giving us more room,
so much so that between the beds a table and two benches can now be put. This was not accomplished, however, without opposition from certain individuals. Finally
the Pole brought the radio. A very good set. Janek S. had a tooth pulled today and
walks around looking poisoned.
Michał Bardzki's neck hurts, and my hands are covered
in blisters, and so on — everyone has something ailing them. As for work, the French
don't push us hard, so that whoever doesn't want to work doesn't have to work much. I in
this work seek forgetfulness; I work so as not to think about what is happening.

October 10, 1940, Thursday
Shirking is beginning in the company; nearly half the men are missing from
work. 300 Germans arrived at the camp, returning from the Foreign
Legion. One of the French officers asked us not to talk with these
Germans. No doubt the French are afraid of a fight breaking out between
us and them. Some contagious illness has appeared in the company, and because of
that we are forbidden to go into town.

October 11, 1940, Friday
Among the Germans who arrived there is one Pole. Apparently a rate of
1 franc an hour has been set for us. The Poles are to vacate the Carpiagne camp
by Sunday.

October 12, 1940, Saturday For the first time in France I bathe in a military bathhouse; it is very well equipped. To my platoon a schoolmate of mine from the 7th form of St. Florian's School in Kraków was assigned — Lipka Stanisław.

October 13 Sunday, 14 Monday, 15 Tuesday, and 16 Tuesday (should read Wednesday).
Work on the road.

October 17, 1940, Thursday

Second Lieutenant Chromiński discreetly slipped 100 fr. into my pocket. It fell to me as if from heaven, since for a good while now I've been living on Jaś S.'s charity, and he doesn't have anything either.
In the evening Janek and I were at the home of a certain French family, fixing their stove. Janek found this job. What matters to me above all is the chance to practice conversation in French.

October 18, 1940, Friday
Lieutenant Sztranek is coming down harder on the company, keeping order more and more strictly. Rumors are going around that several men from our company went to Nîmes to get demobilized a second time. There is suspicion regarding a few men in this matter, but I am not certain of it. Besides, in such cases it is better not to bandy names about.

October 19, 1940, Saturday

We receive 20 francs of relief money from the Red Cross each. In the evening I was again with Janek at the home of the French family living next to the camp, fixing the stove and having a talk. We also ate a very modest supper.
Among the civilian population one senses greater hunger than in our company.

October 20, 1940, Sunday

I spend the day reading a book titled White Fang.

October 21, 1940, Monday

In the evening, while fixing the stove at our acquaintances' home, we met two Greek girls. On the radio the Prime Minister of Great Britain, C[hurchill], addressed the French nation.
The people of France are becoming more and more disposed toward the Germans.

October 22, 1940, Tuesday

While repairing the stove a bolt broke on us and we had to finish earlier. One of the Greek girls we met yesterday called on those French people twice. It looked as if she were making these visits on purpose.

October 23, 1940, Wednesday

I quarreled over something foolish with Jaś, and so, to avoid further quarrels, I went off to work with a different group. In the evening we were at a little chat at the French family's home. I'm beginning to understand a bit of French.

The radio reports on the meeting of Hitler and General Franco at the Spanish border, as well as of Prime Minister Laval with Hitler. Details are lacking.

October 24, 1940, Thursday

We stage a strike for the second time, because the French don't want to pay us for our work as they promised. They only give a wage of 50 cents a day. We are going through moral torment, because about 6000 soldiers arrived from Syria for demobilization, and most of them come from Alsace and Lorraine. And when they found out we are Poles, they look at us as the worst people in the world. Besides that, every twist of the political situation reflects on us, because here we are, so to speak, a link between France and England and between France and Germany. The French feed us with one hand and want to strangle us with the other. On top of that, the uncertainty of tomorrow weighs badly on our souls.
Truly, our lot is not to be envied.

October 25, Friday

We get word that our departure is well underway. Lieutenant Sztraub told me so, though when it is to be, they don't know. The flame of hope has been rekindled once more.

October 26, 1940, Saturday
In the evening I was at the cinema to see "Suez," a very good film. I saw it in Poland too.
The London bulletin reports that France has signed a peace under which it was to lose a certain part of its northern territories to Germany, Tunis and the region of Nice to Italy, Morocco to Spain, Indochina to Japan.
This turned out to be untrue.

October 27, 1940, Sunday
In the evening I was out walking with Jaś and on the way we stopped in at the cinema. On the way back, while waiting for the tram, we talked with a French policeman. From that conversation I sensed that France was only forced into signing that agreement. Because the policeman winked meaningfully and said that Pétain has a head on his shoulders.

October 28, 1940, Monday
The Italians attacked Greece. Our nerves are beginning to fail us. We already have an example of this in Fiedosiejew, who fell into some kind of frenzy, having also had a bit too much to drink.
He wanted to shoot Lieutenant Strauch, the sergeant-major, accusing them of doing nothing for the company and only playing cards. The reason for this was that he had been at the American Consulate and learned that the lieutenant was helping Poles to leave. He ran around the barracks with a revolver; fortunately the officers cleared out of the barracks, and the sergeant-major hid under a blanket on another bed.
Fiedosiejew even fired the pistol once in the barracks, but into the air.
Slowly we managed to calm him down.
Yet there is nothing so bad that no good comes of it. Because our authorities no longer play cards for hundreds of francs. I am no communist, and I don't mean to say by this that they are wrong to have so much money, because I haven't looked into that, but it hurt me to see the gentlemen playing poker all night long for hundreds of francs while I don't have a single penny. It hurts because after all the same misfortune has struck us all, and we are fighting for one and the same goal, and yet there is no unity. It's as if a hungry man were brought into a dining hall where guests are eating tasty dishes and enjoying themselves, and he can only dream about it. Jasiu offered me 20 [francs], but I refused to accept it, because I hadn't gone to help him for the sake of earning money. He treats me to what he can as it is. He himself has just as little as I do.

October 29, 1940, Tuesday

The sergeant-major gave up his post after yesterday's row and in the afternoon worked with a shovel. Only about half the company is working, the rest are loafing.
Lieutenant Sztraube was writing down the names of those who have some arrears of unpaid wages or salary, and on Thursday this matter is to be settled. As for me, though, I couldn't recall anything owed to me. Except for the war supplement, which still hasn't been paid to anyone. And I remain without hope of getting money from anywhere.

October 30, 1940, Wednesday

The French captain does not agree to the dismissal of the chief, so he remains in his post.
Many comrades brought in forms to fill out concerning the trip to the United States of North America.

October 31, 1940, Thursday

I too filled out such a form, but I have no hope of being able to leave. Besides, we are receiving radio news that Greece is defending herself well, so perhaps some changes in the situation will occur.

November 1, 1940, Friday. All Saints' Day

The patriotic spirit still remains strong within us — proof of this is the singing of the hymn "Boże Coś Polskę" [God Save Poland] in the parish church by a number of Poles, even though not the whole company attends — only those who wish to. The French stand for the entire duration of the singing of this hymn, and no one leaves the church until we finish. Jasio treats me to a beer, even though we had quite a serious argument.

November 2, 1940, Saturday
We are not working today; the chief has ordered only a general cleanup of the barracks. In today's issue of "Wiarus Polski" I found an article titled "A Thought on the Past," which I am keeping as a memento. It quite astonished me, because the Wiarus writes in a German-influenced spirit — after all, it is forced to — yet here it produced such an article.

(Article by J.R., "A Thought on the Past" — clipping from the newspaper Wiarus Polski)

Several of our men went to the Soviet Consulate with a request for permission to travel to Poland. For this, they were apparently received with contempt — humiliated — though we don't know the outcome of that humiliation. Present there were chief Kobyliński, Wolski, and Pryszczupa. After their return, political disputes broke out in the barracks between these three and Mateńka, who laughed right in their faces.

November 3, 1940, Sunday

Terrible boredom, no money. Not for everyone though, since many were demobilized for the second time.

November 4 and 5, Monday and Tuesday

No changes.

November 6, Wednesday

We receive news that Mr. Franklin Roosevelt has been elected President of the United States for the third time.

November 7, 1940, Thursday

We receive our first earned wages for the month of October, totaling 168 francs — that's what I received for all my working days. Days absent from work are deducted, as are days excused by the doctor.

November 8, 1940, Friday

I was in town with Jasio S. and Miśko Bardzki; I bought myself leather gloves and rubber soles, and afterward we all went to the "piggy bank." When I got back I found an old issue of "Match," an illustrated magazine, and in it a photograph in which I recognized Lt. Gulim and commandant Zamkowa in front of the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, carrying the flags of the United States of North America.

November 9, 1940, Saturday

Showing this photo to Lt. Stromek, I learned that he knows Lt. Gulin, since they attended officer school together. In the afternoon I resole my boots. I did not do too bad a job of it.

November 10, 1940, Sunday

It rains all day. In the evening I was at the cinema with Kacperek.

November 11, 1940, Monday

Independence Day, we do not work. Besides that, we honored the holiday by singing the National Anthem.

November 12, 1940, Tuesday

Fiedosiejew beat up a Pole (a priest) over some shady dealings. They all steal together in a bunch and afterward tear into and fight each other.

November 13, 1940, Wednesday

Fiedosiejew was punished for yesterday's brawl with 5 days' arrest.

November 14–19, 1940 During this time we go to the quarries to load gravel onto trucks. We work half a day but get paid for a full day's shift, though this is contracted work (a sham).
The radio news is growing ever more encouraging. Greece is beating Italy. There is just one cloud on the horizon, and that is the trip of Russia's foreign minister to Germany.
In our fellowship life there is tension again, and money is, as always, the bone of contention. Lt. Strauch is suspected of embezzling Red Cross money. At the assembly the lieutenant ordered those who had been saying this to step forward, but no one dared to, nor did the chief, who had reported it to the lieutenant, reveal the names of these people. Relations with the French are very good. These are civilian work supervisors. In Wiarus Polski there is an article about our camp titled "A Bright Island near Marseille." The author describes our conditions in very vivid colors, but if he had stayed longer, he surely would have written a different article.

November 20–28, 1940 Between our barracks the fellows built a beautiful eagle out of stones, along with the inscription "Honor and Fatherland." Almost every passing Frenchman takes an interest in it. It draws the attention of the French to the fact that Poles live here.
For several days in a row we go to the port to unload horses from ships. These are young horses from Africa, which the French are exchanging for old ones in the cavalry regiments, while selling the old ones to the Germans.
A choir has been formed in the company, and we are to sing in church on Sunday, December 1. It's going rather poorly, since there aren't enough volunteers. I also belong to this choir. Some Polish colonel visited the company, doing so on behalf of Gen. Kleberg.

November 29 – December 7, Saturday

On Sunday, November 1, the first Polish church service took place in the parish church in Ste Marthe. It was conducted by a young Polish priest, and judging by his first sermon, not too good a preacher. During the service the choir sang; it turned out not too badly. The church has good acoustics.
Last Saturday I was at the cinema with Jasio S., whose manager is an acquaintance of ours. We had a seat in the box for free. These people keep asking Jasio why I don't come. But I don't feel like it, because I'm tired from work. Every day I go to the gendarmerie barracks; we're digging anti-aircraft trenches there. In the evening there's another choir rehearsal, and I don't feel like going out anywhere at all.
From the Greek front, ever new and ever better
news keeps arriving. The Greeks are advancing.

Last night I had a very strange dream. I dreamed that I was at some
church service where everyone present was lying on bunks or
stretchers. Among those kneeling I remember only Mich. Wolski,
because he was lying next to me. Then the two of us, "Kić" and I, were
walking down some road, and suddenly we hear what sounds like the
drone of an airplane. We look to the side, and there stand two
machine guns, so we run to them to open fire, and in the distance the
silhouette of the plane is visible. But this plane turns into a cross and
falls at my feet, shattering, yet all its pieces are still held together by
Christ, who is upon it. I lifted the cross from the ground, when suddenly
some bird flies down over me — something like a falcon, something
like an eagle, not very large and not entirely white — and grabs hold of
the cross and my hands and carries me upward. Then I fall into some
kind of faint; the whole world grows misty before me, and I have the
feeling that death is approaching. I begin to pray very fervently that I do
not want to leave the world yet, and I am very afraid to leave, because I
have so many sins and it has been so long since I went to confession.
And, strange to say, I feel myself sinking toward the earth, and at last I
stood on it fully conscious, and the bird and the cross vanished, and Lt.
Strączek comes up to me and says: "Why, you fool, don't you want to
go to heaven alive?" And I tell him that I would go to heaven, but I did
not know where it was.
Waking up, for a long moment I could not realize that I was lying on a
bunk. And below, Józio was snoring (for I had been sleeping on the
upper bunk).

December 14–15 Gray life, without major changes. Only the radio news keeps getting
better, since the English have launched an offensive against the Italians
in Egypt.

December 16, Monday
80 men from our company, myself and Jasio among them, were
temporarily detached to the barracks of the 43 p.p.f. to repair roads and
yards. We set out there very reluctantly, but along the way our mood
improved, and we entered the barracks singing. The Frenchman
assigned to lead us (the bespectacled one) was very pleased with this,
which he expressed by telling the chief how the company had lined up,
and how the French officers kept asking who these people were, i.e.
"qu'est-ce que c'est — Allemands?" [what is this — Germans?], making
comical faces as he said it. One must understand that the French are
very afraid of the Germans.
On the second day of work, Sadowski and Komar sneaked off to Ste
Marthe, and we grumbled very loudly against this. Jasio Sadecki
needlessly made a scene about it with Sadowski. In the company,
certain individuals accuse Lt. Sroub of embezzling company funds. But
at the company assembly, all of this turned out to be gossip. Lt. Sroub,
because of this, resigned command of the cash fund, but at the
company's request he stayed on. The chief told him that one of the
corporals reminded him that it takes real skill to hold on to one's post when facing adversity and not only success — and this had its
effect.
A demobilization took place in Ste Marthe, and apparently several of
our men were demobilized, while the chief sat in on the demobilization
and collected a certain percentage from these gentlemen for the
company's holiday fund. In this way 10,800 francs were collected. This
demobilization is clearly a sham — it's as if it were an unofficial way of
handing out relief payments; it's a pity that it's always the same people
doing it, over and over.
From this demobilization money that the chief collected, we received
50 francs each.

On Saturday, December 21, Jasio and I were at the cinema with our
acquaintances for the second time.

December 23, 1940, Monday
At the Polish Red Cross I received a pair of boots and a scarf. And this
happened as if five minutes to twelve, because the very next day, or
rather that night, the first snow fell and covered the ground with a
blanket 20 cm thick — and I would have looked fine indeed in my
windbreaker.

December 24, 1940, Tuesday. Christmas Eve
As if nature wanted to cheer us Poles up and bring this holiday as close
as possible to the holidays celebrated every year in Poland, because on this very day abundant snow fell and a frost set in.
After dinner we all moved to Ste Marthe, since that's where we are to
celebrate the holiday. Jasio immediately went to bring holiday greetings
to the Greek girls, and when he came back he said they were very
pleased and surprised.
Then we offered our greetings to our French acquaintances, and one of
the Greek girls, the elder Marja, came and thanked us for the greetings.
Before leaving we conveyed to her, for the Greek nation, our
congratulations on the victory in Albania. The girl was so moved by this
that she burst into tears. We were invited to these French people's place
for 8 o'clock.
Christmas Eve in our company was splendidly organized by Lt. Straub,
with the help of our French commanders. Even the commandant of the
whole camp helped a great deal. Combined with the Christmas Eve
celebration was the opening of the recreation room, which was
excellently organized and decorated, considering the conditions we find
ourselves in.
General Kleberg honored this celebration with his presence, along with
his wife, as did several French officers, headed by the camp
commandant. Also invited was the camp staff, such as the hostesses
from the French recreation room, the head cook, and various overseers.
In the run-up to Christmas Eve, the wife of our French company
commander showed lively activity aimed at making it more pleasant.
The celebration was opened with a speech by Gen. Kleberg, who
thanked the organizers in both Polish and French, and then delivered a
rousing patriotic speech to us. He recalled the past, and about the future
he said that we look upon it calmly, because everything is on a good path. We only need to persevere. He said that there are Poles more
fortunate than us — those who are in England and in Egypt, because
they can strike blows against the enemy, while we can only receive
them. And that we are, as it were, the reserves of the Commander-in-
Chief, but that we would surely be joined with the main forces.
After the speech the company sang the national anthem, "Jeszcze
Polska" [Poland Is Not Yet Lost]. A series of toasts was raised with
champagne, and several Frenchmen spoke in French. Then, until the end
of Christmas Eve, carols were sung without pause, one after another.
And, as usual, it ended with the tossing of certain personalities up in the
air. The last ones tossed were Lt. Osiecki and Lt. Górski.
We left the Christmas Eve celebration early so as not to be late for the
French. The French observe Christmas Eve as an ordinary supper, and
the actual holiday only takes place on the first day of Christmas. There
is an exchange of gifts.
At eleven o'clock we went to Midnight Mass in Ste Marthe. It lasted a
very long time, and a great many French people went up to receive
Holy Communion. There is a custom here that men receive Holy
Communion on the altar steps, while women receive it at the rail, since
they are forbidden to enter the sanctuary.
At the end of Midnight Mass the Poles sang two verses of "Wśród
Nocnej Ciszy" [Amid the Silence of the Night], which turned out
exceptionally well, and none of the French dared to leave the church,
even though the service had ended before the singing was finished.

December 25, Wednesday. First Day of Christmas
The company received an invitation to the French recreation room for
the holiday. So a few of us, myself and Jasio included, went to see how
they celebrate the holiday.
The hall was decorated with French and Polish flags; in the corner
stood a small Christmas tree, with a table of gifts in front of it.
A speech was given by the camp commandant, and then the raffle
began. Everyone present had a number, which they received upon
entering, and when that number was called, they went up to the table
and received a very modest gift from the hostess.

December 26, 1940, Thursday
I spend the evening in the barrack, at the cinema.

December 27, 1940, Friday
Only in the afternoon do we work, clearing the yard in front of the
barracks; on this occasion we made a snowman.

December 28, 1940, Saturday
Rumors are circulating that the Germans are going to occupy the rest of
France. And that General de Gaulle has announced that the French who
are on his side should demonstrate this to the world by not going out
into the streets for one hour, starting at 12 noon on January 1, 1940.
November 29, 1940, Sunday
The President of the United States gave a speech in which he sharply
attacked the Axis powers.

December 30 and 31, Monday and Tuesday
Terrible boredom. Truly, such idleness under these conditions could
drive a man to madness. Thoughts of the past, of family, of Poland keep
coming to mind, and worst of all are thoughts of the future.

January 1, 1941, New Year
The French somehow celebrate this day more festively than Christmas.
Even the dinner was a genuine New Year's dinner. I, however, spend the
whole day in the barracks. Only on New Year's Eve did I spend time at
the cinema.

January 2 and 3, 1941
No changes at all. In these days especially, cold and hunger plague us,
because they feed us nothing but weeds. It reminds me of the stories old
peasants used to tell, about how in years of famine they ate goosefoot.
We eat the very same thing today. We hold no grudge against the
French, though, since they give the same to their own troops. But in
Ste Marthe, where the Poles are, the cooking is worlds better.

January 4, 1941
A radio bulletin reports that Australian units have captured the Italian
fortress of "Bardia" in Libya, where they took 25 thousand prisoners,
including three generals.

January 5, 1941
From our friends in La Penme we receive a letter with New Year's
wishes, along with a letter they had received from Roman Długosz in
Algiers. He apparently fled to England and the French caught him in
Africa; he's sitting in prison. He's a decent fellow, only he's advancing
rather too quickly, since he's already a sergeant. I left a suitcase with
my things in the barracks in Ste Marthe, since I don't need it here, and
there they stole from me a shirt I had bought in Narbonne. So I took the
suitcase to Marseille.

January 6, 1941. Epiphany
Nothing worth noting.

January 7, 1941.
In the evening I got a little tipsy on wine, so much so that the next day I
was ill, since it doesn't take much for me.
Until January 10 we do nothing. News reached us that the Polish priest
(the one who got beaten up by Fiedosiejew) has returned from Spain, in
order to show the way through Spain.
January 10, 1941
As a result of an agreement among the French command, our quarters
are taken from us, and we are forced to return to Ste Marthe.

January 11, 1941, Saturday
I am designated by Lt. Sroub for departure. By what route is not known. I signed up Jasio S. for it too, but he refused. Saying that he
did not want to travel into the unknown.
We waited for departure until evening, and nothing came of it.

January 12, 1941, Sunday
During Holy Mass in Ste Marthe the Polish priest gave the sermon (he
is not a good preacher). On the way back we met a French lady of our
acquaintance who was going out shopping. We walked her home. And
we stayed at their place hunting birds, without success. Yesterday many
Poles arrived at the camp near Nîmes, among them Sgt.
Gbur.
On January 9, Józek Rej, Szatan, and Kuleszka left for Gardanne to
work in the mine. Once again, our departure did not take place.

January 13, 1941, Monday
We (those who are leaving) were supposed to receive overcoats, but
somehow this changed, and only Zarychta received one — of course
with the help of a lie, as everything nowadays seems to happen. To our
recreation room hostesses have been assigned. There are quite a few
games. A professor gives French language lessons.

January 17, 1941, Saturday
We receive orders to depart. I went to say goodbye to the French and to
acquaintances. They strongly advised me against this trip. That evening
we report to the agreed-upon restaurant, and from there we are once
again led to another one, and there we remained, placed under the care of
sailors who were to transport us to Oran or Algiers. We were not
allowed to take any suitcases, but each of us took a small bundle or a
case despite this. And J. Fatyga took a large suitcase. We didn't even
know about his suitcase, since as the group leader he had gone ahead
first for the money, taking the suitcase with him. When we met up at the
café, he scolded us for having suitcases. "And yet he had the biggest
one of all."
In the evening the sailors led us to the ship, but they did not allow us to
take our suitcases, saying they would carry them themselves.
We had to climb up onto the ship on a rope, since there was a guard
posted at the entrance. They loaded us into some kind of storeroom, and
there we sat for 24 hours without light, food, or drink.
Only afterward, once the ship had sailed out of the port, did one of the
sailors bring us food and drink and open a small window, which gave
us a little light and also served as a latrine, since there was no other
way out.
The whole journey, which we spent sitting in the storeroom, lasted 3
days. The ship sailed along the Spanish coast toward Oran. When we
caught sight of the shores of Africa, the sailor looking after us told us to
hide under a shelf and surrounded us with cork life belts. It was
extremely cramped there, and we sat like that for a couple of hours,
because an Italian commission was inspecting the ship in the port.

continued AFRICA part I Africa January 20, 1941. Oran In the evening the sailors took us into the city disguised as sailors, providing us with their seaman's books and bluffing their way past the policemen with loud talk. They led us to an agreed café, where gentlemen who deal with these matters were already waiting for us. They already knew that we were coming and how much money we had. They told us to wait in Oran for a while, showing us where a cheap hotel was and where a cheap restaurant was. The owner of this restaurant, who is also the waiter, is a former Spanish consul. The food is fairly tasty and clean. The hotel where we are staying is very dirty, and its owner is an Arab. One day he brought us big 16-year-old Arab girls for entertainment. They are very passionate. After a week we changed hotels, because Wicek Zarychta, brother of Sergeant Zarychta of the castle gendarmerie, found lice. In the second hotel we took 3 rooms, because we were counting on Kurniak joining us, who had arrived with the next group. But he was not let out, and I live alone in that room, while the five of us pay our share scrupulously.

Africa, or rather Oran, made a very pleasant impression on us. It is a spotlessly clean, modern city; only the sight of Arabs in national dress reminds one that one is in Africa. The tap water here is very salty, probably because it is drawn from the sea.

As for further travel, the situation looks very bad, because the police catch people after they cross the Moroccan border. So we are waiting for something more certain. Captured gendarmes are treated very politely, and on their word of honor are ordered to return to Oran. Here they are quartered in the barracks.

Those to whom this happened say they were asked why the four of them always travel together, and they supposedly answered that in the Polish army one walks in fours, and that in case of arrest a foursome is needed for bridge. The gendarmes believe so firmly that there are always four traveling together that when they catch 2 or 3, they search the whole town until they find the complete foursome.

February 2, 1941 We set out by bus to Marnia, the last town in Algeria on the Moroccan border. There, after leaving town on the road toward Oujda, a police car came upon us and arrested three of our group, namely Fatyga, Grzegorzewski, and Zarychta. I and Jaś Wołk managed to slip away and throw off the pursuit, which was quite numerous and immediate. We at once changed our route from the road to the railway track. We walked along the track all night without being seen by anyone, passing very close by Oujda, as far as the Sidi Bemuki station. Since it was very, very cold with a strong wind, we took shelter in a culvert under the tracks and fell asleep there. We were woken by the whistle of a locomotive. It was a passenger train heading to Casablanca, but it was already too late. We waited in that culvert for about an hour and went to the station to find out what time the train was. The stationmaster explained to us that the next train would not be until evening, and invited us to sit by the fireplace.

After an hour, two policemen appeared on a motorcycle and arrested us, taking us to Oujda. There they drew up a report and handed us over to the secret police so as not to shame us publicly. There they let us spend the night in the telephone operator's room. On February 4, accompanied by a plainclothes agent, they sent us to Tlemcen. The whole time they treated us very politely, but they would not let us go. In Tlemcen they registered us at the garrison headquarters, and the commander issued us a pass and allowed us to sleep in a hotel, ordering us to report to the railway station the next morning.

In one of the cafés we met a Pole serving in the French army. The next day, accompanied by the plainclothes agent, we traveled to Oran, and there we were joined to those who had met this same fate before us.

We live in a barrack that houses representatives of all three Allies, since there are 4 Englishmen, 3 Frenchmen, and 26 Poles, all held for the same reason. They give us as many passes as we want, and the food is better than in Marseille.

So the situation looks better than in Ste Marthe.

February 8, 1941, Friday

Word reaches us that the police arrested several Poles for stealing jewelry, during the Franco-German war, from some château. The culprits are Lt. Szlawski and Cpl. Soja. They testified that they were talked into it by Capt. Iwanowski, who fled to England. We feel great shame, because the whole town is talking about it.

In the evening I go to the cinema to see a film that a French studio produced in honor of the Polish heroine Emilia Plater. A fairly good film, though not strictly accurate historically.

February 9, 1941, Saturday, and February 10, Sunday

I play cards; although I won only 30 francs, everyone looks at me askance.

One of the Arabs got into a drunken fight with an Englishman; the Poles helped the Englishman, but the next day the Arab came and apologized to the Englishman and the Poles.

February 10, Monday

Lt. Polański brings us news that the English terribly bombed Genoa in Italy.

While in town I bought myself a chess set; they are very hard to find, because the French are a very lazy nation and don't feel like playing a game where one has to think a little. On my way back from town I witnessed a scene the Germans could be proud of. Namely, two imprisoned Germans, who had been traveling to England for sabotage work by the same route as us, were being transported somewhere by car, guarded by 8 armed soldiers and two French NCOs — the Germans were therefore in chains. And the French soldiers looked as if they were very afraid, while the Germans laughed scornfully.

February 11, 1941, Tuesday

A new group arrived from Marseille, about 10 men; among them is Karwaciński.

February 12, 1941, Wednesday

Boredom. I don't want to go into town because I have no money, and just wandering the streets is something I really dislike. In the evening I only went with Fatyga for dinner at a second-rate little restaurant.

February 13, 1941, Thursday

The group that included Kuźmiak was detained in Morocco and brought to Oran; they are with us now. One of the Englishmen has disappeared.

February 14 – Friday
Nothing of importance.

February 15, Saturday

In the evening 29 Poles arrived by ship from Marseille, whom the police had arrested in the port and brought to us.

This makes me think that this is already being done in agreement with the French, because they expect the rest of France to be occupied by the Germans. One man from this group was suspected of espionage by some Frenchman, who claimed to have seen him in Lyon in a German uniform. He was arrested.

February 16, Sunday

Cierkosz arrived with the new group, and today, drunk, he already got into a row with Lt. Kluz. In the evening I had a beer with Zelek and Dziadkowiec, who had also just arrived.

February 17, Monday

The French inform us that on Thursday or Friday we will go to a camp, because the Italian-German commission demanded it. They also arrested one of the Poles who had too loose a tongue and somewhat communist views.

February 18, Tuesday

Word reaches us that the English bombed Kraków and the area around Katowice. Another group of about 25 men arrived, including Sgt. Gbur and Czarnota.

February 19, Wednesday

Again a new group arrived, also about 25 men, and 10 Poles, 4 Englishmen, and 1 Dutchman were released from custody, having been held there for about a month. The Englishmen and the Dutchman know how to sing the march "Polonia."

February 20, 1941, Thursday

After dinner Wicek Zarychta and I went for a walk in the surrounding mountains. By accident we wandered onto military grounds, and we had to flee as fast as possible so as not to be taken for spies and locked up. The one who had been suspected of being German was released.

In the evening we were not allowed to leave the barracks despite having passes. The reason was probably that the English fleet was cruising near the port, and indeed several lights could be seen out at sea.

February 21, 1941, Friday

After dinner we go to the station in fours and load onto the train; two carriages are specially reserved, one second class and the other third class, and we travel to Saïda, about 200 km south of Oran. We traveled all night.

February 22, 1941, Saturday

At 7 o'clock in the morning we get off at Saïda, eat breakfast in town, and go to the barracks. We arrive just as the flag is being raised. The garrison commander, Major Fiseau, received us very politely. The barracks are fairly nice, sleeping is comfortable, and the food is very good. Strict rules apply here — one may go into town only between 6 and 9 in the evening. The town itself is very small but clean. The temperature is much lower than in Oran, since we are at an altitude of about 1000 m above sea level. At night, even under three blankets and a sheet, one could freeze.
First order. We are named the First African Company. Discipline is announced.

February 23, 1941, Sunday

Quite early we go to the service in fours, at which we do not sing. Word reaches us that the English have landed at Ostend and that Marshal Śmigły-Rydz has taken command of the Polish army in Egypt. We spend the rest of the day wandering around Saïda.

February 24, 1941, Monday

We begin the day with prayer and exercise. The company is already fairly well coordinated.
(2 photos) Zarychta Wincenty; Grzegorzewski Mieczysław

February 25, 1941, Tuesday

On returning from morning exercise we put on a parade for the garrison commander, with which he was very pleased. Major Brzozowski arrived, bringing us a radio and a chess set, and informed us about the political situation and about our task. In the evening the Jewish men who are in the labor company put on a concert for us. It turned out fairly well. Yesterday several men were punished for returning late from town; among them was Dzikowski, who behaved rudely toward the company commander and was therefore punished with detention. He refused to go, but under pressure from the company he went. For now, internal relations are better than in Ste Marthe.

February 26, 1941, Wednesday

Our daily activities are arranged as follows: in the morning, exercise and a French lesson; in the afternoon, a walk. Within this, two hours a week are set aside for tidying up a monument far outside town, in honor of the legionnaires. I belong to the choir, which rehearses every afternoon in order to sing in church on Sunday.

In the evening I went for a walk with Tadek Mazur, and he lent me 50 francs without my asking.

February 27, 1941, Thursday

The days are very sunny, but the temperature is very changeable, since at noon it is hot and at night it is cold.

February 28, 1941, Friday

The company was divided into platoons. Długosz Roman is listed as sergeant, deputy platoon commander. From Oran 12 Poles arrived, among them Dziudzik.

In the evening, together with Cpl. Szybka, we struck up an acquaintance with several Germans. Among them is a painter, Werner, some doctor, and a third one I don't know about. I drank about four glasses of wine and was already in good spirits, and at night I couldn't sleep.

March 1, 1941, Saturday

A Red Cross delegate arrived at the camp, and at his instruction the chief made a list of what clothing each man was missing.
(2 photos) Kosiński Wiktor; Dziadkowiec Jan March 2, 1941, Sunday
For the Poles a French priest celebrated a special Holy Mass at which the choir sang, but the singing did not turn out as well as we had hoped. In the afternoon I went for a walk in a ravine through which a stream flows. It is very beautiful there, and it serves the residents of Saïda as a place for holiday relaxation.

March 3, 1941, Monday
Several (officer cadets?), such as a certain Peske, Jałowy, and a few others, got drunk and caused a disturbance. For which someone was arrested and held at the barracks guardhouse. Cadet Peszke burst into the barracks hall and tried to rouse the company to go and intervene, but instead he only got a few kicks and some nasty blows for his trouble.
The Germans marched into Bulgaria.

March 4, 1941, Tuesday
The company voiced its opinions about yesterday's disturbance, but because the company commander, Lt. Piątkowski, was absent, the matter was not finally settled. The company put forward a proposal to send the cadets away to an officers' camp, or to suspend military ranks for the duration of their stay, so that only men respected by everyone would be group commanders.

Tadek Mazur lent me 150 francs for trousers. And so in the evening I bought trousers, a shirt, and a tie, quite cheaply too, considering these times.
Karwaciński wanted to lend me 50 francs, but I didn't want to take it, explaining that I don't need it right now, even though I don't have any, and that later I would have nothing to pay him back with — nevertheless, he gave me 50 francs.

March 5, 1941, Wednesday
In the morning we learned that in town Poles (NCOs and cadets) had again caused a scene, this time in a brothel. Because of this the garrison commander issued a ban on leaving the barracks until further notice. On top of that, the company commander is not here, and the matter is still unresolved. One of the participants in the battle for Narvik (Norway) gave a talk today on how Narvik was taken. In the evening there was another row at the "Foyer du Soldat" because the French would not give several drunken Poles more beer. Everything looks as if there were a fifth column among us.

March 6, 1941, Thursday
We received a letter from Ste Marthe, from Czerwiec, saying that they are behind wire there and that the organizers of the journey have been arrested. Czerwiec asks whether things are good for us here and whether it's worth coming here. I also have regards from Sadecki. At the afternoon assembly, tank and car drivers, foremen, and various (metal) craftsmen were listed. The lieutenant explained that the French authorities had ordered this, but it seems that this was actually for our own [Polish] authorities.

In the evening we sit in the barracks because there is confinement to barracks, but the cadets have passes and even laugh about it shamelessly.

March 7, 1941, Friday
Nothing new.

March 8, 1941, Saturday
The barracks confinement was lifted.

March 9, 1941, Sunday
The choir made a terrible mess of one hymn (the Sanctus) at Mass in church. But despite this, the company commander thanked us for the promotion of Polishness. And the choir was invited to sing at the French Holy Mass on Palm Sunday.

March 10, 1941, Monday
Word reaches us that the U.S. Senate passed authorization for President Roosevelt regarding the loan of war equipment to England.

March 11, 1941, Tuesday
Lt. Piątkowski returned and briefed us on the situation; the financial situation in particular looks very bad. For now we are to receive 50 centimes a day.

March 12, 1941, Wednesday
Some French officer arrived and is drawing up personnel lists; we don't know what for — perhaps our authorities know but are keeping quiet. Presumably this is French counterintelligence looking for German saboteurs among us.

March 13, 1941, Thursday
A day without any events.

March 14, Friday
A new group of Poles arrived, about 30 men, among them Sergeant Gbur and Czarnota, Corporals Wołk J., Słowiarski (Zagłoba), and a few others from Ste Marthe. There are new assignments, and once again my platoon commander is Sergeant Gbur.

March 15, 1941, Saturday
In the morning, a hunt for bedbugs, of which there are plenty. After dinner, choir rehearsal in church, which went well.

March 16, 1941, Sunday
At the Polish Holy Mass there were a great many French people present, and the choir sang exceptionally well; besides the choir, Cadet Kwaśniak sang a solo, and at the beginning of the Mass ("Kiedy ranne") and at the end "Boże coś Polskę" everyone sang, accompanied by the organ, played by a Frenchwoman (the mayor's daughter).

After Mass the company returned to the barracks singing. In the afternoon a football match took place between our team and the Saïda team. The match was very interesting, and our side played well despite losing 4–3 in favor of Saïda. In the evening Cierkosz, being tipsy, insulted Fatyga by saying that he was no platoon sergeant.

March 17, 1941, Monday
A report was made, since Fatyga reported Cierkosz for yesterday's incident. Cierkosz was punished with 5 days' detention for improper conduct.

March 18, 1941, Tuesday
Vice-Consul Rzepecki arrived from Oran; apparently he was supposed to give some money to certain people, but I don't know if anyone actually received any.

March 19, 1941, Wednesday
The company numbers over 190 men, but today only about 20 remained for activities, so the lieutenant dismissed even that remainder. I don't even know where these people are hiding out, since they are nowhere to be seen.
March 20, 1941, Thursday
Aspirant Jałowy, the one who deserted from the company to Oran, has a bandaged head, and I heard him say himself that blood poisoning had set in. He supposedly got the wound from being hit by a car while drunk, at least that's what everyone claims. He came back from Oran and was put in the guardhouse for a few days. Aspirant Złotnicki was with him.
In the afternoon we were issued underwear, two sets each, and boots — though I have no boots for now. In the evening 18 Poles arrived from Oran, among them Jezierski Kaziu and Laszkiewicz Stefan, and 2 officers who apparently gave up their pay and the officers' camp to come to our camp.

March 21, 1941, Friday
Lice were found in the underwear issued yesterday, so the drawers were collected again for disinfection. The shirts were new, so they were clean.

March 22, 1941, Saturday
All day we had work shelling peas for tomorrow. Our platoon is on duty.

March 23, 1941, Sunday
Only half the men turn out for the daily roll call, but almost everyone goes to church, and they show discipline, which makes good propaganda for Poland. More and more Frenchmen attend the Polish Mass. The singing went well, and after Mass we returned to the barracks in formation of four, singing, with a whole crowd of boys behind us. In the afternoon, a football match against the team of 1st Saïda, which is the champion of North Africa. We lost 3–1, but the game was interesting.

March 24, 1941, Monday
It rains all day.

March 25, Tuesday
Yugoslavia signed its accession to the Rome-Berlin Axis.
The Yugoslav people are apparently outraged at their government. The weather is the same as yesterday.

March 26, 1941, Wednesday
In the evening Fiedosiejew treats us to an omelet.

March 27, 1941, Thursday
We receive relief funds from the Red Cross, everyone gets 40 francs. A very pleasant surprise was brought by the evening news bulletin, which reports a bloodless revolution in Yugoslavia. King Peter has taken power, Regent Paul has fled, and the ministers who signed the agreement with the Germans have been arrested. In Eritrea the English captured the city of Keren, and in Abyssinia the city of Harar and one other smaller town. The listeners applauded the news broadcast and great joy filled everyone; I felt several years younger, and instead of sleeping we carried on discussions about it late into the night.

Today Lt. Piątkowski, commander of our company, carried out a verification of the aspirants; officially we have no news of it, but rumors are going around that he thoroughly weeded them out — apparently there are only 5 left instead of 24. The NCOs are also said to be checked. And a good thing too, since 80 percent had promoted themselves. I won't name names here, since such dirty laundry is better kept quiet, but you could count on your fingers those who actually hold their ranks properly.

March 28, Friday
They are preparing a record of each of us. An officer arrived from Oran for this purpose. I am helping with the writing.

March 29, 1941, Saturday
Finishing the records. This week I resigned from the choir because of constant quarrels.

March 30, 1941, Sunday
As every Sunday, a service, and after dinner our sports team plays a friendly match against Saïda's reserve team and wins 3–2, one goal for Saïda being an own goal. Two Frenchmen bet each other 550 francs on the outcome of this match, and so our players each got 50 francs.

March 31, 1941, Monday
Our football team receives kits from the Red Cross in Algiers, and besides that there is a net and a ball for volleyball.

April 1, 1941, Tuesday, April
Wake-up is half an hour earlier now, that is at 6:30. A library has been given over for our use; there are quite a few Polish books here.

April 2, 1941, Tuesday
Mietek Grzegorzewski made a bet with Cierkosz for 20 francs that he would drink 3 liters of wine, not vomit, and after coming into the hall not say a word to anyone. But he lost the bet on both counts, and we got a lot of laughs out of him. Kuźniak was punished with detention because he refused to go to a lecture.

April 3, 1941, Wednesday
The radio news reports the death of the Hungarian foreign minister, Count Teleki, who committed suicide, the reason being that the Germans were forcing Hungary to move against Yugoslavia, with which Hungary had signed a treaty.

April 4, 1941, Thursday
Nothing worth noting.

April 5, 1941, Friday
Like a bolt from the blue comes the news that the Germans have taken Benghazi in Libya from the English.

April 6, 1941, Saturday
Today I went to confession. It was a general confession, since almost no one knows French. This was my first confession of this kind. I found it a bit strange, and somehow it seemed to me that it wasn't valid, but later I made my peace with it.
The company commander, Lt. Piątkowski, again punished the whole company with barracks confinement because apparently two men were late and gave false names at the guardhouse.

April 7, 1941, Palm Sunday
The company goes up to Holy Communion, and with us a great many Frenchwomen. There are very many French people at our Holy Mass. The camp was visited by some major (Wysoczański), apparently the senior Polish officer in North Africa. He held a briefing with us in which he noted that we might yet visit many more countries. In the afternoon, a football match against the team we played last Sunday, this time losing 2–1. Our team's play was beneath criticism; half the audience left already at halftime.
The radio news reports:
The Germans have attacked Yugoslavia and Greece.
General Sikorski signed an agreement in Canada on forming a Polish army in Canada and left for Washington. The English took Addis Ababa in Abyssinia.
April 8, 1941, Monday, and April 9, 1941, Tuesday Very scant news from the Balkan front seems to confirm the German advance. The Yugoslavs were said to have entered Albania.

April 10, 1941, Wednesday

The radio was sent back to Oran to be exchanged for a better one. French newspapers report the capture by the English of Massawa, an Italian port in Eritrea.

In the barracks we are doing holiday cleaning, whitewashing the rooms, burning out bedbugs, and so on.

April 11, 1941, Thursday

It is very beautiful in the ravine by the little river; whenever I have a free moment I spend time there. There are countless flowers there, from which we make bouquets to decorate the rooms. We receive relief funds from the Red Cross totaling 37 francs, and besides that I got 10 francs for doing the record-keeping a few days ago.

April 12, 1941, Good Friday

It did not pass in accordance with Catholic principles but rather with drinking and brawling. Jasiński, drunk, struck Aspirant Krowiński in the face, probably because Krowiński was simply the aspirant most hated by everyone. Word of this incident, which happened at a nearby café, spread like wildfire and reached just as quickly the garrison commander, who, in the company of three sentries, came to arrest the man responsible for the act. It brought us great shame; the company commander, Lt. Piątkowski, was terribly outraged and at the briefing presented the matter as one in which we ourselves were wresting authority from his hands, and that if things went on this way, the French would end up ruling themselves.

After this briefing, instead of calming down, several more got drunk and again wanted to beat up the aspirants; among the ringleaders were Kula and Dzikowski, the latter recently holding a grudge against Lt. Raczyński for having him locked up, and for this reason spreading rumors about the lieutenant frequenting a brothel very often and paying too generously, spoiling the price for others. This whole affair upset me very much and filled me with bitterness, and the thought forces itself on me, whether I want it or not, that Poles do not know how to govern themselves and that only a firm hand can hold this nation together.

April 12, 1941, Saturday

The radio set came back the same one, since there was no better one in Oran. The town hall of Saïda issued ration cards to the Poles (those who wanted them), and I took advantage of this and bought myself real Palmolive soap — quite a triumph. Mazur receives a letter from Czerwiec in which he reports that quartermaster Kobyliński, before leaving, stole 12,000 company francs and fled to Spain, but apparently they caught him there and he is now sitting in Madrid.

April 13, 1941, Easter Sunday

At our Holy Mass a mixed choir sang (Poles with Frenchwomen) and it turned out very beautifully. Afterward the choir was to sing at the French High Mass. At the Polish Mass there were far more French people than at the French High Mass. After the service the major, the senior Polish commander in Africa, held a briefing with the company on the subject of Friday's incident, and said he would try to have the culprits removed from our ranks so as not to spoil our reputation. Afterward there was an Easter blessing-of-food gathering, attended by the garrison commander, Maj. Fuzeau, with his wife and daughter, the parish priest, two French officers, and Polish legionnaires and several others of various nationalities. For the holiday, officers from Mascara (Poles) also came to visit us. The dining hall was beautifully decorated with Polish and French flags, and in the middle a large, beautiful eagle. There were many wildflowers on the tables. Lt. Piątkowski was the first to speak. He thanked everyone for coming and offered his wishes. The garrison commander replied in French. The Easter meal itself was very tasty but not overly abundant, so that we finished long before the guests did and began entertaining them with song. We sang the anthems of both nations and several Polish songs. One of our comrades, who knows a little French and was somewhat tipsy, launched into a speech, spouting terrible nonsense, saying among other things: "Nous avons jednego gen. Sikorskiego, Wous avez jednego marszałka Petaina" (we have one General Sikorski, you have one Marshal Petain). His comrades laughed about him all day. In closing, Lt. Raczyński thanked the parish priest for his cooperation with the Poles.

Satisfaction was visible on the guests' faces as they left the gathering.

In the afternoon there was a football match, ending in victory for our players, 2–0.

And in the evening there was a bonfire gathering in the ravine, the first of its kind; the garrison commander was invited with his family, along with many uninvited Frenchmen drawn in by our singing. The bonfire gathering did not turn out too well, the reason being that most people were drunk, starting from the top ranks down.

April 14, 1941, Monday, second day of the holiday

Rain falls with small breaks; we were not at church and everyone stayed in the barracks. But it did not pass without trouble, because again one of those who had beaten Aspirant Krowiński on Friday picked a fight with Dziadkowiec and tore his shirt, but when Dziadkowicz punched him in the mouth, he lost consciousness for a few minutes.

News from the Balkans is bad.

April 15, 1941, Tuesday

We rest after the holidays since it pours rain all day.

April 16, Wednesday

Weather same as yesterday. The aspirants left for the officers' camp at Mascara. Only four remained, those who were needed at the camp.

April 17, 1941, Thursday

The weather improved considerably, so that we could go for a walk. News from the Balkans and from Libya is very bad. The Germans have already entered Egyptian territory. The soap ration cards, which the town hall had issued to us by mistake, were taken back from us.

April 18, 1941, Friday

This evening was marked by a triumph of Polish song, sung by the company choir at a concert whose proceeds were earmarked for French national defense. The concert was held in the hall of the Pallace cinema and drew a large crowd of guests; the hall was packed. This concert, dull at first, was livened up only by our choir. The mere announcement of it already drew a storm of applause.

And when one of our comrades first explained in French the content of the song the choir was about to sing, such silence fell over the hall that you could have heard a fly go by, so intently did they listen to him. It should be noted here that the audience had behaved rather noisily during the earlier parts of the program. The curtain rose, and the first song to flow out was "Rozwiń swe skrzydła białe" (Unfurl your white wings). The beautiful melody, along with the conductor's movements, which made an impression both to the ear and to the eye, as though he were playing some great and mighty instrument, so captivated the audience that when they finished, the applause rang out with redoubled force. Next they sang "Flisacy" (The Raftsmen) and "Warszawianka" (The Warsaw Song), with ever-growing enthusiasm among the diverse audience, for it lacked neither dignitaries nor the educated nor dirty Arabs, and these songs moved them all alike.

The curtain fell, and the explaining comrade appeared again, announcing a polka and explaining its origin. This piece was played on the flute by Cpl. Szpetnar Błażej (the choir's conductor). Lt. Kowalski accompanied on the piano, and in places four choir members sang along: "Żeń się Jasiu, żeń się" (Marry, Jasiu, marry). It began with whistling on fingers, and soon the lively Polish melody cheered the audience so much that long after it ended the crowd could not settle down, howling for an encore. But the curtain did not rise again, since it would have been a shame to spoil the first impression, and an intermission was announced.

As I was leaving the hall, some Arab walked behind me, whistling the polka he had just heard. On the stairs leading down from the balcony I saw the garrison commander shaking hands with our company doctor; I gathered he was offering congratulations. After the intermission, much interest was aroused by a competition of amateur singers, and since all of them were residents of Saïda, well known to the audience from everyday life, that explains the interest.

The troop of Muslim scouts represented themselves very well through their singing and their bearing. To close, the scouts sang the Marseillaise accompanied by the symphony orchestra. Walking back from the concert I pondered what had been the best part of it, and I concluded it was our choir, setting aside my own impressions, which might have been one-sided, but taking into account the audience's behavior.

April 19, 1941, Saturday

We are still living under the impression of yesterday's concert, and if not for the ever-worsening news from the Balkans and Libya, this day could be counted among the happy ones. Szpetnar wanted to give the choir a rest and announced that tomorrow they would not sing in church, but the company threatened him that they would not go to church at all, not wanting to feel ashamed in front of the French.

April 20, 1941, Sunday

And it's a good thing the choir sang, because there were crowds of Frenchmen and Frenchwomen who, it seems, come to church specially to hear the singing. The priest is said to have remarked that since the Poles arrived, over 100 French people have returned to the church. Since yesterday the weather has been beautiful, and we take advantage of it to go for walks and sunbathe in the ravine, where it is even more beautiful and full of flowers. On this occasion I took a few photographs of myself.

April 21, 1941, Monday

Karwaciński received a letter from Poland in which they report that word about me, Jędrzejewski, was passed on in Opatów. The labor camp wanted to put us in uniform, but we refused to accept them.

April 22, Tuesday

Nothing new.

April 23, 1941, Wednesday

It is the name-day of our company commander, Lt. Jerzy Piątkowski. On this day, as though for a name-day present, the garrison commander, Maj. Fuzeau, informed Lt. P. that by order of the French general, the Poles in Saïda are to be regarded as friends from yesterday and as friends tomorrow, and cannot be treated as laborers of a labor camp. This matter had come to a head after Monday's refusal to accept the uniforms. The commander of the labor camp had submitted a report on this to the general, asking how he was to treat the Poles, and received the answer that Maj. Fuzeau conveyed to Lt. P. On account of this same name-day, an amnesty was announced in orders for all those punished up to now.

April 24, 1941, Thursday

Several men signed up for work in the mine, and their papers were sent off to Oran, and many comrades wanted to follow their example, but our officers are holding them back from this. I am not thinking of it yet myself, since it wasn't for the sake of work that I came here, but I may be forced to, because my clothes are already very worn out and there will soon be nothing to wear. They promise us denim overalls, but before that happens my clothes will have fallen apart completely. I received a letter from Janek Sadecki, in which among other things he reports that group 804 is to be split up for work in the forest and on farms.

April 25, 1941, Friday

A day of mourning on account of the death of the French general, commander of the Foreign Legion. The flag is raised at half-mast, and the garrison commander, after raising it, read out the deceased's biography and merits. Rain fell during this ceremony.

April 26, 1941, Saturday

As a result of telegrams sent to Algiers saying the company was on the verge of falling apart, a Red Cross delegate arrived today, familiarized himself with the situation, and promised a pay raise. Apparently pay is now to be according to military rank. There is discontent in the company over this.

April 27, 1941, Sunday

The Red Cross delegate was present at Mass and was very moved by the Polish character of the service. As a reward he allotted 15 francs each to the choir members. The afternoon was marked by a terrible thrashing of our footballers, losing 6–1 to Saïda. Even so, the play was fine.

April 28, 1941, Monday

In the afternoon there was a practice parade for the legion's anniversary celebrations in Saïda. Poles also took part in it; the garrison commander was pleased with us.

April 29, 1941, Tuesday

Lt. Piątkowski held a company briefing on the subject of the Red Cross delegate's visit. We were promised a great deal in kind, and daily income, together with pay, is to amount to 4 francs a day, with payment to be made weekly. He also drew attention to the spreading tall tales, referring mainly to the matter of Mr. Rzepecki from Oran. I found in "Wiarus Polski" (The Polish Veteran) some words of wisdom applicable to this matter, so I am pasting them in as a keepsake. Many comrades admit that during our military service in France we did not have it as good as here.

Newspaper clipping:
A good man draws moral benefit even from evil; a wicked man finds fault even in what is good. L.

April 30, 1941, Wednesday, Foreign Legion Day

It is a day observed very solemnly, much like regimental holidays in Poland. It is celebrated in memory of the battle in Mexico. Wake-up was half an hour earlier, followed by the solemn raising of the standard, a reading by the garrison commander of an account of that battle, the decoration of distinguished legionnaires, then a parade in which the Poles also took part, and, at the garrison commander's request, singing after the parade. After the parade, a service at which our choir sang, and immediately afterward the laying of wreaths at the legionnaires' monument. The Poles also laid a wreath, and the choir sang the Marseillaise.

Dinner was very abundant and tasty, plenty to drink, cigarettes, and one cigar each. After dinner we receive relief funds from the Red Cross totaling 67.50 francs. In the evening the choir went out to a neighboring town to
sing, and I went with Kosiński to the cinema to see the film "The Tsar's Courier"
I remember that film from my childhood years. Fiedosiejew and Cierkosz again, while
drunk, started a brawl.

May 1, 1941, Thursday   Name day of Marshal Pétain

There are parades in the town but we do not take part in them,
we only help eat the festive dinner. After dinner a match
ended in a 2-2 draw, though I was not there,
I was strolling through the ravine and around town.

May 2, 1941, Friday

Since Tuesday we have had no news at all because the radio
set is broken and it will not soon be possible to repair it.
Our food rations were cut by half the wine and the entire dessert, but
the Red Cross added 200 grams of bread per person. With Wicek Zarychta
and Dziadkowiec I had an omelet and ice cream at a Spanish woman's place, we spent
a very pleasant time there. In the evening we go as a whole company to the cinema
for free, and with us were the legionnaires and the travailleurs.

May 3, 1941, Saturday, Constitution Day

Celebrated with a religious service. News reaches us of the
outbreak of an uprising in Iraq against England.

May 4, 1941, Sunday

Like all previous ones, with a match played between
our team and the star (Arabs) team, 2-1 in our favor. Wicek
Zarychta and Dziadkowicz treat us to supper and we are in good spirits.

May 5, 1941, Monday

The radio was repaired, which brought us comforting news. All
evening I play "kupki" [a dice/card game] and won quite a lot. The company commander
awarded certain items of clothing to some of the most needy, and
again not everyone is satisfied. With Zarychta and Dziadkowiec we again
feast.

(photograph)
Saïda, April 30, 1941. The Polish delegation lays wreaths at the legionnaires' monument.

May 6, 1941, Tuesday

Kosiński received some position in the officers' casino.

May 7, 1941, Wednesday

For several days it has been cold and raining, and everyone asks
themselves where those African heatwaves are, since in Poland May was warmer.
The company has switched to civilian food supply, so now they buy for us
using ration cards. Many of my comrades have had certain items cut from their rations; I had
oil cut from mine, and because of this I had a bit of unpleasantness. I gave the oil
to Kosiński, and what he did with it I do not know, but that was still before
the announcement that we would be eating on ration cards. In the afternoon we are issued boots, those who did not have them, but old ones, and new blue drill uniforms. In the hall there is again a quarrel
between Dziadkowiec and Tabaczek about keeping duty watch, because
Dziadkowicz, as the hall commandant, had not kept duty watch up to now. It is not
known what the company commander's position is on the matter.

May 8, 1941, Thursday, and May 9, 1941, Friday
Boredom, bad weather.

May 10, 1941, Saturday

These bedding inspections are already tiresome. We received relief funds
from the Red Cross, 30 francs each, and on the strength of that we drank a little. Cierkosz has
for several days been walking around drunk because he works in the food storehouse and has
something to drink, and this evening he caused some kind of brawl in town and the
police had him locked up in the military guardhouse.

May 11, 1941, Sunday, France's national holiday in honor of Joan of Arc

We do not take part in this holiday except for a few
exceptions. At our Mass there were fewer Frenchmen than usual because they have
their own solemn service right after ours. The choir badly bungled one
hymn. At the solemn French Mass our choir also sang together with
the French. After the service, a solemn laying of wreaths at
the monument to the dead of 1914-1918, and a parade. In the afternoon, at the
municipal stadium, sporting competitions were held, such as gymnastics, jumping, races,
basketball and football; this lasted 5 hours and there were many people because the weather
held up. Four Poles played a match on the legionnaires' team
against Saida and lost 4-2, though the game was good. I have duty as the company's duty NCO, yet I was at the stadium for
the whole competition. Mietek Grzegorzewski, drunk, got into a brawl with
Fatyga, during which he tore up two shirts.

May 12, 1941, Monday

Today is the anniversary of the death of Marshal Piłsudski. The evening
news reports Hess's flight from Germany.

(2 photographs)

Saida, April 1941. We are leaving the barracks, led by Lt. Piątkowski, first

May 13, 1941, Tuesday

The morning news reports that Rudolf Hess landed in a fighter plane
in England (Scotland). This took place on May 10, 1941. Our officers
are making efforts to organize a transport column across the Sahara, but
they want first to make an agreement and find out about the conditions in which
the work will be done there. Many, myself included, have grown interested in this kind
of work because our authorities give no hope of a quick departure.

May 14, 1941, Wednesday

From Stawiarski, who left to work in the mine, we
received a letter in which he complains terribly that he went there, because he earns
poorly and the conditions in which he works are harsh, and when he demanded a
raise he was locked up, and now he asks to be pulled out of these troubles. Our officers have taken up efforts in this matter. Since yesterday,
for absence from roll call they deduct a day's Red Cross allowance.

May 15, 1941, Thursday

Skrzypek and Fabisiak went to Oran for the court hearing of
Lt. Szlawski and Cpl. Soja; both were acquitted. Kula again
got into a brawl with Wawrzyszek.

May 16, 1941, Friday

Cpl. Soja arrived at the camp and has been assigned to our
hall. Rumors are going around that France has ceded bases in Syria, but
some claim it was to the Germans and others to the English; for certain we know nothing
because the radio is broken again.

May 17, 1941, Saturday

The French newspapers report that the English bombed
air bases in Syria because German aircraft were stationed there.
We receive 25 francs each from the Red Cross. The French conduct a medical
inspection.

May 18, 1941, Sunday

We had the service at 10 o'clock because the children had their
First Holy Communion, and that is why it was changed for us, but there were more
people in the church. In the afternoon an unfinished match was held with Saida
because a legionnaire got into a fight with a player from Saida over a foul;
the game was very good despite our loss.

May 19, 1941, Monday
Kula sits in the guardhouse for a second day. Some French general made an inspection
of the garrison but did not enter our area, and the company went out for a walk.

May 20, 1941, Tuesday

For the first time we bathe in the pool, it is very good. The pool
has been made available for our use on Tuesdays and Fridays before noon.

May 21, 1941, Wednesday

Our choir and the sports group departed for Maskara for a sports
festival.

May 22, 1941, Thursday

The weather is nasty, cold and rainy, and so the sports festival in
Saida and Maskara was postponed to Sunday, and those who went to Maskara
are to return on Monday. I was on the "sixteen" [duty shift]. 
The radio reports that for several days fighting has been going on on Crete and that the Germans
are achieving successes.

May 23, 1941, Friday

The radio reports that the English sank 30 German-Italian ships
that were sailing to Crete.

May 24, 1941, Saturday

The radio again reports the sinking of the English battleship Hood
by the German battleship Bismarck.

May 25, 1941, Sunday

Very boring. Holy Mass with no singing at all, which greatly
surprised the priest and the French. We were given something bad to
eat, because everyone, legionnaires and Poles alike, is suffering from stomach trouble.

May 26, 1941, Monday

Our sports team returns from Maskara bringing back
many victories. They lost the football match 2-4 and
basketball by a wide margin, but the other events such as races and jumps
they won, and volleyball too they won by a very wide margin. The choir
sang in the church twice during the Mass for fallen Polish
soldiers. The parish priest of Maskara delivered an uplifting sermon and
devoted many words of recognition to the Poles and the choir. The choir also sang at
the stadium during halftime and at a glass of wine in the restaurant
where the cream of Maskara society gathered, headed by the prefect.
700 francs were collected there for prisoners of war.

May 27, 1941, Tuesday

The evening news reports the sinking by the English of the
German battleship Bismarck. France is sending many troops to
Morocco, and from Saida many are also leaving today. One of our comrades received a
letter from Marseille in which the writer complains bitterly that there are constant
quarrels and brawls there that end in the guardhouse or a penal camp.
Those who returned from Tunis were sent, chained in shackles, to a concentration
camp. Here our authorities are again making moves with passports, but we already view this with indifference, because it has let us down so many times, but
we do what we can because perhaps it will finally work out. Rumors are going around that we are to
be sent back to Poland. Everyone talks only about the trans-Saharan
transport link as a way to get closer to the borders of Nigeria.

May 28, 1941, Wednesday

Holy Mass for the souls of the Poles who fell in Norway. For several
days the weather has been beautiful, so we sunbathe all day because instead of duties we have walks
in the morning and afternoon. The fields are becoming emptier and emptier
because the harvest is in full swing.

May 29, 1941, Thursday
No changes.

May 30, 1941, Friday

Lt. Piątkowski returned from Algiers and announced that he does not have
happy news.

May 31, 1941, Saturday

At the briefing Lt. Piątkowski announced that the Polish Red Cross
has been dissolved and that the German Red Cross is to take over
our care, but only from the first of July. That this week we are to receive
helmets, glasses, and shirts.

June 1, 1941, Sunday

As usual, with a service at which the choir sang despite having
threatened not to sing. At noon Lt. Kaczyński informed
us that the English left Crete. And that an order had come that Poles could
be used for farm work in the vicinity of Saida. Many are
against this, but Lt. Kaczyński explains that the French want to hide us
from the German-Italian commission and that is why they want to disperse us, while those
who do not want to go are to be forcibly transported to the south,
where the commission's reach does not extend, but where the climate conditions
are poor. I decided to go to work, and with me 50 percent
of our platoon, while the rest are still hesitating. In the afternoon Fiedosiejew,
drunk, caused a brawl but got such a beating that he soiled
his trousers.

June 2, 1941, Monday, second day of Pentecost

Service an hour earlier and later agitation for the work.
In the afternoon we sunbathe in the ravine and in the evening we eat 6 eggs each
at the restaurant.

June 3, 1941, Tuesday

We all live under the impression of the farm work, and the news reports
ever new German successes; apparently they have landed in Syria.

June 4, 1941, Wednesday

A great deal is being said about France joining the German side.
All of us find ourselves in spiritual turmoil, not knowing what to
do with ourselves — whether to join the Foreign Legion, or go to the farm as we
call it, to the "paysan," or return to Europe.

June 5, 1941, Thursday

A captain and Lt. Polański arrived from Oran. They went to the head of
agriculture and agreed on the terms of work on the farms: the daily wage is to be
20 francs plus food and lodging. Afterward there was much quarreling at the briefing
with the company because many do not want to go to the farm. Perhaps they have a
point, but someone must go so that we are not all together in one place, because that draws
attention and could harm us.

June 6, 1941, Friday

On the walk in the ravine we found a pepper tree, and now people joke
about us that Hitler banished us to where the pepper grows.

June 7, 1941, Saturday

As usual on Saturdays a bedding inspection was ordered, for which the bedding must be
folded properly, but it did not take place because only a few individuals had done so.
Because of this the command imposed an O.P.R. [drill/punishment exercise]. It must
be admitted, however, that discipline is very good compared to other
camps, which makes this time pass more pleasantly. Besides this, the day was marked by
lively passport activity. They took the radio away for exchange and we do not know
what is happening on the fronts.
June 8, 1941, Sunday Service at 7 in the morning, but it was almost shameful to go to church because the choir did not sing, only Soboszczyk fumbled about on the harmonium and began singing a few hymns, but the company did not sing along. Another group has managed to slip away from Marseille, there are five of them, among them Mietek Szymański. He recounts that yet another group was caught in Oran by the French and no one knows what happened to them. And that the story about Lt. Straub having stolen company funds is a lie. Sadecki and Wolski ended up in some kind of shelter for invalids or the elderly.

June 9, 1941, Monday

News reaches us that yesterday the English attacked Syria and that they have already taken Damascus and Beirut.

June 10, 1941, Tuesday

It turns out that the news from Syria was not so favorable, because those two aforementioned cities have not yet been taken.

June 11, 1941, Wednesday

And again five more arrived from Marseille; I know one of them, namely Rutkowski. He recounts that it is very hard to get through because the French police catch people and take them to a penal camp, but the brotherhood flees anyway however it can; he was traveling in another group but the whole group, except for him, was caught, and he arrived in Oran without a penny. Without money because it was held by the group's leader, who was caught.

June 12, 1941, Thursday, Corpus Christi

This feast, which in Poland we celebrate so solemnly, we honored here with a Holy Mass at which the choir sang. Besides us there were scouts and schoolchildren present. The feast is not observed here at all, so we too were put to work, being ordered to carry all the barracks equipment out to the square in front of the barracks for inspection. The men curse, calling the whole command a bunch of clowns and idiots, but they must obey. The inspection is conducted by the French. Matuszczak and Kowalski caught a young vulture or eagle in the ravine and we are keeping it in the hall; it has a good appetite, today it ate a medium-sized snake and a quarter kilo of meat. Today the police again brought in someone in handcuffs; he is known to us from Carpiagne. Michał Kosiński, some bankrupt aristocrat.

Newspaper clipping:
GOLDEN THOUGHTS
In war, he wins who makes the fewest mistakes.

June 13, 1941, Friday

Today we received the tropical helmets, long promised to us by the Red Cross, and nice dark glasses. Much joking and merriment resulted from this because nearly every one of us is wearing such an outfit for the first time in his life.

June 14, 1941, Saturday

The company commander held a briefing because a report had come from the water and forestry authority that Poles have been going to drink water from the forester's, and this has already happened twice, and he does not wish for it, and that apparently they were to have cut down several young trees from the nursery. The lieutenant berated the company, but witnesses testified that it was the travailleurs who had cut the young trees from the nursery, and that they would be identified, and for now the matter ended there.

(photograph)
Saïda, June 10, 1941. In the swimming pool.

June 15, 1941, Sunday

As usual the choir sang exceptionally well, and after dinner there was a football match against Saïda, which our side won 2-1.

June 16, 1941, Monday
Nothing special.

June 17, 1941, Tuesday

In the afternoon an inspection of the garrison by a French general, at which we too were present, as were the travailleurs. The general took particular interest in the Poles, and afterward called over the Polish officers, spoke with them, and shook their hands on the square in the presence of all the garrison's officers. He is said to have told us not to worry, because we will yet fight shoulder to shoulder with the French. During the inspection, the garrison commander, Major Fuzeau, our best friend, remarked to the general, pointing to the commander of the demobilized groups, our greatest enemy, cadet officer Gradyean, that he was "le surveillant," meaning the overseer.

June 18, 1941, Wednesday

Today a German-Italian commission arrived in Saïda for the purpose of inspecting the garrison. But the French drove the legionnaires, us Poles, and the travailleurs out into the field, leaving behind only about 50 unfit men for show. Several of the travailleurs who wanted to reach the commission were locked in the guardhouse. Of the Poles, apparently two, namely Fiszer and Podniesiński, wanted to go speak with the commission, but Maj. Fuzeau did not allow it, saying that those Polish patriots who wish to return to Poland can do so through the German Red Cross and not through the military commission. Everyone did not return to the barracks until 8:30 in the evening. On June 17, 1941, those who had gone to work in the Sahara returned, terribly exhausted.

June 19, 1941, Thursday through June 21, 1941, Saturday

The heat is beginning, reaching up to 50 degrees in the sun at midday. From 12 to 2:30 at midday we have mandatory rest, but it is impossible to sleep from the heat and the flies bite terribly, so we spend this time playing cards.

June 22, 1941, Sunday
An exceptionally happy day.

Returning from church, where our choir sang beautifully and the church was full of people, we learned that the Germans had attacked Russia and that the English had taken Damascus. Unusual joy prevailed, because whatever the outcome of this encounter, instead of one enemy we shall have fewer. Two hours later I received a letter from my closest family back home saying that they are well and living in their own house. This is the first letter from home to me. The occasion should have been toasted, but there was nothing to toast with; however, in the evening Dziadkowiec turned up wanting to play cards, we agreed to play for beer, and again luck was with me, for I won 4 beers and we immediately toasted the occasion.

June 23, 1941, Monday

In the afternoon while bathing in the pool I struck my knee, which swelled up immediately. Bathing in such heat is a great pleasure.

June 24, 1941, Tuesday

In the afternoon our doctor, Przedborski (from Radom), gave a talk on the topic of venereal diseases. The matter presents itself terribly among us: out of a strength of 200, 36 are sick with gonorrhea and 12 with syphilis, and a further 6 are under observation, suspected of this latter disease. The doctor implored us to be more restrained, describing what lies ahead. The garrison command has also taken up prevention of these diseases; to this end they have ordered that special written permits be obtained from the doctor in order to be able to use the brothel, where one of the NCOs is on duty, not admitting anyone without these permits, and collecting them, and a medical orderly who performs the procedures.

Furthermore, the garrison commander announced that anyone who falls ill will be punished. Such is the state of affairs after a four-month stay, and what will come next?

June 25, 1941, Wednesday

Starting today, 20 men from our company are to work every day on building a sports field. They started with the 1st platoon, in which I am, and so I spent the whole day digging a pit for the long jump and got myself a few blisters, since my hand is not used to it. There were many who did not even pick up a shovel, only argued with Lt. Raczyński about why they were being ordered to work.

June 26, 1941, Thursday

Some French colonel arrived, declaring that he had orders to recruit us for trans-Saharan work; to this end he began a speech saying that Poland and France had always been friends, that France was currently cooperating with Germany in order, after the war, to be able to take part in the new European order and thereby stand up for Poland. The listeners smiled ironically because one may not speak, which the colonel noticed and immediately changed his speech. He claimed that the work in the Sahara has nothing to do with politics, that it is merely a colonial undertaking to which the Poles too should contribute their labor, since they eat French bread. We are to work there as truck drivers and are to earn 30 francs a day plus 4 fr. bonus for travel time, of which food at the military quartermaster's costs 16 francs, so the pay is ridiculously small. Our officers, not knowing what to do, began making volunteer lists in order to delay the matter somewhat and in the meantime ask the Polish authorities what to do, but the colonel was in such a hurry that this gave our officers pause. There were not enough volunteers, so the colonel himself picked from the list of trades. Meanwhile our officers telephoned Algiers, to Mr. Delegate of the Polish Red Cross, Mr. Czapski, who replied that he would come in person, so the colonel stayed until the next day.

The clerks say that the garrison commander, Major Fuzeau, defended us, that he quarreled with the colonel, who told him to get out of the office, and that the major is said to have said to Lt. Piątkowski these words, even though he always addresses him formally as "pan" [sir]: "Piątkowski, save the people," explaining that now in the Sahara one cannot bear the heat, that all work there has already stopped, and that such pay is the worst exploitation of a man.

We recall that the French once before, in history, did the same thing with Dąbrowski's legions, using them for a colonial undertaking on the island (San Domingo), where all of them perished from the bad climate and from fighting with the natives. So history repeats itself.

June 27, 1941, Friday

The French colonel departed today for Algiers, unable to wait for Mr. Czapski's arrival, promising that he would return in 3 days and get his way.

June 28, 1941, Saturday

Hall No. 3 is preparing for the Feast of the Sea to be held tomorrow. The commandant of this hall is the sailor Mikusek. A mast has been set up in the hall with the flags of Poland, England, and the United States, as well as signal flags.

On the mast were placed the names of the Polish ships that have been sunk in this war. Everything was proceeding in the best order, but life always has its veto, and so it was here too, because the supplier who was to deliver 100 liters of wine — colloquially called "ink" — was stopped at the guardhouse and the wine was confiscated. A great loss, but we hope that Major Fuzeau will get it released for us.

June 29, 1941, Sunday

The wine was released from arrest and the barrel was set up under the mast with the flags, and the Feast of the Sea celebration began. Wine was drunk and eaten with bread and tomato salad. Several songs were sung, one of the participants did an impression of "Wiech" [a Polish humorist], and it ended with the singing of the national anthem. Thus ended the official part, at which the company commander, Lt. Piątkowski, was present. From afternoon until late evening unofficial drinking continued, and it reached such a point that one of the participants in this drinking bout used Tadek Mazur's guitar as a chamber pot.

June 30, 1941, Monday

The morning news reports the death of Ignacy Paderewski.

July 1, 1941, Tuesday

News from the Soviet front puts us in poor spirits. We fear that this war, so longed-for by us, will end too quickly and the Germans will not be sufficiently worn down. However, we have faith in the prophecies of Wernyhora, where in one place it says that the Black Eagle will turn to the east and return with a broken wing, and that the hammer and sickle will be defeated in the east (this refers to Japan).

July 2, 1941, Wednesday

Not 20 men but 40 now go to work on the sports field, and we hope that next week even more will go. Today Lt. Piątkowski locked up Oprychał and Kozakiewicz in the guardhouse for refusing to go to work.

I would like to write a letter to Poland, but I do not have even a centime for the letter and the postage, and besides, what would I write, since what I would want to say is not allowed.

July 3, 1941, Thursday

Our hall did not turn out for the morning roll call in protest over the locking up of Oprychał. Because he is a very quiet man, and since he refused to go to work he was in the right. After roll call the company commander summoned our whole hall to the office and looked into the matter; besides that, the hall asked for a change of hall commandant. Lt. Piątkowski gave no answer, but in the afternoon Oprychał was released from the guardhouse, while Kozakiewicz was transferred to prison for 15 days.

June [sic] 4, 1941, Friday
50 men are now working at the stadium today.

July 5, 1941, Saturday

On the day of Ignacy Paderewski's funeral (as the radio reports), a funeral service was held in the parish church, at which
Major Fuzeau, our friend, took part. I imagine what our life in Saïda would be like if someone else were the commandant here given today's political situation. We would have hell.

July 6, 1941, Sunday

As usual for every holiday. Holy Mass was celebrated by some visiting priest who, after finishing the Mass, during the singing of our "Boże coś Polskę," wanted to leave the altar, not knowing what this hymn was, but one of the altar boys (a French scout) held him back by the surplice and the priest remained until the end.

July 7, 1941, Monday
Normal, no changes.

July 8, 1941, Tuesday

In the afternoon the French drove us out again to the ravine because an Italian commission was supposed to arrive. The sun has already scorched all the grass; only the trees are green, but their leaves are already falling, as with us in autumn.

July 9, 1941, Wednesday

Rumors are going around that General Weygand is to come to Saïda. I am working on building a sports field. Radio from London reports that Syria is asking for a ceasefire.

July 10, 1941, Thursday

Last night Ślęzak was beaten for absolutely no reason. The perpetrators were Fiszer, Podniesiński, and one more whose surname I don't know, commonly called Bazyl. All of them were locked up under arrest. People just cannot sit still without causing trouble.

Apparently a letter came to Major Fuzeau saying we are to go to the Sahara.

July 11, 1941, Friday

We are working at the stadium, where we had no small amount of amusement because Mietek Grzegorzewski lost his license for all vehicles, since the mule he used to haul stones would not obey him at all, and the French lieutenant had to relieve him of that job. Radio reports that the Vichy government rejected the English ceasefire conditions.

July 12, 1941, Saturday

Briefing by the company commander, Lieutenant Piątkowski, at which he acquaints us with the situation. It is not as bad as it seemed to me.

(2 photographs)
Karwaciński Stanisław; Szymański Mieczysław.

June 13, 1941, Sunday

A truly unlucky 13th. I already had my fill of shame in church because the choir did not sing, since it had not been released from work duty for rehearsals during the time when
work was going on at the stadium, and in general nothing went right. General Deutz signs the ceasefire in Syria.

July 14, 1941, Monday

We honored the French national holiday with a service in church, free from work, and good food and very abundant wine — for example, we got 4 times more than usual.

July 15, 1941, Tuesday

I am putting in my turn of work at the stadium.

July 16, 1941, Wednesday

Today I found in a French newspaper the motto of the French combatants' legion, and it is "travail, famille, patrie," while ours is "God, Honor, and Fatherland." I wonder which of these is better. We lost our Fatherland but kept our faith and honor, while they lost their fatherland and their honor. Placing family before fatherland already proved ruinous for France, because the French soldier preferred to individually defend his family rather than fight at the front in the last war.

July 17, 1941, Thursday

Today a Polish priest arrived from Algiers and after supper gave the first talk in the dining hall. He spoke very well, so we all listened with great attention. It is impossible for me to give even a summary in the diary, but in general I will say it was a speech more patriotic than religious. At the end he asked that we sing together with him 3 verses of the "Rota." And while singing this song, hearts hardened by misfortune softened like wax under the influence of warmth, and I saw how a dozen or so men furtively wiped away a tear stubbornly forcing its way to their eyes. What power the words of the "Rota" hold within them will be felt only by one who has lived through what we are living through today.

July 18, 1941, Friday

Today those of the men who do not work at the stadium attended Mass and the talk in church, and in the afternoon the choir had confession.

July 19, 1941, Saturday

Confession for the rest, myself included. In general about ¾ of the company came to confession.

July 20, 1941, Sunday

At a special Polish Holy Mass we took Holy Communion, and the priest delivered a sermon, which surprised the French present at our Mass because he did not read from a book as French priests do. After the Mass the priest thanked the choir for the singing. In the afternoon a football match took place between our team and the legionnaires. We won 6–2. There are several good players among those who recently arrived to us from Tunis. Today 3 more Poles arrived who had spent a long time in prison for illegally crossing the border.
July 21, 1941, Monday Today a Polish requiem Mass was held for the souls of soldiers of the 10th Armored Cavalry Brigade who fell. A French colonel unexpectedly came to Saïda to check whether the Poles are working, because Major Fuzeau had been writing reports saying the Poles were working, while Aspirant Grand-Jean wrote that they didn't want to do anything. Well, the colonel found the Poles working properly at the stadium of our enemy, blessed it thoroughly, and Major Fuzeau gave him the opinion that he is a ham and completely limited in mind, at the same time asking for him to be removed from his post. Lieutenant Raczyński told us about this.

July 22, 1941, Tuesday

Lieutenant Raczyński read us today an order that we are to draw uniforms for the Sahara and that we may leave any day now.

July 23, 1941, Wednesday

A radio bulletin from London reports that the Vichy government handed over the naval bases in Indochina to Japan and agreed to the occupation of that country by Japan.

July 24, 1941, Thursday

I am starting to attend choir rehearsals, to which Szpetnar invited me, though he himself is withdrawing from it because he has signed up for the legion's orchestra until he can legally leave Africa. Kula, Kaszubowski, and Czarnecki went along with him.

July 25, 1941, Friday

I am selling, through Cierkasz as intermediary, a leather jacket for 300 francs; from the Red Cross we get a relief payment of 40 fr.

July 26, 1941, Saturday
Nothing to note.

July 27, 1941, Sunday
Like every previous Sunday.

July 28, 1941, Monday

I am sending two letters, one to Poland and the other to Janek Sadowski in Marseille. The choir sang today during the requiem Mass for the soul of a legionnaire NCO who fell in the war. In the evening, with Dziadkowiec, Zarychta, and Zelek, we arranged a campfire for ourselves in the ravine with scrambled eggs and melon.

Today in the order there was a list of those who were designated for the Sahara. I am among them, as is nearly our whole room.

July 29, 1941, Tuesday

Today, vaccination against typhus, and right on the first day I got properly ill, so much that at midday I couldn't even go out for a natural need, but by evening I felt much better and slept well during the night.
July 30, 1941, Wednesday We are resting after the vaccination and they are feeding us bouillon and coffee. The London radio news reports on the signing of an agreement between Poland and Russia, under which Russia recognizes Poland's eastern borders of 1939, that a Polish army will be formed in Russia, and that an exchange of ambassadors will follow immediately after the agreement is signed. This Sikorski really does have a head for politics.

June 31, 1941, Thursday

The third day after the vaccination I am completely well, and to the meatless dinner they added our normal portion of bread. Lieutenant Raczyński told us that a request had come in for craftsmen for the mine near Colomb-Béchar, where the pay is 50 to 70 francs a day. Apparently Major Fuzeau wrote to Oran that it would be better to send the Poles there.

August 1, 1941, Friday

The widow of a legionnaire who fell at the front, for whose soul the Mass was held last Monday, gave the choir her thanks and 200 francs.

August 2, 1941, Saturday

Today a French colonel who during the war had been attached to the staff of the 2nd Polish Division carried out an inspection of the garrison. He also went around our living quarters and asked the garrison commander what we do.

August 3, 1941, Sunday

Szpetnar continues to lead the choir, which as usual sang in church. After dinner I was at the cinema with Kosiński and Zarychta. The film was nice. In the evening our men played a football match against the Legionnaires and lost 4–1 because they underestimated them. They fielded a lineup of men who mostly barely play football at all.

August 4, 1941:

Today at noon several ringleaders got drunk and called a company assembly. Some men went of their own will, some out of fear, and still others didn't go at all — I didn't go either, nor did anyone from my room; Zelek and Kowacz didn't go either, and there were even cowards who were afraid to go one way or the other and hid in the latrine for the duration. The assembly was called in order to force Lieutenant Piątkowski to release from arrest Grajnert, who had been punished for trying to undermine the company commander's authority and for demanding that the lieutenant be replaced in command. The lieutenant did not come to the assembly but ordered an assembly at 3 o'clock instead. I was present at this official assembly too. At the assembly those ringleaders who had shouted the loudest hung back at the rear. Only after some thought did Kosarzewski speak up, demanding the above-mentioned matters, but the lieutenant categorically refused both points. After Kosarzewski a few more fools blurted something out, like Flond, Pieńkowski, and Kula, but to no effect. Kosarzewski bases his stand on the fact that the lieutenant gives overly harsh punishments, as a result of which Kozakiewicz, punished with 15 days of arrest for the same thing as Grajnert, fell ill and went to the hospital.
August 5, 1941, Tuesday At the morning assembly Lieutenant Piątkowski announced that an order had come recalling the departure for the Sahara for now, adding that this was not the doing of the rabble, such as the Flonds, Kozakiewiczes, and others, but of someone else. A few days ago the ringleaders made a list of the company where it was written that those listed below did not agree to the trip to the Sahara, for which they were ready to bear all consequences. Many, however, did not sign this list — for instance, our entire room, with the apparent exception of Skrzypek. That is why the lieutenant added the remark about the rabble.

August 6, 1941, Wednesday

By today's order the company commander deducted pay for the underwear from those who had sold theirs — and there is a large percentage of them — and suspended the Red Cross relief payments for those he considered to be perpetrators of Monday's incident.

August 7, 1941, Thursday
No changes.

August 8, 1941, Friday

The garrison orchestra, half of whose musicians are Poles, gave its first concert today. The concert turned out well. Major Fuzeau was present at the concert along with several French officers with their wives, and Polish officers. It took place on the barracks square next to the Foyer.

August 9, 1941, Saturday

Today, vaccination, the second dose, but the choir has its postponed to Monday. In the afternoon an Italian-German commission unexpectedly showed up, so those who had not had their injections had to leave the barracks for a certain period of time, and by the back entrance at that, so the commission wouldn't see it, while those vaccinated had to lie in their bunks, and no one was allowed to be seen on the barracks square.

August 10, 1941, Sunday

Early in the morning, all the healthy ones were ordered to clear out of the barracks and the town. The choir did not sing either, because the Italian-German commission is visiting Saïda. In the afternoon the commission left and it was allowed to go into town. There an entertainment group is performing, with a carousel, swings, a shooting gallery, and lotteries. We spent the whole afternoon watching these amusements.

August 11, 1941, Monday

The choir was supposed to get its injections today but they were postponed until tomorrow.

August 12, 1941, Tuesday

Today I received the second injection. I felt fine all day, only in the evening and at night I had a slight fever. They are feeding us milk — for dinner and supper together they issued 2 liters of milk.

August 13, 1941, Wednesday

We still have leave from duty after the vaccination, but in the morning the choir has a rehearsal.
August 14, 1941, Thursday
Like yesterday.

August 15, 1941, Friday — Polish Soldier's Day
We begin with a service in the parish church and then a morning celebration in the dining hall decorated with Polish eagles and flags.
The morning celebration opened with an occasional address by Lieutenant Piątkowski, who ended it with the words of the "Rota," which the company picked up, and 3 verses were sung; then a short skit involving a card game, and the choir sang "Rozwiń swe skrzydła białe." Still before the end of the celebration the choir went for a second breakfast at the Jewish café, specially ordered with money received for singing at the requiem Mass for the NCO "legionnaire." Then the choir sang at the French High Mass. In the evening a tombola was held at the municipal theater, during which the garrison orchestra performed. I went to listen. A good deal of wine was added to dinner and supper, as a result of which several men got drunk and got into fights.

August 16, 1941, Saturday
One of our afflictions is a plague of bedbugs; plenty of these creatures are found in the walls, shelves, and beds. The nights spent in their company are the worst. Many emigrate at night to sleep on the barracks square or in the dining hall, where there are fewer of them. Some are so oversensitive on the subject of bedbugs that nothing else can be discussed with them, and they kill them all day long, while at night they turn on the light every 5 minutes and hunt bedbugs. I have found it is better to pay them as little attention as possible — at least then one's nerves stay calm.

August 17, 1941, Sunday
Last night a troop transport passed through Saïda, which was received by the local garrison with a supper and orchestra. Because of this, there was no bread for today's breakfast. A very sultry day, the early hour, and the lack of breakfast all contributed to the choir singing poorly at Mass. In the evening, at the municipal theater, the conclusion of Friday's tombola, and late in the evening again a troop transport passed through, received as yesterday. For several days now shirts have been disappearing in the company, and today one of our fellows recognized his shirt; pressed with questions, the thief gave up who he'd bought it from, and so one was uncovered, and we expect there will be more.

August 18, 1941, Monday
Today another shirt thief was tracked down and given a beating, and the company commander put him under arrest.

August 19, 1941, Tuesday
Rumors are circulating again that the departure for the Sahara is once more current, and that the workshop men are to leave by September 1.
August 20, 1941, Wednesday
The shirt thieves, namely Walmus and Szymański Mieczysław, were punished by today's order with 15 days of strict arrest, of which 8 days in a cell. In the evening the gendarmes brought in legionnaires who had deserted from the Legion two days ago. They were placed under arrest right away.

August 21, 1941, Thursday
For July and August we were not issued soap, and because of this I haven't washed with soap for several days.

August 22, 1941, Friday
Nothing special.

August 23, 1941, Saturday
At 10 in the morning a medal ceremony took place for 5 people; among them is one Pole who has served 15 years in the Legion. Poles were also present during the decoration ceremony, after which a parade took place.

August 24, 1941, Sunday
The choir did not sing at Mass because Szpotnar has no time to rehearse since he plays in the orchestra. In the afternoon Poles and legionnaires played a match against the Arabs, whose goalkeeper was from Saïda's first team, and won 3–7. August 25, 1941, Monday
We received the 3rd injection and turned in our mattresses, and in exchange got straw pallets. We were also issued soap coupons, and finally there is something to wash with. On Saturday the Red Cross gave us 10 dkg of soap each.
London radio reports the entry of English and Soviet troops into Iran (Persia).

August 26, 1941, Tuesday
We saw the Arabs eating young leaves, or fruit of the cactus — I don't know what they're properly called. This fruit has a sweet taste but is dangerous, because as self-defense it has countless tiny little spines which, on contact, stay in your hand and are hard to get rid of. Many of our fellows caused themselves pain with it, but I found a way around it. Namely, I watched how the Arabs do it, and they do it in such a way that before picking the fruit they knock all the spines off it with branches. I tried it that way and it worked well.

August 27, 1941, Wednesday
We got from the Red Cross a shot in the pocket of 50 francs each and set off, four of us, to the cinema. The film was good. During
the intermission Lieutenant Raczyński told us that an order had come for departure to the Sahara, and that the first group is to be in Colomb-Béchar by September 5.

August 28, 1941, Thursday
The garrison orchestra's bandmaster has been transferred to Senegal and said goodbye to us very warmly, even though he is a German. A new sergeant has taken his place.

August 29, 1941, Friday
The orchestra performed on the barracks square, but it came out terribly badly because the new bandmaster is no good.

August 30, 1941, Saturday
By order of Major Fuzeau, a Pole, Sergeant Hankiewicz, becomes bandmaster. In Poland he was bandmaster of the 20th Infantry Regiment, and yesterday's bandmaster is now to handle only administrative matters. The orchestra is very pleased with this change.

August 31, 1941, Sunday
It is the holiday of the French combatants. It was honored with a service, then speeches and a parade. We took part in the capacity of observers. After supper a football match took place against a Saïda selection. It was won by the Poles 3–2, though it wasn't interesting. At the same time as the match, the further part of the morning's celebrations took place in town, where the garrison orchestra performed.

September 1, 1941, Monday
Today we begin the 3rd year of the war. Those who had not yet gotten the third injection received it today.

September 2, 1941, Tuesday
My name day we celebrated, the three of us, with Zarychta and Dziadkowiec, with a modest little supper.

September 3, 1941, Wednesday
Those leaving tomorrow for the Sahara are drawing their equipment today. In the evening I was at the cinema with Zarychta and Kosiński, watching the film King Kong.

September 4, 1941, Thursday
The first group, under the command of one of the officers who came from Mascara, numbering 23, left for Colomb-Béchar. Among them, of my closest friends, went Zarychta W., Zelek S., Kowacz J. I saw them off at the station, and there Lieutenant Kowalski took a farewell photograph of us.

September 5, 1941, Friday – September 6, 1941, Saturday
News reached us that Major Fuzeau has been transferred from Saïda, and everyone expresses regret about it, because he was a great friend to us.
September 7, 1941, Sunday
One of the finest matches in Saïda took place; the game was very hard-fought, and the Poles won 2–4.

September 8, 1941, Monday
In the evening, at the officers' casino, a farewell dinner was held on the occasion of Major Fuzeau's departure for Dakar to become chief of a half-brigade. During the dinner the Polish choir sang, in reduced numbers due to lack of space, along with Spaniards and the garrison orchestra.

September 9, 1941, Tuesday
Before noon Major Fuzeau said farewell to the garrison, after which a parade took place. The Poles did not take part in the farewell. At noon Major F. left by bus for Oran, and the garrison orchestra played during his departure.
News from the Soviet front is heartening, because the Russians are defending themselves bravely and for several days have even been conducting an offensive on the central front; Odessa and Leningrad are putting up fierce resistance. Let them slaughter each other as much as possible — they're both worth as much as each other.
Yesterday I got a letter from Wicek Zarychta saying that they were received warmly in Colomb-Béchar and that the matter does not look as dire as they had supposed.

September 10, 1941, Wednesday
No changes.

September 11, 1941, Thursday
Lieutenant Piątkowski gathered the choir and said that the French authorities in Oran are asking that the Polish choir give 3 concerts in that city. One for the garrison officers, another for certain sub-units, and a third for school youth. He stressed the propaganda significance of this event and asked that the choir make every effort, and that he would take care of what he could, and that those who had had their Red Cross benefits suspended would probably get them back. But there turned out to be a few individuals who, feeling that they were needed, set their own demands as a condition, and a quarrel arose over this, and how it actually ended I don't know because we dispersed for dinner, but in the order there was a release from duty for all choir members. Quite a while ago old clothes came from America through the Red Cross, but only today were they given out, and I had my jacket exchanged for a better one, though the lining is very worn and torn.
Going out on my daily evening walk I encountered a funeral that amazed me by its scale and by the fact that it was in white rather than in crepe. A white hearse, horses covered in white caparisons, and young people dressed in white. Later I learned that it was the funeral of a young unmarried woman. She was among the prettiest in Saïda and apparently died as the result of an accident with a primus stove.
September 12, 1941, Friday
The chief engaged me to make copies from ID documents for sending them to Vichy for the purpose of granting combatant badges.

September 13, 1941, Saturday
The parish priest of Saïda asked the choir to sing at High Mass tomorrow.

September 14, 1941, Sunday
At High Mass two choirs sang, Polish and French, and only here could one really notice how much better our choir sings. In the evening the garrison orchestra performed in town. Hankiewicz was a bit tipsy and conducted very well. Despite the holiday the choir rehearsed the whole afternoon. For several days now the weather has turned bad, rain falling all day with only small breaks.
Radio from London reports that Finland has ceased military operations against Russia.

September 15, 1941, Monday
Returning from an evening walk with Seidel, we heard a Polish melody ("Rozszumiały się wierzby płaczące") being sung by young French men and women, since they had heard us singing it and liked it very much.

September 16, 1941, Tuesday — The choir is preparing for Oran
September 17, 1941, Wednesday September 18, 1941, Thursday
At the evening news there was another brawl — Kula and Szpetnar beat up Czajkowski and Łukawski, the reason for which, as everyone claims, was reporting to the Chancellery everything anyone said in the company. About Łukawski (the company chronicler) it is being said, in turn, that he was supposed to have written in the chronicle that the choir is now waiting for someone to die so it can earn something, and that we concern ourselves too much with individual matters and not general ones. I haven't read the chronicle and don't know if it's true; I only write what the general opinion says.

September 19, 1941, Friday
The choir does not rehearse today because it was assigned to work. I gathered that this is connected with yesterday's brawl, and Kula and Szpetnar were punished with 8 days of arrest.

September 20, 1941, Saturday
Right after the flag was raised, a briefing was held at which Lieutenant Piątkowski discussed our financial situation, asked that we work willingly and do at least something at the stadium, because the command had summoned him several times saying that we do nothing, and that Grandjean writes reports that the Poles sit around at the stadium all day and thereby demoralize his laborers, and he explained a few more rumors as well. Several men attacked the company sergeant-major, Benal Rudolf, saying he treats people improperly, but I saw no grounds for it. Wiktor Kosiński is treating me to the cinema because he works at the officers' casino and always earns a few francs. The film
was good. Before evening, Mr. Czapski arrived, delegate of the Committee for Aid to Poles, as the P.C.K. [Polish Red Cross] is now called.

September 21, 1941, Sunday
At High Mass our choir sang together with the French choir in alternation, as it was the previous Sunday. Mr. Czapski was present at High Mass. He was then present at dinner and supper and went around the rooms talking with individual people about their needs. After dinner a football match took place between Saïda I and Sidi Bel Abbès. Our team played beforehand against the Arabs and won for the 4th time, 3–1. Saïda also won, 2–0; Sidi Bel Abbès played very poorly. Only the goalkeeper played brilliantly. It rained the whole evening, because of which the Sunday concert was cancelled. I sent a letter to Wicek Zarychta through a lieutenant whom Mr. Czapski sent to Colomb-Béchar.

September 22, 1941, Monday
September 23, 1941, Tuesday — The choir rehearses
September 24, 1941, Wednesday
Lieutenant Piątkowski received a telephone message that tomorrow a colonel from Mascara would arrive, and he ordered a list of tank crewmen to be drawn up.

September 25, 1941, Thursday
Unexpectedly we got an order to move to the laborers' barracks. We were getting ready for the move when the colonel arrived, and the move took place with him present. We had to haul heavy iron beds and whole bundles of equipment like pots, buckets, plates, and tables with benches. It lasted until noon. The barracks here are poor but much better than in Argelès. The French colonel apparently came in connection with engaging us for the mechanical workshops in Mascara. What the outcome of this was I don't know. In the afternoon Lieutenant Zawalski arrived and brought news that apparently the camp from Morocco is on its way to Saïda. I am going to bed earlier because I am very tired, since I haven't worked this hard in a long time as I did today.

September 26, 1941, Friday
In the new camp we feel more at ease; we have a beautiful view of Saïda here, the food is more plentiful and better made. One weak point of the camp is the lack of sewerage, and because of this the latrine buckets have to be carried out far into the field twice a day. Naturally this causes a lot of trouble because no one wants to do it.

September 27, 1941, Saturday
September 28, 1941, Sunday
The choir sang at High Mass as it did last Sunday, and in the afternoon 15 Poles arrived at the camp from the camp in Morocco; they say they are the remnant of that camp, since most are in England or on their way there. They managed to slip away. These men had also tried to escape but were caught by police.
September 29, 1941, Monday
Among those who arrived yesterday is one very good tenor. My friends taught me to play poker, and I kill all my free moments with it now. A new garrison commandant arrived; he is a French captain.

October 1, 1941, Wednesday
Wiktor Kosiński no longer went to the casino today because our officers now dine at the hotel, and only 6 people remain at the casino, so one is enough. On the staff there is a legionnaire.

October 2, 1941, Thursday
Somehow nothing new is happening. We have more peace and quiet here than in the barracks.

October 3, 1941, Friday
Many legionnaires have joined the garrison orchestra, and because of this our men were removed from it.

October 4, 1941, Saturday
I'm having a very unlucky day at cards; playing just for 10 centimes I lost about 30 fr.

October 5, 1941, Sunday
I was at High Mass. The choir did not sing because the French were singing, and the company goes to church individually. In the afternoon a football match took place at the municipal stadium between Saïda and Oran (sailors); Saïda lost 3–1. I watched the match from the green stand because entry costs 8 fr. 50 cm., which is expensive for our pockets.

October 6, 1941, Monday
They don't exchange our sleeping bags for us the way they did with the sheets, so we have to wash them ourselves, and washing one is a nasty job and uses a lot of soap. That's just what I did today.

October 7, 1941, Tuesday
The new garrison commandant carried out an inspection of our barracks. Everyone living in them stood at assembly. The captain asked the barracks commander, Mr. G., what we do and whether we have only these one set of overalls. And he answered that he had offered us more but we didn't want to accept it. The captain reportedly said that tomorrow a colonel, a friend of the Poles, would arrive and it would somehow get sorted out. After the inspection I went for a walk. We now have a second spring. All the plants scorched by the summer sun have come back to life, and there are many flowers. I brought Wiktor a nice little bouquet from that walk. Radio reports that the Germans have abandoned Leningrad, directing their offensive at Moscow.
October 8, 1941, Wednesday
About two weeks ago Dzikowski and Klewka slipped away from the camp, and today Piekarski and Dzida did. Our authorities reported these incidents to the French authorities. Today a French general, commander of the sub-division from Tlemcen, paid us a visit. He asked about our life, work, and clothing, even though we do not belong to him. This visit raised us in Grangeon's eyes. I attend choir rehearsals, but I am singing worse and worse.

October 9, 1941, Thursday
The gendarmerie brought in Frydman, who had slipped away a few months ago. He was taken straight to arrest.

October 10, 1941, Friday — As usual
October 11, 1941, Saturday
October 12, 1941, Sunday
At Mass the choir sang, and there was a duet by Mr. Kwaśniak and Mr. Konewka.
After dinner Saïda played a match against Sidi-Bel-Abbès. It lost 2–1. Having no money, I was in the green stand, as most of the Poles do. During the match, apparently one of the Poles was flashing a mirror at the stands. The police searched Laszkiewicz for a mirror but did not find one.

October 13, 1941, Monday
Reveille is an hour earlier and we go to work at the shooting range, about 4 km away, where we are repairing the embankments. Dinner was brought to us there, and on top of that Grandjean gave us a pack of cigarettes each.
Tadek Mazur went for bread and wine to Naizarek and swore a great deal because Naizarek, which had looked so close, turned out to be far away, and he lost 3 hours of time over it.

October 15, 1941, Wednesday
In the afternoon hours a French general, commander of the Oran division, visited us. He took a lively interest in our affairs and expressed admiration for our moral bearing. The laborers, during this visit as during the previous one, were not at the assembly but were working.
Radio reports that the Germans are 100 km from Moscow and are still advancing. That Polish units are in Tobruk.

October 16, 1941, Thursday
The company went today to work at the airfield. I got out of it because my boots are badly torn.

A day off from work after yesterday's labor.

The Vox cinema is showing the film "Taras Bulba." It is a film set against the fighting of the Cossacks with the Poles. I saw it and liked it very much.
October 19, 1941, Sunday
The singing in church went very well. In the afternoon our football team played one of the finest matches against Saïda's first team, drawing 2–2. Three of our players will now play for Saïda's team. They are Oprych, Apollo, and Tomaszewski.

October 20, 1941, Monday
Slippers came again from Algiers, but they're too small for me. Lieutenant Piątkowski told me to find some in town myself and he'd give me money to buy them.

I am working at the shooting range.

October 22, 1941, Wednesday
I am looking for slippers in town but cannot find any.

October 23, 1941, Thursday
Some Spaniard beat up Sierota in town for going around with Spanish girls.

October 24, 1941, Friday
The Spaniard beat up Sierota again, even though the Poles had organized a manhunt against him.

October 25, 1941, Saturday
No sandals can be found anywhere in town, so I gave up further searching.

October 26, 1941, Sunday
In the afternoon a football match took place, Saïda–Oran, ending in a 3–3 draw. Poles did not play. I was in the green stand because of lack of money. The day is overcast and very cold.

October 27, 1941, Monday
Nothing worth noting.

October 28, 1941, Tuesday
The chief announced there is demand for 30 men to work picking olives. The pay is 20 fr. per 100 kg of olives picked, and it is possible to pick 200 kg. From the earnings one has to pay 15 fr. for board, which the farmer deducts at payment. Kosiński and I signed up.

October 29, 1941, Wednesday
From dawn we work picking olives. But with the greatest effort the three of us can pick 200 kg, so it turned out we had to pay extra toward our board without even thinking about earnings. But our officers asked us to go once more tomorrow, saying they would arrange something so we wouldn't work for nothing.
October 30, 1941, Friday
I am working picking olives. On returning from work some mysterious activity could be noticed, and during the night about 30 men disappeared.

November 1, 1941, Saturday
We are given strict orders for departure. We travel divided into various small groups which were to board and get off at different little stations and then gather at a precisely designated point by the sea.
Before departure I managed to sell my for-now unneeded shirts.

November 2, 1941, Sunday
We were supposed to leave by train at 5:25 but the train was delayed by 2½ hours; still, luckily we managed to leave and get to the designated place. I got off at Portaux Poules station, others at La Vacta and others still at other stations. Everything would have gone well, because it was fairly well organized and carried out, but the bad luck that pursues us played us a trick. Because a gamekeeper happened upon the group that had left on Saturday, which had been resting at its waiting position for 24 hours already, and reported it to the police. During the night the police carried out a raid and caught some of the men. And our officers, seeing that everything was lost, lined us up in fours and we marched to Saïda. Along the way the column stretched out over a distance of several kilometers. Throughout the whole department police and troops were put on alert, and individual groups were caught. I was caught right before Perregaux, along with about 70 of our men.

November 3, 1941, Monday
At the police post in Perregaux we wait for reports and decisions by the authorities for about 24 hours, sleeping on a concreted yard; it was cold as hell, and I have no warmer covering at all, only summer clothing, so I spent the greater part of the night walking about. We feed ourselves at our own expense, and it costs a lot. Arabs stand around the yard guarding us.

November 4, 1941, Tuesday
Surrounded by the Perregaux gendarmerie and Arabs, we were escorted through the whole town to the station, where there was already another group of Poles and a strong detachment from the police school, and these drove us to Saïda. At Tira station the officers and aspirants were taken over by gendarmerie and troops from Mascara. After us, at the Saïda station, the Saïda gendarmerie arrived to help. From the night of November 4–5 we were brought to the barracks and placed under arrest. We were given only straw pallets, and it's cold as hell, so that one cannot sleep all night, nor even walk about, because the cells are tiny and we sit three to a cell.

November 5, 1941, Wednesday
Nearly the whole company is sitting under arrest; only a few individuals remain out. I wonder what will happen to us next. And so our autumn maneuvers ended. And we lost the second Battle of Britain. The brotherhood sits under arrest cursing the guards for all they're worth. This arrest is painful to us, that it was meted out to us by the allies for whose
...cause for which we fought and for which so many Poles fell, and today, because we want to continue fighting for our common cause against the age-old enemy, they are throwing us in jail — for how long we don't know, and what will happen next we don't know either. The only thing is that almost the whole company is here, except for the 18 who remained in the barracks, and Lt. Piątkowski and Raczyński, who are being held under guard in a hotel — this makes the arrest easier for us to bear, and there is even some good humor.

November 6, 1941, Thursday
The provost of the jail is a Czech who speaks Polish not badly at all, and he carries out personal searches of the cells and belongings, sending these items to a commission that examines them, since, as I suppose, the French are conducting an investigation, because they surely think we were supposed to help the English land an invasion force. They can conclude this from the large quantity of gasoline, lubricants, and hardtack from Gibraltar that they found in the bushes on the coast.
The gasoline and oil had been sent from Gibraltar in order to transport us from Saïda to the coast, but our organizers could not buy a vehicle, so we traveled by rail instead. The hardtack was meant to save us from hunger while we waited.

November 7, 1941, Friday
Officer cadets and lieutenants Kowalski, Wojniłowicz, and Dr. Przedborski are held in cells together with us; the only distinction they have is that their cells are left open during the day. Today Lt. Przedborski was summoned for something to the garrison commandant, and when the latter wanted to shake the lieutenant's hand, he refused to offer his own, saying that if the French treat Poles this way, then he could not shake the hand of a French officer. The garrison commandant replied that he was only carrying out the orders of his superiors — "so am I," replied Lt. Przedborski.

November 8, 1941, Saturday
Only today was our cell searched, but I managed to hide my diary, photographs, and compass in the cell.

November 9, 1941, Sunday
Nothing happened, except the food was much better — every day they give us just enough to survive.

November 10, 1941, Monday
Rumors are circulating that they intend to take us to the Sahara; others say it will be to a penal camp near Algiers.

November 11, 1941, Tuesday
We spend the annual Independence Day holiday under arrest, and the rainy weather puts us in a very bad mood. The overseers from the travailleurs make us sign lists of pay and equipment.

November 12, 1941, Wednesday
Very early we were given breakfast, and along with it a bread bag containing a mess tin, spoon, and fork, and we were told to take our blanket and go out into the corridor. Here we were joined by the 19 men who had still been in the barracks, and after checking the roll and surrounding us with gendarmes,
they marched us to the railway station. The gendarmes, taking every precaution as though escorting the worst criminals, loaded us into freight cars and assigned two gendarmes per car to keep watch. At 7:25 the train, from whose locked cars singing could be heard, set off for Colomb-Béchar. Although this injustice pains us greatly, we do not lose our spirits and sing the whole way. The provisions for the journey are dry but good and plentiful.

November 13, 1941, Thursday
We arrive at the border station between Algeria and the territories of the Sahara at 0:30 and stand there until morning. The cars are so crowded that it's impossible to lie down, and not everyone can even sit. The night is very cold and troubles us badly. In the morning we continue on, having eaten breakfast together with lunch. The territories we pass through are almost uninhabited, full of stones, with clumps of grass here and there. Around 4 p.m. we passed through a larger town whose name I don't remember. In the town there are beautiful barracks in Arab style, on which we saw the French flag lowered to half-mast — this greatly intrigued us. Around 10 p.m. the train arrived at Colomb-Béchar. Here at the station we ate a cooked supper and were again herded into the cars. We were not allowed to speak with the comrades who were already working here; we only saw them from a distance. We don't know now what they will do with us — whether they'll take us further or leave us here.

November 14, 1941, Friday
Finally at 8 in the morning we were unloaded from the cars, counted, and, surrounded by gendarmes, led to the travailleurs' barracks. Here our presence was checked again by name, and the gendarmes left us; we were given a cup of coffee each and a slice of bread, which we could only eat after being placed on the platforms of the train that was waiting for us on the newly built railway line toward Morocco. A detachment of Arabs took charge of guarding the train, and they don't allow anyone to move even a step away, even for natural needs. Some provisions and beds and tables for our commanders were also loaded onto the platforms. Every one of us just shakes his head and says, "well, they've really got us now." The train carried us about 5 km and stopped in the open desert. Here it unloaded us and moved on. Trucks brought our tents and all the camp equipment. And we were immediately put to work setting up the camp. After lunch, spirits improved and some began cracking jokes one after another. We were ordered to rake the assembly ground, and while working one man asked another, "what are you doing?" and he answered, "raking the Sahara" — and a whole series of other such exchanges. We were issued blankets, but we have to sleep on the bare ground — there's nothing to lay down as bedding. Apparently they're supposed to bring us mats. Toward morning we froze badly, because the nights are very cold and the days aren't exactly warm either.

November 15, 1941, Saturday
We work all day building the camp. The boys gathered dry grasses, which grow very sparsely and are as tough as
wire, to make bedding so we could sleep at least somewhat. In the evening the choir gathered and sang a few songs, which our commander liked very much. According to rumor, he is a lieutenant of the French air force who tried to flee to General de Gaulle's army and was caught. As punishment he was sent out here for 5 months — and it seems we are to be here that long too.

November 16, 1941, Sunday
Until noon we finish building the camp, and in the afternoon some of the men are issued boots, since there aren't yet enough for everyone. A canteen has also been set up for us, though there is still little in it. From somewhere a French newspaper made its way into the camp, informing us that General Huntziger, the Vichy government's Minister of War, died in an airplane accident. And now we guess that the flags at half-mast that we saw during our journey were marking his death.

November 23, 1941, Sunday
For a whole week I didn't have a free moment to note anything down for memory. Only today, Sunday, do we have a day off from work, though everyone is still busy working for themselves — doing laundry, bathing, and fixing up the tents. On Monday we were taken 2 km from the camp to gather stones and pile them up beside the railway track. We're supposed to collect 1 cubic meter of stones per person, but no one managed it, and on average it came out to about ½ cubic meter per person — and yet they're satisfied with us. We work from 7 to 12 and from 1 to 5. The food is very bad, as it's constantly peas and groats. We got potatoes once, one tablespoon per person, as a dessert. After eating these peas we get terrible stomach bloating, and the tents are full of flatulence all night long. The stench is
something awful. All week we work just as we did on Monday, with nothing special happening, until Saturday, November 22, when during work Lt. Janicki, who commands the Poles working in Colomb-Béchar, came to see us. He spoke only with the chief for about 5 minutes, and afterward the chief told us that the lieutenant is trying to get the drivers pulled out of the camp and that the matter is progressing well.
After returning from work a surprise awaited us — namely, the lieutenant, the camp commander, arranged a bathhouse for us, and we now have a small shower for 4 people. French newspapers report on the English offensive in Cyrenaica and that on the first day they advanced 75 km. This afternoon Soja and one of his comrades from Colomb-Béchar sneaked into the camp, and from them we learned what Lt. Janicki had told us yesterday, as well as about the life of our comrades in Colomb-Béchar. Soja was afraid to leave the camp in case the Arabs guarding us shot him, so they went to the lieutenant, the camp commander, to ask permission to speak with us, as if they had just arrived, but the lieutenant refused, since he has such orders, though he gave them the address of his commander in Colomb-Béchar so he could grant them permission. So Soja went off as if nothing had happened. The camp commander is a very good man; only the sergeants oppress us at every turn — for example, today the bread ration sold in the canteen, run by one of the sergeants, a Russian, is given to us as one loaf for 8 men instead of for 6, and only today did this come to light, and the lieutenant gave the guilty parties a proper dressing-down. Our belongings, which had been taken from us during the arrest in Saïda, were handed back to us this week, a few each day, but without the documents, which the camp commander kept. Mine were returned without
any shortfall, but many others are missing a lot of things. Both yesterday evening and today the choir held singing evenings, which the camp commander likes very much. Yesterday, for instance, we sang praying for Libya, that things would go well for the English, and for the French we sang a funeral march. This week I shaved my head, since conditions here aren't suitable for keeping hair clean, and besides, it's been falling out badly.

November 24, 1941, Monday
Today we worked on the road, and during the lunch break a car drove up and stopped near us, and two men jumped out and began repairing it. We recognized two of our comrades from Colomb-Béchar. Only two of our men managed to approach the car and grab some bread from it, because the lieutenant, the camp commander, arrived and would not let anyone near it. Our comrades, after tinkering with the engine a bit, drove off, and the lieutenant noted down the car's number. Five more comrades joined the company, men who had been working on farms around Saïda or had been in the hospital, and who had nothing to do with our escape.

November 25, 1941, Tuesday
The food is very poor, and today, for instance, they gave us peas full of worms, and some sweet, unpleasant-tasting tubers, so the whole company refused the peas, and several dozen men took the tubers but later threw them away. So we decided to work very slowly, and that's what we did, because over the whole afternoon we hauled only 5 wheelbarrow loads per person,
whereas normally we hauled 15 to 20. When the camp commander noticed this, he began asking why we didn't want to work, so we told him through the interpreter. He replied in turn that it wasn't too little, and as proof showed us the urine sample, which was indeed quite abundant, so we had to explain to him that it was because the food wasn't nourishing. That's where the matter ended, and the lieutenant left for Colomb-Béchar. On the other hand, supper was good, with a dessert like never before, and it was promised that there would be coffee every day after supper. Several of our men were under Arab guard in Colomb-Béchar and learned that the man who brought us bread yesterday reportedly got 8 days' arrest. Others say that we won't be here long and are to be moved to barracks in Colomb-Béchar, and that Mr. Czapski is expected to arrive soon.

November 26, 1941, Wednesday
The food is much better. They give us more vegetables, and for lunch we had two desserts — oranges and radishes. We have no news from Libya. The camp was visited by the commander of the travailleur camps, some French major, and at roll call we were told he was very satisfied with the visit, and that as a result we are to receive 2 mess-tins of wine per week as a supplement.

November 27, 1941, Thursday
The French authorities have not allowed any newspaper into the camp since the start of the English offensive in Libya, whereas before that the newspaper "Echo d'Oran" was posted in the tent
where the canteen is located. Nevertheless, news made it into the camp that the English had taken Derna in Cyrenaica.

November 28, 1941, Friday
A small incident occurred with the Russian sergeant. It happened like this. One of our comrades received very torn trousers from the stores, so he went to the Russian sergeant, since he's the quartermaster, for several days asking for an exchange, but without success. Today this comrade (Ludwik) went again with his request, arguing that yesterday Senior Sgt. Benal received new trousers even though his old ones were whole, and he [Ludwik] was asking for those old ones — but again he was refused. A quarrel broke out between them in which Ludwik apparently said that it wouldn't be long before he'd stop tormenting us, as a result of which he was written up for a disciplinary report and punished with 4 days' arrest. When the lieutenant told him this, he replied, "Merci, mon lieutenant, tout pour la patrie," and left. Two hours later, when he ran into the Russian, the latter told him he didn't want to be the cause of his suffering, and tore up the report. As for another sergeant, whom they call "the chameleon," they mock him this way: the sergeant ordered them to make 400 bricks a day, and they threaten him that when the English come, he'll have to make 1,400 a day.

November 29, 1941, Saturday
The camp commander left in the morning for Colomb-Béchar, and he was replaced by a senior sergeant of Austrian origin, and from morning a fly must have landed on his nose, because everything displeased him. But it was only after lunch that things turned bad, because the supply platoon, which never stands for the afternoon roll call, was a little slow about it this time, and the Austrian flew into a rage over this and began cursing them as "cochons polonais" (Polish pigs), and for some unknown reason threw one man into the arrest area, which is under the open sky in a strictly designated spot, and for the night he was given only one blanket. It must be devilishly cold there, because we freeze even in our tents under 3 blankets. The company was out at work when this happened and only learned about it all after returning. Right away the chief, Benal, went with the interpreter to the camp commander, who had already returned, and laid out the matter for him; the commander for his part asked that it be let go, since the Austrian is sick and prone to such outbursts, and that it wouldn't happen again.

November 30, 1941, Sunday
Somehow the day passed quietly, although the food is much worse since we don't work — until, unexpectedly, right before supper, a roll call was ordered. The Austrian arrives at the roll call furious, shouting something in French, and goes straight to Budzyński, who was a little drunk and swaying in the ranks but not causing any trouble, and ordered him locked up in the arrest area.
The commander said that some commission had found large, unbroken stones in the piles that we build, and that tomorrow one platoon would go and break them up for free, at which the Austrian began shouting that this was sabotage and more besides, which caused the company to start howling. The commander immediately responded to this by saying that for two days we would get no wine. At that point Sgt. Haukiewicz stepped forward and asked the lieutenant for permission to speak on behalf of the company, but the lieutenant refused and told the sergeant
to come see him after the roll call, and ordered the company to go to supper. The men dispersed, but no one went to supper, as a sign of protest, because he did not want to speak with us, and because the lieutenant had said at roll call that we, the workers, have no rights here — so nothing to say — only obligations. By a lucky chance, an officer from French intelligence happened to be at the camp conducting interrogations, and Hankiewicz went to him. He assured us that we are still considered combatants and asked us not to cause trouble and to work, and that everything would change, and that the Austrian's harassment would not happen again. As for those who were arrested, Budzyński is to be released in the morning once he sobers up, but Durkowski is held for a different matter — intelligence ordered his arrest, so he won't be released. Among other things, this officer reportedly said that they had given us 2 days to leave, but we hadn't taken advantage of it. I don't know now what that's supposed to mean. Most of the men went to supper after Hankiewicz's conversation with the intelligence officer, but a few were found who still reproached Hankiewicz for having handled the matter badly.

December 1, 1941
It seems that with the last day of November the evil fate left us, because the Austrian apologized to us, and because he asked the camp commander to lift the ban on issuing wine, which he had caused.

December 2, Tuesday - December 5, 1941, Friday
The Austrian must have heard a proper lecture from the French intelligence lieutenant, because he changed completely and sits as if he weren't there, no longer driving anyone
to work. Time passes in daily monotonous labor, and in the evenings there's nothing to do, because the lighting in the barracks (tent) — carbide lamps — is very poor, and it's cold. The mornings, too, are very cold, and there's frost on the tents and the railway ties. On Friday evening Dzida and Czarnecki arrived at the camp. The former had been held under arrest in Tlemcen for trying to escape to England, and the latter had been a cook in Mascara for our officers. The officers are held under arrest and the cadets under guard in a hotel, so Czarnecki claims.

December 6, 1941, Saturday
Today is the opening of the first section of the Mediterranean-Niger railway line. As a mark of this, the "Lux Torpédo" express passed over the track we're working on. We're supposed to earn 4 francs 50 centimes a day, but as for wages — nothing has come, and nothing keeps coming. Through the efforts of our chief, Benal, the French lieutenant agreed to grant credit at the canteen up to 4 francs a day, for which one can get 2 sandwiches — which one absolutely has to buy, or else go hungry. We asked that they sell plain bread instead of sandwiches, since it would be cheaper, but the Russian keeps making sandwiches because he earns more on them, despite the camp commander's orders. We complained about this to the camp commander, and he gave the "Russian fellow" a proper scolding. At the daily orders, the camp commander informed us that the railway had sent 3 francs a day for those working on the track and 4.50 for those in the camp, so on
behalf of the company he did not accept this money, asking instead for a raise. As it happens, he and the Austrian are both very courteous to us these days.

December 7, 1941, Sunday
In the morning Baron Kosiński, who is currently in Colomb-Béchar, arrived at the camp and brought several letters and news that there is money waiting for us with Lt. Janicki, so in the afternoon the chief, accompanied by one of the overseers, went into town and brought back a little news and 10,000 francs.

Camp in the Sahara, December 1941 December 8, 1941, Monday
It is not Saturday but today that is the opening of the railway line — we only saw the first passenger train and a few airplanes that came for the ceremony. Chief Benal Rudolf informed the camp commander that we had received money from the Polish Red Cross, so that he wouldn't think we were plotting something behind his back, and distributed the money to us. We received 50 francs each, and it's badly needed, because we were hungry and had no money to buy bread.

December 9, 1941, Tuesday
As a second breakfast, called here "casse-croûte," we've been getting soup for several days, but today the soup was bad, so the Austrian went into the camp and got us a can of sardines for every 3 men. This Austrian, after the last incident, is unrecognizable, he's improved so much.

December 10, 1941, Wednesday
Today we were told that we're to get more than 7 francs a day for work on the track. Our output is more than ½ cubic meter per person. News reaches us of the outbreak of the American-Japanese war.

December 11, 1941, Thursday
Out here in this wilderness we no longer think about the needs of a civilized man or any entertainments like those in Saïda, or about women, whom I haven't seen in a month and a half; my mind is occupied with only two questions of the day: whether there will be bread in the canteen today, and whether the platoon will manage the required 50 m of stones. As for politics, we are passive, and the news that reaches us is so uncertain and contradictory that it's not worth bothering with it and wearing out one's nerves.

December 12, 1941, Friday
Today while working my hand swelled up from the strain of breaking stones.

December 13, 1941, Saturday
The doctor forbade me from coming to work, because my right hand is swollen and an abscess is forming between my thumb and index finger.
December 14, 1941, Sunday
Last night I didn't close my eyes because of the pain in my hand. In the afternoon the doctor lanced the abscess and now it doesn't hurt so much.

December 15, 1941, Monday
From morning a strong wind blows with sand, and it's cold. The company went to work but worked only half the day, because it was impossible to continue. I have leave until Thursday. News came from Colomb-Béchar that the Russians have apparently taken Kiev and Smolensk, and that Poland has declared war on Japan.

December 16, 1941, Tuesday
I lie in the tent doing nothing, and my hand no longer hurts, although it's still swollen. So as not to be bored, I bought myself a couple of kilos (a full bread bag) of dates and I chew on them — I want to sicken myself of them, since I love them so much. For some time now I've had a partner in food matters, one Dziadkowicz, a man very cunning at gathering provisions. The weather has improved somewhat, as it rained at night and the wind no longer carries clouds of sand, though it's very cold. The company took blankets to work to cover themselves while breaking stones. In today's orders we were reminded that those excused from work by the doctor receive no pay at all, while those excused from heavy labor will receive 2 francs a day. I'm writing nonsense and forgot the most important thing — namely, since Saturday, that is December 13, we have had a French flag and a Polish flag in the camp. They are arranged so that the much larger French flag is on top, and below it, half the size, the Polish one, with the inscription "Honor and Fatherland." At the foot of the flagpole, comrade Szymański arranged stones and painted them to form the Polish eagle and a double-edged axe with the letters E.F., which is the emblem of the present-day French State.

December 17, 1941, Wednesday
The weather is beautiful and warm — makes you want to live. Rumors are going around the camp that our officers have been taken from Mascara somewhere near Colomb-Béchar.

December 18, 1941, Thursday
Nothing new.

Work and camp at Colomb-Béchar December 19, 1941, Friday
Even though my hand hurts and is still swollen, I went to work, because it's very boring in the camp.

December 20, 1941, Saturday
With the casse-croûte, the story from December 9, 1941 repeated itself, when the company refused to eat the soup and the Austrian brought sprats. Chief Benal read us a letter from Mr. Czapski, in which he informs us
that at Christmas we'll get 15 francs each, and promises to come to the camp in the coming days.

December 21, 1941, Sunday
In the morning we dig small trenches on the camp square, meant to serve as benches for Christmas Eve. After lunch, smallpox vaccination. In the evening the camp was visited by two Legion non-commissioned officers with one woman. One of these NCOs was a Pole who had served in the Foreign Legion for over 10 years. He asked us to sing him a Polish song. Of course we obliged his request. The guests listened, very moved, and when we finished he thanked us and raised the cry "Long live France, long live Poland!" after which the choir sang the Marseillaise and "Jeszcze Polska" [Poland Is Not Yet Lost]. After the Marseillaise, the Austrian was the first to start shouting "Marsz, marsz, Dąbrowski."

December 22, 1941, Monday
December 23, 1941, Tuesday
The camp commander informed us that the priest whose arrival we had expected on the second day of the holidays would not be coming. Great bitterness in the company that even this is denied us. The altar had even been prepared, and now nothing will come of it. The chief apparently sent a telegram to Algiers for a Polish priest, but we don't believe he could come, since it's too late. The weather is foul, with a strong wind and very cold.

December 24, 1941, Wednesday, Christmas Eve
From morning the weather is very overcast; around 11 it turns to rain and we stop work. At noon it clears up a little, so the French sent us back to work, but just as we started working, the rain drove us back into the tents again. We thought nothing would come of our shared Christmas Eve, but God had not forgotten us, because around 4 o'clock the rain stopped, so we hastily began building tables. The Austrian treated the company to a pack of cigarettes and two oranges per person, issued to us by the canteen. We began Christmas Eve at 5 o'clock with a prayer, after which Chief Benal R. spoke first, offering good wishes to everyone.
The Austrian was the most senior man in camp, since the lieutenant had gone to Colomb-Béchar, so the chief offered him wishes first, then the sergeants, and then everyone began wishing each other well — the Austrian gave his wishes to each man individually. Then we ate a modest meal and the choir sang a few carols. While we were eating, a caravan of camels and a flock of sheep passed by the camp. The Austrian bought a fine ram from this caravan for 5.50 francs and gave it to the company as a gift. In gratitude the company tossed him up in the air, and then the sergeants too. The sergeants had promised us a cup of wine each for Christmas Eve. After everyone dispersed, the canteen sold wine for cash to those who had it, and on credit, since few had money, as we still hadn't received our wages or the 15 francs from the Red Cross. The brotherhood got a bit tipsy and sang carols, and somehow forgot about our situation and about our loved ones. But there turned out to be one swine who spoiled these moments, because Fiedosiejew, drunk, stabbed Fiszer's hand with a knife; Podniesiński stood up in his defense and smashed with his boot the head of
the attacker. So unfortunately that they could not stop the bleeding, and an ambulance called from Colomb-Béchar took him to the hospital.

December 25, 1941, Thursday, Christmas Day
In the morning I was called to the office, where I received 100 francs from Wicek Zarychta of Adrar. This Wicek is a very decent fellow and understands our situation well. I paid off a few debts and gave a little to friends, and I don't go hungry today, since the canteen isn't giving credit.

December 26, 1941, Friday, Second Day of the Holidays
They won't let us celebrate. We were ordered to dig a pit for a water tank. Supposedly we're to be paid for a full day's work even though we only worked 3 hours. At night the guard posts were doubled, and our overseers walked among the tents all night.

December 27, 1941, Saturday
News reached the camp from somewhere that the travailleurs in Kenadsa had mutinied, and that the Arabs refused to sell sheep, fleeing with them into the mountains, which is why we were guarded so closely at night. Political news keeps coming in various forms, but increasingly better — about the Germans retreating, about an uprising in Poland, and about the English advancing in Libya.

December 28, 1941, Sunday
Rumors are going around that Mr. Czapski is in Colomb-Béchar.

December 29, 1941, Monday
Mr. Czapski, long awaited, arrived at the camp. It happened during work, which we stopped for a few minutes to give our report. Mr. Czapski stayed at the camp until late evening, spoke with everyone who wanted something from him, and was present for the lowering of the flag, which the Austrian reported to him, standing remarkably at attention and addressing Mr. Czapski as "mon colonel." The camp commander, on the other hand, behaved very badly, since during the report and during the conversation with Mr. Czapski he kept his hands in his pockets and generally behaved dismissively, which the Austrian pointed out to him. Also arriving with Mr. Czapski were Mr. Przesmycki, his deputy, and Mr. Borowski, delegate of the Commission for Aid to Poles.

December 30, 1941, Tuesday
Somehow we're working badly; the camp commander is dissatisfied with this and even locked one man up for slow work, but released him after the intervention of Chief Benal. Mr. Czapski, with his companions, paid us 50 francs each.

December 31, 1941, Wednesday
We work much better than yesterday.

January 1, 1942, Thursday
The New Year was announced in the camp with twelve strikes of a pickaxe, after which good wishes were offered to the camp commander and the choir sang 3 songs (Polish Banners on the Kremlin, and two carols).
There was drinking afterward until morning, since the canteen had brought in wine with special permission. From morning a sirocco blows, so that one doesn't feel like getting up or washing. Before evening Mr. Czapski arrived at the camp. He settled a few more matters with several men, and on leaving offered us New Year's wishes. Among other things he said that the road ahead of us is long and hard, but the goal is great, and for its sake we must endure.

January 2, 1942, Friday
A sirocco with snow and rain doesn't let us work. We sleep, doing nothing, and don't even feel like going out for food. During the night the sirocco tore one tent, so we had to take in its occupant.

January 3, 1942, Saturday
The wind has died down a little, but rain falls in the morning and we don't go to work.
Those found unfit went to Colomb-Béchar for a medical board. In the afternoon the weather improved and we work near the camp.

January 5, 1942, Monday — Nothing new
January 6, 1942, Tuesday, Epiphany
A new overseer arrives from Saïda and brings news of the death of Cpl. Henryk Olszewski in the hospital in Saïda. He was buried in that same town. Present at the funeral was one of the Polish officers (probably Capt. Szewalski), delegations of Legion veterans, travailleurs, and local combatants. The travailleurs bought a wreath. According to the doctors' opinion, the late Henio Olszewski died of diabetes, but those who knew him from Warsaw, like Mietek Grzegorzewski, claim it was a family history of consumption [tuberculosis]. Poor Henio marked our road with his grave. As I later learned, the funeral took place on January 5, 1942, so he died on January 2 or 3, 1942.

January 7, 1942, Wednesday
During the night a sirocco blew with unusual force, and because of this we don't go to work until 9 o'clock. Just before lunch the sirocco picked up again, and during lunch it kept piling sand into our mess tins until it drove us back to camp.

January 7, 1942, Wednesday - January 8, 1942, Thursday
January 9, 1942, Friday
A certain group of Poles was supposed to leave for the new location of our camp somewhere near Bou Arfa, but for some unknown reason it did not come about. In the evening, by the light of carbide lamps, a ceremony was held to honor the memory of the late H. Olszewski. The chief said a few words and ordered a minute of silence, after which the choir sang a funeral march. To close, everyone sang the camp song.

January 11, 1942, Sunday
After lunch I went with Zawistowski to the castle that can be seen from the camp. But upon reaching it, the "castle" turned out to be a rock formation of stones resembling castle ruins.

January 12 and 13, 1942, Monday and Tuesday — Nothing.
January 14, 1942, Wednesday
We are preparing to change our camping location. The camp commander allowed one man from each tent to go to Colomb-Béchar for shopping, but in the afternoon many others went without permission too — myself and Zawistowski among them. Our goal was to help Kotala carry back the purchases. We met him in town, drank a little, took part of the purchases, and returned safely, but Kotala went missing somewhere and didn't get back to camp until 5 in the morning, without Wołk's briefcase and Karwaciński's boots, which he had borrowed from them. He went back into town once more for them, but without success.

January 15, 1942, Thursday
In the morning a train was brought for us and carried us 60 km north toward Bou Arfa. At the new site we have a few mud huts where the kitchen, the office, and lodgings for the overseers have been set up. We live in tents. On the 16th and 17th we set up the camp. Under the tents we dig a half-meter pit, which gives us more room in the tent and makes it much warmer. My tent was so unfortunate that half the pit had to be chiseled out of rock. I badly strained my hand doing this work.

January 18, 1942, Sunday
I lie in bed all day, since I've caught a chill and my hand hurts.

January 19, 1942, Monday, and January 20, 1942, Tuesday
After a lot of fuss, I was excused from work on the track. Instead I work peeling carrots and turnips for the lunches, since that's all they feed us here. As payment for a full day's work, they credit me with 2 hours.

February 21, 1942, Wednesday
I go back to work on the track. Chief Benal and Sgt. Porwal have some conflict with Baron Kosiński, Mieczysław, who is the provisions quartermaster, so they asked the camp commander to remove him from this post, but the commander refused. Wanting to protest this, they worked alongside us instead of supervising. The company, seeing that the chief was in conflict with the camp commander, slowed the work pace in order to press its own demands for better food and for the canteen to be allowed to make purchases in town more often. The Austrian, seeing the slowed pace of work, began to drive us on, but eventually gave up and went to the camp commander to complain. In the evening he took heart drops from the doctor and showed up completely drunk for the evening roll call, but it passed without incident.

January 22, 1942, Thursday
The company continues its slow work.

52
January 23, 1942, Friday
Seeing our slow work, the camp commander ordered the chief to assemble the company; he himself alerted the Arabs who still guard us, and only then, revolver in hand, began speaking sharply to us, announcing that if the company did not work at the rate of 1 cubic meter per person every day, he would not allow wine and meat to be issued, and would close the canteen.
By noon the pace of work had not improved, so there was no wine at lunch, and the camp commander began provoking some kind of incident. After lunch the company again worked slowly. A few minutes before the end of work the camp commander came over and began accosting several men for working too slowly, but they gave way to him — until he ordered Tomaszewski to light a fire and get to the wheelbarrows; the latter did not carry out the order, for which the camp commander personally marched him off to the arrest area. In the company someone shouted, "Don't let this happen, let's drop our tools!" and the whole company left its tools and started walking toward the camp, but the Austrian blocked their path and asked them to gather up the tools for him. The company turned back and gathered the tools.
While this was happening, the camp commander summoned the Arabs and blocked the company's path, but the company paid no attention to this and walked past unhindered. Only once the company had dispersed to the tents did he post guards by the tents, one Arab per two tents, and forbade anyone to leave them under threat of being shot at. When supper was announced, no one came out for it, and so the hunger strike began. We feel this was not the right course to take, but it's already done.

January 24, 1941, Saturday
We go hungry, lying in the tents. The weather is foul, as the sirocco blows with great force, so we wouldn't have gone to work anyway. In the company, the cooks and those on duty, like the storekeeper and the orderly, do not go hungry, because the latter is very afraid. The cooks secretly brought us a small piece of bread with onion at noon, and a cup of soup for supper. They did this so openly with the soup that it seems the French must have noticed.

January 25, 1942, Sunday
During the night more Arabs arrived by truck, and around 10 in the morning the major who commands the travailleurs arrived from Colomb-Béchar. He summoned Chief Benal and spoke with him. After this conversation the chief assembled the company and announced that the major had said he could not improve the food, and rejected our other demands as well; he only promised that we would get the money we were asking for, for which he gave his word of honor, and that if he could find another water tank somewhere, we would have Saturday afternoons free for laundry. As for Tomaszewski, he said that the man had disobeyed an order and would be court-martialed for it. As for the company, if it wants to starve or work, that's up to it — he would not force us. Here he reminded us that one condition of the armistice was that Poles be handed over to the Germans, but that they [the French] are hiding us — yet if we cause trouble, the German Commission will find out about us and take us away. Hearing this, Sgt. Gbur immediately ran to the major asking to be sent to Germany, adding that there were plenty of volunteers here for that. The major reportedly promised to send forms to fill out for 53
the commission. Sgt. Gbur claims he's doing this in order to have a chance to escape along the way. The political situation is such that it's not clear whether it would be better to escape to the English from Africa or from Europe. As he left, the major took Tomaszewski with him under arrest, and also took Chief Benal to the colonel in Colomb-Béchar to discuss our case. After the major left, the company took food, and those who had most urged the hunger strike now sit like frightened rabbits.

January 26, 1942, Monday
In today's orders it was announced that as long as work output remains at 1 cubic meter per person, we will not get wine and the canteen will not be opened. The company commander punished Tomaszewski with 8 days' solitary confinement, separated from the group. But despite such a strict order, the output we achieve today is 1/3 of what's required, because the stone we haul is available only in very small quantities, at a distance of 300 m from the track. I have the impression that they won't be opening the canteen — where I can buy dates, which I'm mad about — anytime soon.

January 27, 1942, Tuesday
Output is the same as yesterday, but at the request of the Polish and French overseers, the commander allowed wine to be issued and the canteen to open. That very evening I spent over 50 francs on purchases. Tonight Chief Benal arrived back from Colomb-Béchar, where he had been summoned to the Second Bureau rather than to the colonel, as he had been told. There, apparently, he got a proper dressing-down for stirring up the company. On the same train, Sierota and Ołota departed for Colomb-Béchar.

January 28, 1942, Wednesday
Today a new commander arrived at the camp, in the rank of captain — an older man, very fierce-looking, with a bristling mustache. He limps slightly, and on his chest are many decorations. On the night train, 3 Poles arrived: Piekarski, who once escaped from Saïda and was subsequently imprisoned, and two others who claimed to be officers, but those who came to our company from Morocco know them and say they are only privates.

January 29, 1942, Thursday
The new company commander is partly taking over the company.

January 30, 1942, Friday
January 31, 1942, Saturday
Today's train brought us 30 sheep. All these sheep escaped during the night, and when the comrade who tends the flock tried to bring them back, the Arabs turned him back at gunpoint. In the morning the commander sent the Arabs out searching and threatened that if the sheep weren't found, they would pay for it. Around 10 o'clock an Arab brought back one, and by noon they had brought back 28 head, along with one torn hide. The Arabs claim jackals devoured the sheep. On examining the hide, however, it turned out the skin had been cut with a knife.

54
The situation with water is very bad, because what they bring us is too little for washing clothes and ourselves, and on top of that the lack of soap is badly felt, as is the lack of any container for washing. Many in the company already have lice. Today at noon I stole a little water and washed my laundry, not with soap but with a washing powder called here "Lessive" — it removes dirt very well, but it also loosens the threads, and a shirt won't survive more than 3 washes, and we only have one shirt each. So everyone limits himself to washing once a month. So how, under these conditions, could one avoid getting lice.

(End of the First Notebook of Stefan Pisulewski's Diary)
