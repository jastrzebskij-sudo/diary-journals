May 17, 1940, Friday
I was at the doctor's after my leave; he didn't examine me, just took my word for it. In the evening we arrange a feast at our quarters consisting of scrambled eggs made from 20 eggs and a tomato salad.

May 18, 1940, Saturday
Cpl. Dzierzyk still hasn't returned and the command has sent out a wanted notice. I am on duty as the company's duty NCO.

May 19, 1940, Sunday
The company received light machine guns and is to maintain anti-landing readiness for the whole week.

May 20, 1940, Monday
Ever worse news from the front. The Germans took the cities of Amiens and Arras. Świątkowski and I cook scrambled eggs from 40 eggs. I sent Świątkowski to the field for chives and he brought me onions, claiming that this was chives, only old. I fried the eggs and they were very tasty. In the evening, after the news bulletin in which there was news of the taking of Arras, I am very despondent and keep thinking about Irenka Ciemniakówna, because the poor girl is surely in the area now occupied by the Germans.

19 May 21, 1940, Tuesday
Outpost exercises. By the captain's order I am the outpost commander, and the captain observed me because he wanted to get to know me. The exercise turned out well overall.

May 22, 1940, Wednesday
The company digs anti-aircraft trenches.

May 23, 1940, Thursday
The part of the company that had already been vaccinated against typhus during my leave received the second dose. In the evening, news about the halting of the German offensive and the recapture of the city of Arras. Dzierzyk still hasn't returned.

May 24, 1940, Friday
We're loafing about alongside those who were vaccinated.

May 25, 1940, Saturday
I am transferred to the 2nd platoon and in connection with this I change quarters. As a farewell to the old quarters we arrange a little scrambled-egg feast. I make the new quarters nice for myself.

May 26, 1940, Sunday
In the afternoon I learn that 2nd Lt. Micherdziński (Pigułka) is leaving us and that command of the company is being taken over by Capt. Ziemski. I was very pleased about this, because I had heard much about his combat merits, but I feel sorry for the former commander because I had already grown close to him.
As a farewell, our comrade Kanar takes our photograph. Świątkowski made a fool of himself and went to ask if the girls who work at the silk factory right next door would be photographed with us. They did not agree and were even offended, and Dulemba (the cook from France) immediately felt the need to insult them. However, two girls unknown to us passed by and they did get photographed with us.

May 27, 1940, Monday
We prepare to depart. In the afternoon, departure by bus to Orange. On saying goodbye the French were very sad and some Frenchwomen cried. At the train station in Orange the professional soldiers receive an advance of 100 francs. Departure from Orange at about 8 p.m.

May 28, 1940, Tuesday
We travel through Lyon, Bourges, Orléans. At Vierzon station two trains from opposite directions pull up at one platform. One, full of singing, is carrying Polish troops heading to the front, and the other, sad, is carrying evacuees from Belgium. After a moment the singing quiets down, and the Poles, having learned who is on the train standing opposite, carry bread, tripe stew, and condensed milk to the carriages and share what they can with the refugees. The Belgians gladly accept. After some time the troops depart, warmly bid farewell by the evacuees. In Orléans we meet a transport of Polish troops (artillery).

May 29, 1940, Wednesday
We arrive in Versailles at 3 a.m. I stay behind to unload the battalion's food supply depot. Then I set off for the new quarters by military vehicle, but the driver is Polish. On the way I passed by the famous Palace of Versailles. At our quarters we learn sad news from the front about the French retreating and the capitulation of Belgium.

May 30, 1940, Thursday
We settle in at the quarters and draw equipment and uniforms. I am assigned to the platoon of Lt. Osiecki. With me in the second tank is Lt. Helik, and the deputy platoon commander is Officer Cadet Sokół Szachil.

May 31, 1940, Friday
We turn in the equipment issued yesterday. Engineer Kaczkowski and I go for a walk in the evening hours, and along the way we stop for tomato salad. 1 kg of tomatoes costs 10 francs. At another café in lower Chateaufort we meet Sgt. Suma with young Ziemski, my captain's brother, both of them completely drunk. The little walk to the forest outside town was very pleasant.

June 1, 1940, Saturday
We receive our pay. In the evening hours I go with two senior riflemen to bathe in a stream that we had heard flows nearby. After a long search we find it and choose ourselves a suitable spot in some park right in front of a very beautiful palace. In general the area here is very beautiful. At night I go on patrol.

June 2, 1940, Sunday
For the first time in my life I fire a cannon. They are very accurate.
In the afternoon I went out into the town and met Cpl. Helik, and went with him to a café. In the café were Kurcz, Sgt. Suma, Cpl. Leńczyk, Jodłowski, Komar and a few others sitting in the company of French airmen. Drinking began. I lost about 200 francs then. Kurcz started a brawl with some civilian. And Helik apparently shouted "Heil Hitler." I was no longer present for the brawl because I was very drunk and had left earlier.

June 3, 1940, Monday
At the morning report the corporal gave a sermon to the drunkards, because yesterday's brawl had spread widely; apparently there is to be an investigation. In the afternoon the corporal ordered all the professional soldiers to pay for their board two weeks in advance, and I was left penniless. The corporal laughed that now there would be nothing left to drink with.
At noon, a German air raid on Versailles and the airfield next to us.

June 4 to June 8
While waiting for equipment we go to exercises. In the evenings, with the engineer Kaczkowski, we go every day for tomato salad.

June 9, Sunday
The chaplain was late for Mass because he was hearing general confession for the 10th Armored Cavalry Brigade. He was very worked up and delivered a patriotic sermon. In the afternoon I receive my equipment. My driver is Cpl. Widło Roman.

June 10
We familiarize ourselves with the equipment. In the evening I take a bath in the stream. Paris is being evacuated. And the sight of fleeing cars has a negative effect on the army's morale.

June 11. We move with our equipment into the area of our post. The political situation is very serious. Italy has declared war on the Allies.

June 23, 1940, Sunday (date uncertain)
(Location: near Avignon)
I stopped writing because of the fast-moving events, but I will try to summarize them so I can keep writing. I don't remember any dates, so I won't give them. One evening we were alerted and went to sleep by our equipment near Versailles, and the next day we move the equipment to the area of (Chateaufort, phonetically). Here, one day's stop, and we go to load onto a rail transport. By rail we go around Paris, and all the little suburban stations are completely deserted. From Paris we travel the line toward Dijon. A few stations before Dijon we turn east and travel all day. There, the whole transport nearly fell into German hands. Thanks to Capt. Ziemski's cunning, we escape back.
At a certain station not far from Dijon our company (i.e., 1st Company, 2nd Battalion) plunders a freight car of chocolate and pickles. We unload at Dijon. A French general arrived and gave us orders for the defense of that city.
They supplied us with fuel, but the captain got out of it and we drive at night toward Roanne. There we were again put to work to defend the city along a 30-km stretch, 1st Company. We took up positions defending the city. We stood there the whole night and day. During the day Italian planes bombed us.
In the evening the captain gave the order to withdraw, and as it later turned out, without the French's orders. We were to go by platoons to the town of Le Puy. In the town of Toy the French authorities arrested us, saying that Capt. Ziemski was under arrest; Lt. Osiecki was also arrested. Finally an understanding was reached and Lt. Osiecki was released, but we know nothing about the captain. The French ordered us to defend the small town of Ste Just, near Ste Etienne.
Here news reached us that we are handing over the equipment to the French and the Polish army is going to England. Part of the tanks we hand over at that location, and 6 go to Monistrol, and there is a misunderstanding there, because the French captain thought that we didn't want to give up the tanks but were fleeing with them to England.
Finally an understanding was reached and they gave us food. We drive to Le Puy by trucks, the whole unit led by Lt. Stanek. There the French gave us orders to go to Marseille.

Near Avignon our officers went to get their bearings on the situation, and apparently we are to head toward Bordeaux.
That is for now as regards events from the theater of war, but I had one personal incident with Sgt. Gbur which I nevertheless want to remember. While stationed at Roanne we received an order to prepare and service the tanks, because in an hour we were going into action. And the sergeant ordered my crew to bring coffee for himself and for his own crew, of course, and I refused. A row started; the sergeant wanted to put me on report for failure to carry out an order, but seeing that everyone was laughing at him, he gave up.
It is in difficult moments that one truly gets to know people, and so it was again with Sgt. Gbur. Now that we are leaving France today and losing our pay, he says that we should go back to Poland, under Russian partition, because he must think of his wife and old age, and in the army he'll never live to see a pension.
In Avignon we were supplied with fuel and provisions, and from Ste Cecile 3 Polish soldiers joined us, and we travel through Alès, Florac. In the area of Florac we pass through beautiful, high mountains. The journey is like a beautiful sightseeing trip. Then through Millau, Montauban to Toulouse. There, at the Polish consulate, they supply us with fuel and provisions and order us to head toward Foix, Perpignan, to the port of Vendres, right by the Spanish border on the Mediterranean Sea. They are directing us to such a small port because all the ports on the Atlantic are occupied by the Germans. The transport is led by Lt. Stanek; in the company there is great discontent over the long stops and the cooking of meals. Right before Perpignan Cpl. Wołk crashes the tractor into a lorry.

June 25, 1940, Tuesday
We arrive at Port Vendres. We find three Polish soldiers whose ship has already left. They tell us that 5 hours before our arrival an English ship departed which took 1,900 Poles from the 1st Division.

June 25, 1940, Wednesday
While waiting for a ship I tour the nearby surroundings of the port. The port is situated in the mountains, almost natural.
Guarded by coastal artillery. In the port stand 3 French destroyers and 3 French merchant ships.
While touring the beautiful rocky coast I bathe in the sea for the first time. The water is salty and soap won't lather at all, so you can't do laundry.
In the evening a Yugoslav freighter, the Petar, puts into port. The company waits for a ship on the harbor square, joined by stragglers from other Polish units and Polish civilians. 2 of them are even married with their wives. Our officers are paying a lot of attention to one of them, a young and pretty one. About 10 French tank crewmen join our company, wanting to keep fighting, but the French government won't allow them to leave. They are now learning Polish. The thought inevitably comes to mind: "what goes around comes around" (lit. "the scythe's turn has come for Maciek too"). We used to learn French, and now they [learn] Polish.
From the newspapers we learn of the conclusion of peace (a ceasefire) with the Germans, under which France loses ¾ of its territory, including Paris and the entire Atlantic coast.

June 27, Thursday
Rummaging through the cars standing on the harbor square, left behind by French airmen, I found a small bottle of ink, with which I am now beginning to write.
The whole day I lie about and ponder over this war. I have already been through 2 wars, or rather two acts of the European drama, and I haven't fired a single shot, because I haven't even seen a single "boche," as the French call the Germans. But I think that in this third, decisive act, I really must do something. Toward evening we learn that we will still have to wait a few more days for a ship, so I decided to build myself a tent. I found myself a buddy and out of a tent-cloth we made ourselves a tent and enlarged it with tarpaulins found in the aircrews' cars. I fenced the tent with posts and wrapped it with electric cable on the posts, and I fastened flowers to it (cacti, which grow here as commonly as thistles do in Poland).
In the evening the lieutenant gathered the company and put the whole company on anti-aircraft defense duty, because a few fools like Jodłowski, Fatyga, Rydlewski, and Dzięcioł were riding around town on motorcycles like madmen. The French gendarmerie even brought Rydlewski back to the camp. Sergeant Major Kobyliński demanded the confiscation of revolvers, because they had been threatened with being shot at. In general the army is very demoralized.

June 28, 1940, Friday
I got up at 10:30 in the morning; my buddy brought me breakfast in bed. A very strong wind from the sea kept me from shaving.
Right before dinner the officers ordered us to move the tents to another spot and to completely clear that plot. And so again I had work to do for some 3 hours. And afterward, again nothing to do! I lie in the tent and think, and thoughts of Irenka Ciemniakówna come to my mind most of all. I found so much heartfelt warmth in her that I cannot forget.
In the evening I go for a stroll into town, but I got very bored in town, so I went with two Poles into the mountains, because I feel better in the bosom of nature than among people. Returning from the walk I found at our post 2 Polish majors who were urging us to go to a camp where we would be treated as soldiers and not as internees; however, we have no trust in that kind of camp.
But whatever the authorities decide is what we have to do. Some slipped away, but those were Poles from France, whereas I don't know French and have no papers at all, so I'm afraid that it could end even worse for me.

June 29, Saturday
Reveille, by order of Lt. Stanek, is sounded for us at 7 o'clock by the cook, which drew down the wrath of the whole company upon him. At 9 o'clock we loaded onto trucks and drive to a camp 10 km from the port. Before the camp the gendarmerie greeted us with shoving and jostling. The company was outraged; Lt. Stanek also protested and was shoved about too. The lieutenant himself formed us up and led us to the barracks, because the company didn't want to go, and it nearly came to shooting.
General outrage at the lieutenant for having led us here. We look around the barracks — filth, squalor, and fleas. Our area borders the area where Poles are confined, and, as the gendarmes say, communists, and further on are Spaniards and various other nationalities. There are also women, fenced off by a triple row of wire, much denser, so that the men cannot get to them. There are women there, even pretty young girls, and children. After getting my bearings in the terrain I go back to our area and found a more decent barrack — it at least has a floor, because in the others it's sand, and at the slightest movement it kicks up dust so that the barrack goes dark. The whole camp is situated on the beach, and at midday it's hard to bear here — it's like the Sahara.
Before evening I go with Rydlewski to bathe in the sea. We were called up from the seashore to a formation at which the lieutenant announced to us that the colonel of the gendarmerie apologizes to us for the morning's incident, because he thought we were headed to a concentration camp, and that our pay would be issued, only we should conduct ourselves in soldierly fashion. In the evening we sing, which the French liked very much. The prayer was also sung together.

June 30, Sunday The company goes to the little church on the beach. A poor but very pleasant little church — it could barely hold 1 company. On top of that a few airmen arrived who are also awaiting departure, along with a few civilians. During the service we sang church hymns, and at the end, "Boże coś Polskę" [a Polish patriotic hymn]. Then the priest said a prayer in French for Poland and France. After the service the company marched back to quarters in formation of fours, singing. The French came out onto the road and watched us, because they generally don't sing. We showed them that we are soldiers, and they immediately began treating us differently — they even salute our officers now.
After lunch I enjoy a swim in the sea.

July 1, 1940.
Corporal Sadecki and I do our laundry in a nearby stream. The very clean, soft water, together with a very sunny day, helped me wash 3 shirts, 2 pairs of underpants, 2 towels, and 1 pair of socks — within 3 hours they were completely dry and neatly folded into squares. Corporal Sodecki was quite amazed at how cleanly they were washed. In the afternoon I take over duty as NCO of the day for the company. The company is only supposed to form up for roll call three times a day, and even that they don't want to do. I had to put in a lot of effort to get even half the company to assemble. The heat is unbearable — so much so that even sitting in the barrack without moving, everyone sweats until the sweat runs down them in streams.

July 2, 1940, Tuesday
A company of black soldiers from Sudan arrives at the camp. Black as tar, with hair on their heads like young lambs. One of them started combing his hair and couldn't pull the comb back out, even though the hair was no longer than half a centimeter — it was that thick and curly. They are very disciplined, only uneducated. In the evening we invited a few of them into our barrack. At first they were very shy, but once we treated them to wine, and made a little chain bracelet for one of them, they loosened up and talked freely, and we had no small amount of laughter at their expense. They come from Sudan, and have two or three wives each, or even more, depending on how rich a man is. They are of various faiths; several are Catholic, and these wear medallions and little crosses.
We laughed quite a lot when one of our friends showed them binoculars and they looked through them. When asked how old he was, one of them answered with fits of laughter, and finally said that maybe his father knew, but he didn't, because he was small then and didn't remember.
While out for a walk on the beach before evening, I saw a group of black soldiers sitting on the sand, when suddenly a French NCO came up with a camera and, without saying anything to them, wanted to photograph them, but he didn't succeed, because as soon as they saw him they scattered as if a bomb had fallen among them. After long negotiations he managed to persuade one of them to be photographed.
Rumors are going around the camp that the English bombed Berlin with 500 aircraft. That they bombed military targets in Poland and in France, and even bombed Paris. That the English, with the help of the Abyssinians, have nearly driven the Italians out of Abyssinia. And that Russia has seized Bessarabia and Bukovina from Romania, and Subcarpathian Rus' from Hungary.
