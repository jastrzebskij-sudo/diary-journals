June 12, 1941, Thursday, Corpus Christi

This feast, which in Poland we celebrate so solemnly, we honored here with a Holy Mass at which the choir sang. Besides us there were scouts and schoolchildren present. The feast is not observed here at all, so we too were put to work, being ordered to carry all the barracks equipment out to the square in front of the barracks for inspection. The men curse, calling the whole command a bunch of clowns and idiots, but they must obey. The inspection is conducted by the French. Matuszczak and Kowalski caught a young vulture or eagle in the ravine and we are keeping it in the hall; it has a good appetite, today it ate a medium-sized snake and a quarter kilo of meat. Today the police again brought in someone in handcuffs; he is known to us from Carpiagne. Michał Kosiński, some bankrupt aristocrat.

Newspaper clipping:
GOLDEN THOUGHTS
In war, he wins who makes the fewest mistakes.

June 13, 1941, Friday

Today we received the tropical helmets, long promised to us by the Red Cross, and nice dark glasses. Much joking and merriment resulted from this because nearly every one of us is wearing such an outfit for the first time in his life.

June 14, 1941, Saturday

The company commander held a briefing because a report had come from the water and forestry authority that Poles have been going to drink water from the forester's, and this has already happened twice, and he does not wish for it, and that apparently they were to have cut down several young trees from the nursery. The lieutenant berated the company, but witnesses testified that it was the travailleurs who had cut the young trees from the nursery, and that they would be identified, and for now the matter ended there.

June 15, 1941, Sunday

As usual the choir sang exceptionally well, and after dinner there was a football match against Saïda, which our side won 2-1.

June 16, 1941, Monday
Nothing special.

June 17, 1941, Tuesday

In the afternoon an inspection of the garrison by a French general, at which we too were present, as were the travailleurs. The general took particular interest in the Poles, and afterward called over the Polish officers, spoke with them, and shook their hands on the square in the presence of all the garrison's officers. He is said to have told us not to worry, because we will yet fight shoulder to shoulder with the French. During the inspection, the garrison commander, Major Fuzeau, our best friend, remarked to the general, pointing to the commander of the demobilized groups, our greatest enemy, cadet officer Gradyean, that he was "le surveillant," meaning the overseer.
