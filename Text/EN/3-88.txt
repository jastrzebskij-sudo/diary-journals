He replied in turn that it wasn't too little, and as proof showed us the urine sample, which was indeed quite abundant, so we had to explain to him that it was because the food wasn't nourishing. That's where the matter ended, and the lieutenant left for Colomb-Béchar. On the other hand, supper was good, with a dessert like never before, and it was promised that there would be coffee every day after supper. Several of our men were under Arab guard in Colomb-Béchar and learned that the man who brought us bread yesterday reportedly got 8 days' arrest. Others say that we won't be here long and are to be moved to barracks in Colomb-Béchar, and that Mr. Czapski is expected to arrive soon.

November 26, 1941, Wednesday
The food is much better. They give us more vegetables, and for lunch we had two desserts — oranges and radishes. We have no news from Libya. The camp was visited by the commander of the travailleur camps, some French major, and at roll call we were told he was very satisfied with the visit, and that as a result we are to receive 2 mess-tins of wine per week as a supplement.

November 27, 1941, Thursday
The French authorities have not allowed any newspaper into the camp since the start of the English offensive in Libya, whereas before that the newspaper "Echo d'Oran" was posted in the tent
where the canteen is located. Nevertheless, news made it into the camp that the English had taken Derna in Cyrenaica.

November 28, 1941, Friday
A small incident occurred with the Russian sergeant. It happened like this. One of our comrades received very torn trousers from the stores, so he went to the Russian sergeant, since he's the quartermaster, for several days asking for an exchange, but without success. Today this comrade (Ludwik) went again with his request, arguing that yesterday Senior Sgt. Benal received new trousers even though his old ones were whole, and he [Ludwik] was asking for those old ones — but again he was refused. A quarrel broke out between them in which Ludwik apparently said that it wouldn't be long before he'd stop tormenting us, as a result of which he was written up for a disciplinary report and punished with 4 days' arrest. When the lieutenant told him this, he replied, "Merci, mon lieutenant, tout pour la patrie," and left. Two hours later, when he ran into the Russian, the latter told him he didn't want to be the cause of his suffering, and tore up the report. As for another sergeant, whom they call "the chameleon," they mock him this way: the sergeant ordered them to make 400 bricks a day, and they threaten him that when the English come, he'll have to make 1,400 a day.

November 29, 1941, Saturday
The camp commander left in the morning for Colomb-Béchar, and he was replaced by a senior sergeant of Austrian origin, and from morning a fly must have landed on his nose, because everything displeased him. But it was only after lunch that things turned bad, because the supply platoon, which never stands for the afternoon roll call, was a little slow about it this time, and the Austrian flew into a rage over this and began cursing them as "cochons polonais" (Polish pigs), and for some unknown reason threw one man into the arrest area, which is under the open sky in a strictly designated spot, and for the night he was given only one blanket. It must be devilishly cold there, because we freeze even in our tents under 3 blankets. The company was out at work when this happened and only learned about it all after returning. Right away the chief, Benal, went with the interpreter to the camp commander, who had already returned, and laid out the matter for him; the commander for his part asked that it be let go, since the Austrian is sick and prone to such outbursts, and that it wouldn't happen again.

November 30, 1941, Sunday
Somehow the day passed quietly, although the food is much worse since we don't work — until, unexpectedly, right before supper, a roll call was ordered. The Austrian arrives at the roll call furious, shouting something in French, and goes straight to Budzyński, who was a little drunk and swaying in the ranks but not causing any trouble, and ordered him locked up in the arrest area.
The commander said that some commission had found large, unbroken stones in the piles that we build, and that tomorrow one platoon would go and break them up for free, at which the Austrian began shouting that this was sabotage and more besides, which caused the company to start howling. The commander immediately responded to this by saying that for two days we would get no wine. At that point Sgt.
