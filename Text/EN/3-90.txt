The men dispersed, but no one went to supper, as a sign of protest, because he did not want to speak with us, and because the lieutenant had said at roll call that we, the workers, have no rights here — so nothing to say — only obligations. By a lucky chance, an officer from French intelligence happened to be at the camp conducting interrogations, and Hankiewicz went to him. He assured us that we are still considered combatants and asked us not to cause trouble and to work, and that everything would change, and that the Austrian's harassment would not happen again. As for those who were arrested, Budzyński is to be released in the morning once he sobers up, but Durkowski is held for a different matter — intelligence ordered his arrest, so he won't be released. Among other things, this officer reportedly said that they had given us 2 days to leave, but we hadn't taken advantage of it. I don't know now what that's supposed to mean. Most of the men went to supper after Hankiewicz's conversation with the intelligence officer, but a few were found who still reproached Hankiewicz for having handled the matter badly.

December 1, 1941
It seems that with the last day of November the evil fate left us, because the Austrian apologized to us, and because he asked the camp commander to lift the ban on issuing wine, which he had caused.

December 2, Tuesday - December 5, 1941, Friday
The Austrian must have heard a proper lecture from the French intelligence lieutenant, because he changed completely and sits as if he weren't there, no longer driving anyone
to work. Time passes in daily monotonous labor, and in the evenings there's nothing to do, because the lighting in the barracks (tent) — carbide lamps — is very poor, and it's cold. The mornings, too, are very cold, and there's frost on the tents and the railway ties. On Friday evening Dzida and Czarnecki arrived at the camp. The former had been held under arrest in Tlemcen for trying to escape to England, and the latter had been a cook in Mascara for our officers. The officers are held under arrest and the cadets under guard in a hotel, so Czarnecki claims.

December 6, 1941, Saturday
Today is the opening of the first section of the Mediterranean-Niger railway line. As a mark of this, the "Lux Torpédo" express passed over the track we're working on. We're supposed to earn 4 francs 50 centimes a day, but as for wages — nothing has come, and nothing keeps coming. Through the efforts of our chief, Benal, the French lieutenant agreed to grant credit at the canteen up to 4 francs a day, for which one can get 2 sandwiches — which one absolutely has to buy, or else go hungry. We asked that they sell plain bread instead of sandwiches, since it would be cheaper, but the Russian keeps making sandwiches because he earns more on them, despite the camp commander's orders. We complained about this to the camp commander, and he gave the "Russian fellow" a proper scolding.
