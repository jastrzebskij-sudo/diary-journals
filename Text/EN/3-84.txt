According to rumor, he is a lieutenant of the French air force who tried to flee to General de Gaulle's army and was caught. As punishment he was sent out here for 5 months — and it seems we are to be here that long too.

November 16, 1941, Sunday
Until noon we finish building the camp, and in the afternoon some of the men are issued boots, since there aren't yet enough for everyone. A canteen has also been set up for us, though there is still little in it. From somewhere a French newspaper made its way into the camp, informing us that General Huntziger, the Vichy government's Minister of War, died in an airplane accident. And now we guess that the flags at half-mast that we saw during our journey were marking his death.

November 23, 1941, Sunday
For a whole week I didn't have a free moment to note anything down for memory. Only today, Sunday, do we have a day off from work, though everyone is still busy working for themselves — doing laundry, bathing, and fixing up the tents. On Monday we were taken 2 km from the camp to gather stones and pile them up beside the railway track. We're supposed to collect 1 cubic meter of stones per person, but no one managed it, and on average it came out to about ½ cubic meter per person — and yet they're satisfied with us. We work from 7 to 12 and from 1 to 5. The food is very bad, as it's constantly peas and groats. We got potatoes once, one tablespoon per person, as a dessert. After eating these peas we get terrible stomach bloating, and the tents are full of flatulence all night long. The stench is
something awful. All week we work just as we did on Monday, with nothing special happening, until Saturday, November 22, when during work Lt. Janicki, who commands the Poles working in Colomb-Béchar, came to see us. He spoke only with the chief for about 5 minutes, and afterward the chief told us that the lieutenant is trying to get the drivers pulled out of the camp and that the matter is progressing well.
After returning from work a surprise awaited us — namely, the lieutenant, the camp commander, arranged a bathhouse for us, and we now have a small shower for 4 people. French newspapers report on the English offensive in Cyrenaica and that on the first day they advanced 75 km. This afternoon Soja and one of his comrades from Colomb-Béchar sneaked into the camp, and from them we learned what Lt. Janicki had told us yesterday, as well as about the life of our comrades in Colomb-Béchar. Soja was afraid to leave the camp in case the Arabs guarding us shot him, so they went to the lieutenant, the camp commander, to ask permission to speak with us, as if they had just arrived, but the lieutenant refused, since he has such orders, though he gave them the address of his commander in Colomb-Béchar so he could grant them permission. So Soja went off as if nothing had happened. The camp commander is a very good man; only the sergeants oppress us at every turn — for example, today the bread ration sold in the canteen, run by one of the sergeants, a Russian, is given to us as one loaf for 8 men instead of for 6, and only today did this come to light, and the lieutenant gave the guilty parties a proper dressing-down. Our belongings, which had been taken from us during the arrest in Saïda, were handed back to us this week, a few each day, but without the documents, which the camp commander kept.
